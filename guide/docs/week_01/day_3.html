DAY 3 · THE DISTRIBUTED GRAPHMLDSASystem Design

Split the graph across machines, then keep it fresh

Where we are in the design. Days 1 and 2 ran People You May Know on one machine: read your friends' adjacency lists, count mutual friends, keep the top 10. At a billion users the adjacency lists alone are about 1.6 TB (the back-of-envelope estimate in R.6), so they cannot live on one machine. Today builds the box under candidate generation in the architecture of R.7: the graph store that holds the friend lists across many machines, and the candidate store that holds precomputed suggestions and is updated as friendships form.

Three blocks. The ML block teaches how to measure latency honestly: percentiles from samples, and a confidence interval for p99. The Algorithms block teaches how to split keys across machines (hashing, consistent hashing, rendezvous hashing), what one request then costs in shards touched, how to place friends near each other, and how to cache hot lists. The Design block puts the store together: precompute versus compute on request, exactly which suggestions change when two people connect, and why every response is checked again before it is shown.

ML · measuring latency: percentiles and a confidence interval for p99

The question

The service promises: "99% of PYMK requests finish within budget." You log the cost of 1,000 requests. Is the promise kept? Two things are needed: an estimate of the 99th percentile from a sample, and a statement of how far off that estimate might be. As the cost of a request we use W(u) = Σf∈N(u) deg(f), the number of adjacency entries the exact algorithm reads (R.4). On a real server time grows with it, and here we can compute it exactly for every one of the 10,000 users, so we know the true answer and can check our estimators against it.

First attempt: report the mean

Ten logged request costs: 310, 120, 950, 480, 205, 3100, 640, 150, 820, 270. Their mean is 7,045 / 10 = 704.5. But 7 of the 10 requests cost less than the mean, and one cost 3,100, more than four times the mean. The mean answers "how much work in total", not "how slow is a slow request". On the week's graph the mean W is 1,477, only 38.7% of users are above it, and the 99th percentile is 7,284: 4.9 times the mean. A budget set from the mean would be broken by a large share of requests, and the users who break it are the same users every time (those with popular friends, Day 2).

Definitions: quantiles and order statistics

Let X be the cost of a random request, with cumulative distribution function (CDF) \( F(x) = P(X \le x) \). The p-quantile is the smallest value that at least a fraction p of requests do not exceed:

\[ q_p = \inf\{\,x : F(x) \ge p\,\}. \]

The p50 (median), p95 and p99 are \( q_{0.5}, q_{0.95}, q_{0.99} \). From a sample \( X_1,\dots,X_n \) we only have the empirical CDF \( F_n(x) = \frac1n \#\{i : X_i \le x\} \), the fraction of the sample at or below x. Sort the sample: the k-th smallest value \( X_{(k)} \) is the k-th order statistic. Applying the definition of \( q_p \) to \( F_n \) gives the nearest-rank estimator:

\[ \hat q_p = X_{(\lceil p n\rceil)} , \]

because \( F_n(X_{(k)}) \ge k/n \), and k = ⌈pn⌉ is the smallest k with k/n ≥ p.

The nearest-rank percentile picks one sorted value. With n = 10 the p99 is the largest value, so it says nothing about the 1% tail. How large must n be? That is the next question.

Why the estimator works, and how far off it can be

The key identity. Fix a value x. Each sample is ≤ x with probability F(x), independently, so the count \( B_x = \#\{i : X_i \le x\} \) is Binomial(n, F(x)). The k-th smallest value is ≤ x exactly when at least k samples are ≤ x:

\[ X_{(k)} \le x \iff B_x \ge k, \qquad\text{so}\qquad P\big(X_{(k)} \le x\big) = P\big(\mathrm{Bin}(n, F(x)) \ge k\big). \]

Consistency. Take F continuous and strictly increasing near \( q_p \), and any ε > 0. Then \( F(q_p - \varepsilon) = p - \delta_1 \) and \( F(q_p + \varepsilon) = p + \delta_2 \) with δ1, δ2 > 0. With k = ⌈pn⌉, the estimate is too low only if \( B_{q_p-\varepsilon} \ge k \ge pn \), that is, if a Binomial with mean \( (p-\delta_1)n \) exceeds its mean by \( \delta_1 n \). Chebyshev bounds that by \( \frac{\mathrm{Var}}{(\delta_1 n)^2} \le \frac{n/4}{\delta_1^2 n^2} = \frac{1}{4\delta_1^2 n} \to 0 \). The "too high" side is the same argument. So \( \hat q_p \to q_p \) in probability.

A confidence interval with no assumptions about F. Put x = \( q_p \) in the identity. For continuous F, \( F(q_p) = p \), so \( B = \#\{X_i \le q_p\} \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n, p) \), and

\[ P\big(X_{(j)} \le q_p < X_{(k)}\big) = P(B \ge j) - P(B \ge k) = P(j \le B < k) = \sum_{i=j}^{k-1} \binom{n}{i} p^i (1-p)^{n-i}. \]

This probability does not depend on F at all. Choose the narrowest ranks j < k whose binomial mass is at least 0.95, and \( [X_{(j)}, X_{(k)}] \) is a 95% confidence interval for the true p99, whatever the distribution. This is the order-statistic interval.

How many samples p99 needs. The highest rank is n, so the best possible upper end is \( X_{(n)} \), the sample maximum. It fails when every sample is below \( q_{0.99} \), which happens with probability \( 0.99^n \). For a 95% upper bound we need \( 0.99^n \le 0.05 \), so

\[ n \ \ge\ \frac{\ln 0.05}{\ln 0.99} = 298.07 . \]

With fewer than 299 samples no method can bound p99 from above at 95% using sample values. At n = 100 the chance that all samples miss the 1% tail is \( 0.99^{100} = 0.366 \).

How wide. A first-order (delta method) approximation shows why tail percentiles are noisy. Near \( q_p \), \( F_n \) rises with slope about f(\( q_p \)), the density there, and \( F_n(\hat q_p) \approx p \). Linearize: \( F_n(\hat q_p) \approx F_n(q_p) + f(q_p)(\hat q_p - q_p) \), so \( \hat q_p - q_p \approx \frac{p - F_n(q_p)}{f(q_p)} \). Since \( n F_n(q_p) \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n,p) \), \( \mathrm{Var}(F_n(q_p)) = p(1-p)/n \), and

\[ \mathrm{Var}(\hat q_p) \approx \frac{p(1-p)}{n\, f(q_p)^2}. \]

In a heavy tail the density at \( q_{0.99} \) is tiny (few requests have costs near it), and dividing by its square makes the variance large. Compare the mean, whose variance is \( \sigma^2/n \): that does not involve a density at all.

The bootstrap

The order-statistic interval works only for quantiles. For any other statistic (a ratio, a difference of two p99s between versions, an AUC on Day 5) we need a general tool. The sampling distribution of an estimator \( \hat\theta = t(X_1,\dots,X_n) \) depends on the unknown F. The plug-in principle: replace F by \( F_n \), the distribution that puts mass 1/n on each observed value, and compute the sampling distribution under \( F_n \) by simulation. Drawing n values from \( F_n \) is drawing n values from the sample with replacement. That is the bootstrap (Efron, 1979):

  1. Repeat B times: draw a resample \( X^*_1,\dots,X^*_n \) with replacement from the data, and compute \( \hat\theta^*_b = t(X^*) \).
  2. The percentile interval is the 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles of \( \hat\theta^*_1,\dots,\hat\theta^*_B \).

Why it can fail for p99. A resample contains only values from the sample, so every bootstrap p99 is at most the sample maximum \( X_{(n)} \). Whenever the whole sample lies below the true \( q_{0.99} \), the bootstrap interval lies below it too and misses. That has probability \( 0.99^{100} = 0.366 \) at n = 100, so the coverage of the "95%" bootstrap interval is at most 0.634 there. The bootstrap is justified when \( \hat\theta \) is a smooth function of \( F_n \) and n is large; an extreme order statistic from a small sample is neither.

Cost. The nearest-rank percentile: sort, O(n log n), or O(n) expected with quickselect (Day 1). The bootstrap: B resamples of n draws plus a percentile each, O(B·n log n) as written below (O(Bn) with selection), and O(Bn) memory when vectorized. The order-statistic interval: O(n log n) for the sort plus O(n log n) to scan ranks with a binary search over the binomial CDF.

percentiles.py · nearest rank, bootstrap CI, order-statistic CI
import math
import numpy as np

def percentile(xs, p):
    """Nearest-rank p-th percentile: the ceil(p·n)-th smallest value (1-based)."""
    s = sorted(xs)
    return s[max(1, math.ceil(p * len(s))) - 1]

def bootstrap_ci(xs, p=0.99, b=2000, level=0.95, seed=0):
    """Percentile bootstrap: resample with replacement, recompute, take the middle 95%."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    x = np.sort(np.asarray(xs))
    n = len(x)
    k = max(1, math.ceil(p * n)) - 1
    reps = np.sort(x[rng.integers(0, n, size=(b, n))], axis=1)[:, k]
    a = (1 - level) / 2
    return np.quantile(reps, a), np.quantile(reps, 1 - a)

def order_stat_ci(xs, p=0.99, level=0.95):
    """Distribution-free CI [x_(j), x_(k)): P(j <= B < k) >= level, where B ~ Binomial(n, p)."""
    s = sorted(xs)
    n = len(s)
    logpmf = [math.lgamma(n + 1) - math.lgamma(i + 1) - math.lgamma(n - i + 1)
              + i * math.log(p) + (n - i) * math.log1p(-p) for i in range(n + 1)]
    cdf = np.cumsum(np.exp(logpmf))            # cdf[i] = P(B <= i)
    best = None
    for j in range(1, n + 1):                  # P(j <= B < k) = cdf[k-1] - cdf[j-1]
        k = int(np.searchsorted(cdf, cdf[j - 1] + level - 1e-12)) + 1
        k = min(k, n + 1)                      # k = n + 1: no sample value is high enough
        if cdf[k - 1] - cdf[j - 1] >= level - 1e-12 and (best is None or k - j < best[1] - best[0]):
            best = (j, k)
    j, k = best
    return s[j - 1], (s[k - 1] if k <= n else math.inf)

Where PYMK uses it

Every latency promise in the design is a percentile: "p99 of the candidate read under budget", "p95 of the whole request". Dashboards compute them from samples of requests, and two versions of the service are compared by their p99s, so the comparison needs an interval, not two point estimates. Day 2 showed why the tail matters more under fan-out: a request that waits on many shards is as slow as the slowest one. This block says how much data that tail needs before you can trust a number about it. The rule of thumb that follows from the derivation: to talk about p99 you need many hundreds of requests per group; for p99.9, many thousands.

Solution

Smallest n. The upper end can be finite only if \( P(B \le n-1) = 1 - p^n \ge 0.95 \), so \( n \ge \ln 0.05 / \ln p \). For p99.9 that is \( \ln 0.05 / \ln 0.999 \approx 2{,}994.2 \), so n ≥ 2,995. For p50, \( \ln 0.05/\ln 0.5 \approx 4.32 \), so n ≥ 5 for the upper end alone (a two-sided interval needs both tails, \( 2\cdot 0.5^n \le 0.05 \), so n ≥ 6).

n = 300. \( 0.99^{300} \approx 0.049 \), so the bootstrap's ceiling on coverage is about 0.95: it can just reach nominal coverage in the best case, and in practice falls short, because resamples rarely contain the few largest values in the right proportion.

Paired difference. Resample users and compute both arms on the same resampled users: the two p99s are positively correlated (the same heavy users are heavy in both), and pairing keeps that correlation, which makes the interval for the difference narrower and correct.

Combining p99s. Two pairs of servers with the same per-server p99s but different union p99s prove that no formula in the two p99s can work. Pair A: server 1 has 100 values of 1 (p99 = 1); server 2 has 100 values of 2 (p99 = 2); the union's p99 (rank 198 of 200) is 2. Pair B: server 1 as before; server 2 has 98 values of 0 and 2 values of 2 (its 99th value is 2, so p99 = 2); the union is 98 zeros, 100 ones and 2 twos, and its 198th value is 1. Each server must send a mergeable summary instead: a histogram on shared bucket boundaries, which adds bucket by bucket.

Dependence. \( B_x \) is binomial only if the samples are independent. Bursts of correlated requests make B overdispersed, so the true interval is wider than the formula says.

Algorithms · sharding, consistent hashing, fan-out, caching

This block treats the placement of keys on machines as a subject in its own right. It starts with the simplest rule, proves exactly how badly it behaves when the cluster grows, derives consistent hashing and its load bounds, then measures what a PYMK request costs once its friends' lists are spread across shards.

1 · The question

Store each user's adjacency list on one of S machines (shards). Every server that handles a request must be able to compute, from a user id alone, which shard holds that user's list, without asking anyone. When we add machines, as few lists as possible should move, because every moved list is data copied over the network while the service runs. And each shard should hold about the same number of lists.

2 · First attempt: hash mod N

A hash function h maps a key to a 64-bit integer that looks uniformly random: changing the key slightly changes the output completely. The code below uses splitmix64, a few multiplications and shifts. Mod-N sharding puts key k on shard \( h(k) \bmod N \). It is fast, needs no state, and balances well: each key lands on each shard with probability 1/N, so on the week's graph with N = 16 the fullest shard holds 1.08 times the average.

Now add one machine. Keys 0 to 11, with \( h(k) = k \) for the example:

mod 3012012012012shard of keys 0…11 with N = 3
mod 4012301230123Only keys 0, 1, 2 stay: 3 of 12. 9 of 12 move.

Claim. If \( h(k) \) is uniform, going from N to N+1 shards moves a fraction N/(N+1) of all keys.

Proof. N and N+1 share no factor, so by the Chinese remainder theorem the map \( x \mapsto (x \bmod N,\ x \bmod (N{+}1)) \) is a bijection from \( \{0,\dots,N(N{+}1)-1\} \) onto all pairs (a, b) with a < N, b < N+1. A key stays exactly when a = b, and there are N such pairs (b = N cannot equal any a). So within every block of N(N+1) consecutive hash values, exactly N stay: a fraction 1/(N+1). A 64-bit hash spans so many blocks that the partial last block does not matter. ∎ Doubling is better but still bad: \( x \bmod 2N \) equals \( x \bmod N \) exactly when \( x \bmod 2N < N \), half the time, so half the keys move.

3 · Consistent hashing: the key idea, derived

Definitions. Treat the hash range \( [0, 2^{64}) \) as a circle, the ring. Hash each server to a point on the ring as well. A key belongs to the first server point at or after its own hash, going clockwise and wrapping past the top (its successor). So each server owns the arc between the previous server point and its own.

Servers A, B, C at positions 10, 45, 75 (filled). Keys (squares) go clockwise to the next server: k1 (5) → A, k2 (20) → B, k3 (50) and k4 (60) → C, k5 (90) wraps to A. Add server D at 30 (hollow). Only the shaded arc (10, 30] changes owner: k2 moves from B to D. Nothing else moves.

Claim 1 (only the new server gains keys). When a server is added, every key that moves, moves to the new server. Proof. The old server points are unchanged. A key's successor in the new set of points is the first point clockwise among old ∪ new. If that point is old, it is the key's old successor, since no old point lies between them that did not before. Otherwise it is a new point. ∎ Removing a server is the same argument in reverse: only its own keys move, each to its successor. On the week's graph, adding server 17 to 16 moved keys only to server 17, and removing server 3 moved exactly the 6.1% of users it held (with 100 virtual nodes, below).

Claim 2 (the expected fraction moved is 1/(N+1)). Model the N+1 server points as independent uniform points on the ring. Proof. Fix a key at position x. It moves iff its successor among all N+1 points is the new server's point. The points are independent and identically distributed, so the labelling is symmetric: each of the N+1 servers is equally likely to own the first point after x. The probability is 1/(N+1) for every key, and by linearity of expectation the expected number of moved keys among K is K/(N+1). ∎

The catch: one point per server balances badly. A server's share is the length of the arc before its point. For M independent uniform points on a ring of length 1, a given arc is longer than x exactly when the other M−1 points all avoid the length-x stretch before that point:

\[ P(\text{arc} > x) = (1-x)^{M-1}, \qquad E[\text{arc}] = \int_0^1 (1-x)^{M-1}\,dx = \frac1M . \]

The mean is fair, but the spread is as large as the mean. Square: \( E[\text{arc}^2] = \int_0^1 2x(1-x)^{M-1}dx = \frac{2}{M(M+1)} \), so

\[ \mathrm{Var}(\text{arc}) = \frac{2}{M(M+1)} - \frac{1}{M^2} = \frac{M-1}{M^2(M+1)}, \qquad \frac{\text{std}}{\text{mean}} = \sqrt{\frac{M-1}{M+1}} \approx 1 . \]

And the largest arc is much larger than average. By the union bound over the M arcs, \( P(\text{some arc} > \tfrac{c\ln M}{M}) \le M(1-\tfrac{c \ln M}{M})^{M-1} \approx M\cdot M^{-c} \), so with c = 2 the largest arc is below \( 2\ln M / M \) with probability about 1 − 1/M: the fullest server can carry about ln M times its fair share. (The exact expected largest arc is \( H_M / M \), with \( H_M = 1 + \frac12 + \dots + \frac1M \): 3.38 times the mean for M = 16.)

Virtual nodes. Give each server V points instead of one (its virtual nodes), so there are M = NV points. A server's load is now the sum of V arcs. The arcs sum to 1, so \( \mathrm{Var}(\sum \text{arcs}) = 0 \), which fixes their covariance: \( M\,\mathrm{Var} + M(M-1)\,\mathrm{Cov} = 0 \), so \( \mathrm{Cov} = -\mathrm{Var}/(M-1) \). For a server's V arcs:

\[ \mathrm{Var}(L) = V\,\mathrm{Var} + V(V-1)\,\mathrm{Cov} = V\,\frac{M-1}{M^2(M+1)}\cdot\frac{M-V}{M-1} = \frac{V(M-V)}{M^2(M+1)} \;\overset{M=NV}{=}\; \frac{N-1}{N^2(NV+1)} . \]

Dividing the standard deviation by the mean load 1/N:

\[ \frac{\text{std}(L)}{E[L]} = \sqrt{\frac{N-1}{NV+1}} \;\approx\; \frac{1}{\sqrt V}. \]

A hundred virtual nodes cut the spread by ten. A second benefit: when a server leaves, its V arcs go to V different successors, so its load spreads over many servers instead of doubling one neighbour's.

Cost. Keep the NV points in a sorted array. A lookup is one binary search, O(log NV) time. Memory is O(NV). Adding a server inserts V points: O(V log NV) in a balanced tree, O(NV) in the sorted Python list below (each insert shifts the array).

consistent_hash.py · splitmix64, mod-N, a ring with virtual nodes, rendezvous
import bisect

MASK = (1 << 64) - 1

def h64(x):
    """splitmix64: a fast, well-mixed 64-bit hash of a non-negative integer."""
    z = (x + 0x9E3779B97F4A7C15) & MASK
    z = ((z ^ (z >> 30)) * 0xBF58476D1CE4E5B9) & MASK
    z = ((z ^ (z >> 27)) * 0x94D049BB133111EB) & MASK
    return z ^ (z >> 31)

def mod_shard(key, n):
    return h64(key) % n                       # the shard of a key among n shards

class Ring:
    """Consistent hashing with v virtual nodes per server."""
    def __init__(self, servers, v=100, salt=0):
        self.v, self.salt = v, salt
        self.points = []                      # sorted list of (position, server)
        for s in servers:
            self.add(s)

    def _positions(self, s):
        # salt the server id: hashing raw (s, i) like a key would put key i exactly on a point
        base = h64(s ^ (self.salt << 32) ^ 0x632BE59BD9B4E019)
        return [h64((base + i) & MASK) for i in range(self.v)]

    def add(self, s):
        for p in self._positions(s):
            bisect.insort(self.points, (p, s))

    def remove(self, s):
        self.points = [(p, t) for p, t in self.points if t != s]

    def lookup(self, key):
        i = bisect.bisect_left(self.points, (h64(key), -1))
        return self.points[i % len(self.points)][1]   # first point clockwise; wrap at the end

def rendezvous(key, servers):
    """Highest-random-weight hashing: the server with the largest h(key, server) wins."""
    return max(servers, key=lambda s: h64(h64(key) ^ (s * 0xD6E8FEB86659FD93 & MASK)))

4 · Rendezvous hashing

Definition. Rendezvous (highest random weight) hashing gives key k to the server s with the largest \( h(k, s) \). No ring, no stored points: just the list of servers.

Why it has both properties. For a fixed key, the N+1 weights \( h(k,s) \) behave like independent uniform values, so each server is equally likely to hold the maximum: the load is balanced (each key independently uniform), and when a server is added the key moves only if the new server's weight is the new maximum, probability 1/(N+1), and then it moves to the new server. Removing a server moves only the keys for which it was the maximum, each to its runner-up. Measured: adding server 17 moved 0.0577 of users; the fullest of 16 servers held 1.05 times the average.

Cost. A lookup computes N hashes: O(N) time, O(N) memory for the server list. That is fine for tens of servers and slow for thousands, where the ring's O(log NV) wins. Weighted servers are easy (scale each weight), which is one reason rendezvous hashing is common for choosing among replicas or caches.

5 · Scatter-gather: shards touched per request

The question. The PYMK request for u reads N(u) from u's shard, then the adjacency list of every friend (R.3). With lists hashed to shards, the friends' lists are scattered. Sending one RPC (remote procedure call) per friend costs du round trips. Batching by shard groups the friend ids by shard and sends one RPC per shard holding at least one of them, in parallel: the scatter. The answers are merged into one mutual-friend count: the gather. Example: friends {3, 8, 11, 14, 21} on 4 shards (shard = id mod 4) → shards {3, 0, 3, 2, 1}: 4 RPCs instead of 5.

Derivation. With d friends hashed uniformly onto S shards, let \( I_k = 1 \) when shard k holds at least one friend. A given shard holds none with probability \( (1-1/S)^d \), so by linearity of expectation

\[ E[\#\text{shards touched}] = \sum_{k=1}^{S} P(I_k = 1) = S\Big(1 - \big(1 - \tfrac1S\big)^d\Big) . \]

For small d this is close to d (\( (1-1/S)^d \approx 1 - d/S \)): almost every friend is on a different shard, and batching saves little. For d ≫ S it approaches S: every request touches every shard. A user with exactly 20 friends touches 11.6 of 16 shards, 17.29 of 64, 19.27 of 256 on average. The function is concave in d, so by Jensen's inequality the average over the heavy-tailed degrees is lower than the value at the mean degree.

fanout.py · shards touched, batching, cross-shard edges
from collections import defaultdict

def shards_touched(adj, u, shard_of):
    """The distinct shards a two-hop request must read: one per shard holding a friend's list."""
    return {shard_of(f) for f in adj[u]}

def batch_by_shard(friends, shard_of):
    """Group the keys to read by shard: one RPC per shard instead of one per friend."""
    batches = defaultdict(list)
    for f in friends:
        batches[shard_of(f)].append(f)
    return batches

def expected_shards(d, s):
    """E[distinct shards] when d keys land uniformly on s shards."""
    return s * (1 - (1 - 1 / s) ** d)

def cross_shard_fraction(edges, part):
    return sum(part[u] != part[v] for u, v, *_ in edges) / len(edges)

6 · Placing friends together: hash versus locality

The question. An edge (u, v) is cross-shard when u and v are on different shards. Fewer cross-shard edges means fewer shards per request. Under hashing the two endpoints are placed independently, so \( P(\text{same}) = \sum_{k} P(u \in k)P(v \in k) = S \cdot \tfrac{1}{S^2} = \tfrac1S \), and the cross-shard fraction is \( 1 - 1/S \): 93.8% at 16 shards. Hashing ignores the graph completely.

Locality partitioning. Choosing S equal-size parts with the fewest cut edges is balanced graph partitioning, which is NP-hard (it contains minimum bisection), so practical systems use heuristics. A simple one for this graph, where 35% of friendships come from shared groups:

  1. Give each user a primary group: the one of their groups that contains most of their friends.
  2. Pack groups onto shards, largest first, filling the least-loaded shard and spilling to the next least-loaded when a shard reaches its capacity, 1.05 times the average shard size.
  3. Label propagation under the capacity: up to 5 passes over the users; move a user to the shard holding most of their friends if that is strictly more than on their current shard and the target has room. Each move strictly increases the number of that user's friends on its shard, so it never increases the cut; each pass costs O(n + m).
partition.py · group packing plus capacity-limited label propagation
def group_partition(adj, groups, s, slack=1.05, rounds=5):
    """Place users by group, then move users toward their friends while shards have room."""
    n = len(adj)
    cap = int(slack * n / s) + 1
    members = defaultdict(list)
    for u in range(n):
        # primary group: the user's group holding most of their friends
        g = max(groups[u], key=lambda g: (sum(g in groups[f] for f in adj[u]), -g))
        members[g].append(u)
    part, load = [0] * n, [0] * s
    for g in sorted(members, key=lambda g: (-len(members[g]), g)):   # biggest groups first
        k = min(range(s), key=lambda i: load[i])
        for u in members[g]:                   # keep a group together until its shard is full
            if load[k] >= cap:
                k = min(range(s), key=lambda i: load[i])
            part[u] = k; load[k] += 1
    for _ in range(rounds):                    # label propagation under a capacity cap
        moved = 0
        for u in range(n):
            votes = Counter(part[f] for f in adj[u])
            best, c = max(votes.items(), key=lambda kv: (kv[1], -kv[0]), default=(part[u], 0))
            if best != part[u] and c > votes[part[u]] and load[best] < cap:
                load[part[u]] -= 1; load[best] += 1; part[u] = best; moved += 1
        if moved == 0:
            break
    return part

7 · Caching hot adjacency lists

The question. Some lists are read far more than others. Which ones, and how much does a small cache save? A request by u reads the list of f exactly when u ∈ N(f). If requesters are uniform, list f is read by a fraction deg(f)/n of requests: read frequency is proportional to degree, the friendship paradox of Day 2 seen from the storage side.

LRU recap. A least-recently-used cache of capacity C keeps the C keys used most recently. It needs two structures: a hash map from key to node, for O(1) lookup, and a doubly linked list in order of use, for O(1) "move to front" and "remove the last". Both operations touch a constant number of pointers, so get and put are O(1); memory is O(C). Sentinel head and tail nodes remove the empty-list special cases.

lru.py · LeetCode 146 with the list written out
class Node:
    __slots__ = ("key", "val", "prev", "next")
    def __init__(self, key=0, val=0):
        self.key, self.val, self.prev, self.next = key, val, None, None

class LRUFromScratch:
    """The same cache with the doubly linked list written out. Sentinels avoid edge cases."""
    def __init__(self, capacity):
        self.cap, self.map = capacity, {}
        self.head, self.tail = Node(), Node()  # head.next = most recent, tail.prev = least recent
        self.head.next, self.tail.prev = self.tail, self.head

    def _unlink(self, x):
        x.prev.next, x.next.prev = x.next, x.prev

    def _push_front(self, x):
        x.prev, x.next = self.head, self.head.next
        self.head.next.prev = x
        self.head.next = x

    def get(self, key):
        x = self.map.get(key)
        if x is None:
            return -1
        self._unlink(x); self._push_front(x)
        return x.val

    def put(self, key, value):
        x = self.map.get(key)
        if x:
            x.val = value
            self._unlink(x); self._push_front(x)
            return
        if len(self.map) == self.cap:
            lru = self.tail.prev
            self._unlink(lru); del self.map[lru.key]
        x = Node(key, value)
        self.map[key] = x; self._push_front(x)

Python's OrderedDict is exactly this pair of structures, so the short version is:

lru_short.py · LeetCode 146 with OrderedDict
from collections import OrderedDict

class LRUCache:
    """LeetCode 146. get/put in O(1): a hash map plus a recency order."""
    def __init__(self, capacity):
        self.cap = capacity
        self.d = OrderedDict()                 # least recent first

    def get(self, key):
        if key not in self.d:
            return -1
        self.d.move_to_end(key)                # now the most recent
        return self.d[key]

    def put(self, key, value):
        if key in self.d:
            self.d.move_to_end(key)
        self.d[key] = value
        if len(self.d) > self.cap:
            self.d.popitem(last=False)         # evict the least recent

Which cache is best? If requests are independent draws with fixed probabilities \( p_f \) (the independent reference model), the hit rate of a fixed set A of cached keys is \( \sum_{f\in A} p_f \), which is largest when A is the C keys with the largest \( p_f \). Here \( p_f \propto \deg(f) \), so the optimal static cache holds the top-degree users' lists. LRU admits every miss and evicts useful lists to make room for one-off reads, so under this model it does worse; its strength is adapting when popularity changes over time.

8 · Where PYMK uses it

The graph store places adjacency lists with consistent hashing (virtual nodes, so machines can be added a few at a time without copying the graph), keeps a cache of hub lists in front of it, and coalesces concurrent reads. The fan-out measurement is the main argument of the Design block: computing two hops on request touches nearly as many shards as the user has friends, so the request path should read a precomputed answer from one shard.

hashmap.py · LeetCode 706
class MyHashMap:
    """LeetCode 706. Separate chaining; the table doubles when the load factor passes 0.75."""
    def __init__(self):
        self.size, self.buckets = 0, [[] for _ in range(8)]

    def _bucket(self, key):
        return self.buckets[h64(key) % len(self.buckets)]

    def put(self, key, value):
        b = self._bucket(key)
        for i, (k, _) in enumerate(b):
            if k == key:
                b[i] = (key, value); return
        b.append((key, value)); self.size += 1
        if self.size > 0.75 * len(self.buckets):
            old = [kv for b in self.buckets for kv in b]
            self.buckets = [[] for _ in range(2 * len(self.buckets))]
            for k, v in old:
                self._bucket(k).append((k, v))

    def get(self, key):
        for k, v in self._bucket(key):
            if k == key:
                return v
        return -1

    def remove(self, key):
        b = self._bucket(key)
        for i, (k, _) in enumerate(b):
            if k == key:
                b.pop(i); self.size -= 1; return

Amortized O(1) put. The table doubles when the size passes 0.75 × buckets. A resize to 2b buckets moves about 0.75b entries and happens after 0.375b new inserts since the last one, so each insert pays O(1) extra on average. With a good hash, each bucket holds 0.75 entries on average, so get is O(1) expected.

class TimeMap:
    """LeetCode 981. Timestamps arrive increasing per key, so each list stays sorted."""
    def __init__(self):
        self.times, self.vals = defaultdict(list), defaultdict(list)

    def set(self, key, value, timestamp):
        self.times[key].append(timestamp); self.vals[key].append(value)

    def get(self, key, timestamp):
        i = bisect.bisect_right(self.times[key], timestamp)   # first time > timestamp
        return self.vals[key][i - 1] if i else ""

Why the map stores the node in 146. Moving a key to the front needs its position in the list; storing the node gives it in O(1), while storing only the value would force an O(C) search of the list.

Weighted servers. Ring: give a server of weight w about wV virtual nodes; its expected share is its fraction of the points. Rendezvous: use the score \( -w_s / \ln(u_s) \), where \( u_s \in (0,1) \) is the hash scaled to a uniform; the maximum of these is server s with probability \( w_s / \sum w \), because \( -\ln(u_s)/w_s \) is exponential with rate \( w_s \) and the smallest of independent exponentials is index s with probability proportional to its rate.

Edge hashing. A request needs all edges of each friend, and those are now on up to deg(f) shards each, so it touches up to min(S, W(u)) shards: worse. Edge partitioning helps batch jobs that scan all edges evenly, not lookups by user.

System Design · the graph store, precompute, incremental updates

The question

Where does the PYMK answer come from at request time? Either compute two hops on request (the scatter-gather above), or compute every user's candidates ahead of time and store them. If we store them, they go stale the moment two people connect. Which stored suggestions must change, and how do we stay correct in between?

A graph store, as a published design describes it

The TAO paper (Bronson et al., USENIX ATC 2013) describes Facebook's store for the social graph. In its data model, objects are typed nodes (users, posts, places) and associations are typed directed edges between objects, each with a time; queries are mostly "the list of associations of a given type from this object, newest first" and "how many are there". The paper describes a read-dominated workload, persistent storage in sharded MySQL, and a two-level cache tier (followers that serve reads, and leaders that sit between followers and the database), with eventual consistency between regions. We use it here as a public example of the pattern, not as a description of any current system: an adjacency-list store, sharded by object id, read through caches.

First attempt: compute on request

Online two hops read W(u) entries (mean 1,477, p99 7,284) from about 15 of 64 shards per request, and the shard count approaches the degree as the cluster grows (Algorithms part 5). It is always fresh, but slow and fragile in the tail.

The key idea: precompute as a self-join

Write the friendships as a table of ordered pairs (x, v), both directions. Joining the table with itself on the middle user v produces one row (x, y) for every two-hop path x – v – y. Grouping the rows by (x, y) and counting gives \( m(x,y) = |N(x)\cap N(y)| = (A^2)_{xy} \). Why the count is right: each common friend v of x and y contributes exactly one row, the path through v, and nothing else produces a row for (x, y). Cost: user v produces deg(v)(deg(v) − 1) ordered rows, so the join emits \( \sum_v d_v(d_v - 1) \) rows (Day 2's \( \sum d^2 \), minus the x = y pairs). A distributed batch job partitions the join by v, so each machine expands its own users, then shuffles rows by x to group them.

precompute.py · the batch two-hop self-join
def precompute_all(adj):
    """Batch two-hop: the self-join of the edge list with itself on the middle user."""
    m = defaultdict(Counter)
    for v in range(len(adj)):                  # v is the mutual friend
        for x in adj[v]:
            for y in adj[v]:
                if x != y:
                    m[x][y] += 1               # deg(v)·(deg(v)-1) rows for this v
    return m                                   # m[x][y] = |N(x) ∩ N(y)|, friends included

Measured on the past graph (the first 80,000 friendships): the join emits 9,519,454 rows, which group into 8,214,888 ordered pairs with at least one mutual friend (1.16 rows per pair), of which 8,091,284 are not yet friends: 809.1 candidate entries per user on average. The candidate store keeps only each user's top few (10 for display, a few hundred for the ranker of Day 5), so the stored output is small even though the join is large.

Incremental updates: whose candidates change when u and v connect

The question. Re-running the 9.5-million-row join after each new friendship is absurd. Which counts does one new edge change?

Derivation. Write the mutual count as a sum over possible middle users:

\[ m(x,y) = \sum_{w} [\,w \in N(x)\,]\,[\,w \in N(y)\,] . \]

Adding the edge u–v changes only two memberships: v joins N(u), and u joins N(v). A term of the sum changes only if one of its two brackets changes, so only when (x, w) or (y, w) is (u, v) or (v, u). Take x = u, w = v: the term becomes \( [v \in N(y)] \), so \( m(u, y) \) rises by 1 for every y ∈ N(v), the new paths u – v – y. Symmetrically, \( m(v, x) \) rises by 1 for every x ∈ N(u), the paths v – u – x. The pair (u, v) itself would need v ∈ N(v), which never holds, so m(u, v) is unchanged. Nothing else changes. So:

  • Changed counts: exactly \( d_u + d_v \) pairs (degrees before the edge), each +1, stored in both directions.
  • Users whose candidate rows change: \( \{u, v\} \cup N(u) \cup N(v) \). In addition, u and v leave each other's candidate lists, because they are now friends.
  • Everyone else is untouched, so their stored suggestions stay exactly correct.
beforea–u v–b,v–cN(u) = {a}, N(v) = {b, c}.
add u–vm(u,b) +1m(u,c) +1m(v,a) +1u, v no longer candidates of each otherdu + dv = 3 changed counts; rows of u, v, a, b, c change. Pair (b, c) is untouched: its middle users did not change.

Expected cost. The two endpoints of a new edge are not average users. The 20,000 future friendships form mostly among sociable users, and an endpoint of a random edge is a user picked with probability proportional to degree, whose expected degree is \( E[d^2]/E[d] \): the friendship paradox again (Day 2). So the expected update is about \( 2\,E[d^2]/E[d] \) counts, not \( 2\,E[d] \).

incremental.py · update mutual counts for one new friendship
def add_friendship(adj, m, u, v):
    """Update mutual counts m in place when u and v become friends. Returns the users touched."""
    for x in adj[u]:                           # new two-hop paths x - u - v
        m[x][v] += 1; m[v][x] += 1
    for y in adj[v]:                           # new two-hop paths u - v - y
        m[u][y] += 1; m[y][u] += 1
    adj[u].add(v); adj[v].add(u)
    return {u, v} | adj[u] | adj[v]

A differential check confirms the derivation: on 50 sampled edges, the counts of 80 users each (40 touched, 40 random) match a full recomputation, and untouched users' counts did not change. A friendship with a hub is expensive: the largest update touches 1,747 users. That is write amplification, the mirror image of Day 2's read skew, and the same fix applies: cap or delay updates through hubs.

Consistency: why the final check runs on every response

The candidate store is updated asynchronously: an event "u and v are now friends" travels through a queue to the shards holding the touched rows, and the batch job rebuilds everything periodically. Between the event and the update, a reader can see a stale row. This is eventual consistency: if writes stop, all replicas converge, but a read can return an old value. How stale does a list get without updates? Take 2,000 users' top-10 lists at the end of the past graph and let the 20,000 future friendships happen: 17.3% of those users now have at least one suggestion who is already their friend (2.0% of all suggestions). Blocks and privacy settings change the same way, and showing a blocked person is a serious failure, not a ranking error. So the last step of every response re-checks the ten suggestions against the source of truth (the friend list, the block list, privacy settings): ten membership tests per request, cheap enough to run every time. Stale counts are tolerable (a slightly wrong rank); stale eligibility is not. One more promise is worth making: the user who just connected with someone should not see that person suggested on their next page load (read-your-writes), which the final check against the friend list also guarantees.

Where PYMK uses it

The design is now: a graph store sharded by user with consistent hashing and a hub cache; a candidate store filled by a batch self-join and kept fresh by the incremental rule; a request path that reads one key from the candidate store; and a final eligibility check against the graph store on every response. Day 4 shrinks the candidate generation work with sampling and sketches, and Day 5 ranks the stored candidates with a model.

Problem variations
  • The update queue for a hub's new friendship holds 1,747 row updates. Bound the delay before every touched user is fresh, given a per-shard write rate.
  • Replace the nightly batch with incremental updates only. What goes wrong over months (lost events, bugs), and how does a periodic batch fix it?
  • Candidates from shared groups (not two hops) change when someone joins a group. Derive whose candidates change.
Solution

Unfriend. The same derivation with −1: \( m(u,y) \) falls by 1 for y ∈ N(v) and \( m(v,x) \) for x ∈ N(u) (neighbourhoods after removing the edge), and u, v become candidates of each other again with count m(u, v). If a user stores only their top 10 and one of them drops below the 11th, the true 10th is a candidate whose count was never stored, so the store must keep the full count map (or more than 10) for decrements, or recompute that user.

SQL. SELECT a.x, b.x AS y, COUNT(*) AS m FROM friends a JOIN friends b ON a.v = b.v AND a.x <> b.x GROUP BY a.x, b.x. It emits \( \sum_v d_v(d_v-1) \) rows before grouping; filter out pairs that are already friends with an anti-join.

Top-10 under increments. Counts only grow. Let t be the 10th largest count. A candidate outside the top 10 has count ≤ t; if it was not incremented its count is unchanged and t did not fall, so it still cannot pass the 10th. So after each increment, compare only the incremented candidate with the current 10th (a size-10 heap per user, Day 1).

Group join. When u joins group g, the pairs (u, y) for every member y of g gain a shared group, so u and every member are touched: |g| users, up to 3,035 here. That is why large groups are usually capped or sampled as a candidate source.

The incremental rule is tested against a full recomputation on 20 random graphs in day_3_solutions.py, and on the week's graph in day_3_measure.py, section 5.
