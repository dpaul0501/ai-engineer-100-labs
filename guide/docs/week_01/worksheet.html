Ship pymk on the week's graph

Where we are. Over five days the question "which ten people should we suggest?" grew from intersecting two friend lists into a small system: an exact count you can trust, a top ten picked without sorting everything, a graph whose hubs make some requests expensive, a model that learns who actually connects, and a service that never shows someone it must not. Now you build all of it yourself, as one tested Python package.

The package lives in the lab folder week_01/: stubs in pymk/, a SPEC.md to fill in, and acceptance tests in tests/test_acceptance.py. The graph is the one every page measured: 10,000 users and 100,000 friendships, rebuilt by data/make_graph.py with seed 0.

WI-3Workload report System Design ML

What you build. A short script that measures, for every user, how many friend-list entries the exact algorithm reads (the work W, the sum of your friends' friend counts), plots its distribution in buckets, and times exact.suggest for every user, reporting p50, p95 and p99 with metrics.percentile. The math, step by step: why scaling by d/s is right on average, and how noisy it is

Step 1: the setting. You have d friends. You share m of them with candidate v, so the true count is m. You read s of your d friends, chosen uniformly without replacement, and count how many of the m shared ones you read.

Step 2: one shared friend. Each friend is in the sample with probability \(s/d\). So each of the m shared friends adds \(s/d\) to the expected count, and \(\mathbb{E}[\text{count}] = m\,s/d\).

Step 3: scale back. Multiply the count by \(d/s\): the expectation becomes \(m\,(s/d)(d/s) = m\). The estimate is unbiased: right on average.

Step 4: the spread. The count is hypergeometric (draws without replacement), with variance

\[ s\,\frac{m}{d}\Big(1-\frac{m}{d}\Big)\frac{d-s}{d-1}. \]

The scaled estimate has \((d/s)^2\) times that. For d = 10, s = 3, m = 4: the count's variance is \(3 \times 0.4 \times 0.6 \times 7/9 = 0.560\), and the estimate's is \((10/3)^2 \times 0.560 = 6.22\), as the acceptance test checks.

Step 5: read it. The factor \((d-s)/(d-1)\) is zero when you read every friend: no sampling, no noise. And reading 3 of 10 friends gives a standard deviation of \(\sqrt{6.22} \approx 2.5\) on a true count of 4: unbiased is not the same as precise.

  • Fix the reading order with random.Random(f"{seed}:{u}"), so each user gets its own order and every run repeats exactly.
  • A friend whose list does not fit in the remaining budget is skipped, not the end of the loop: a shorter list later may still fit.
  • Bootstrap over users, not over candidate pairs: the user is what was sampled (Day 4, data science §1).

Two ways to compute AUC

AUC is the chance that the model scores a real future friendship above a pair that did not form. What changes the number is which pairs get compared. build_dataset pairs each future friend of a user with one other candidate of the same user: a friend of a friend who did not connect. Every user therefore brings as many negatives as positives, and the question is "among this person's own candidates, who will they connect with?". Day 4 §9 pools all candidate pairs of all users into one set, so a future friend of one user is often compared with a non-friend of another user.

The within-user comparison is harder. Two candidates of the same person share that person's friends, friend count and city, so the score can only win on what differs between the candidates. In a pool, the score can also win on differences between the users themselves. That is why the same features score lower here: common neighbours 0.590 against 0.620 pooled on Day 4, Adamic–Adar 0.721 against 0.805, logistic regression 0.742 against 0.832. The within-user number is the closer match to the app's job, which is ordering one person's list. Whichever you compute, say which pairs it compares.

The eligibility filter (not yourself, not current friends, nobody blocked in either direction, nobody who has hidden themselves) runs on every response, cached or not. add_friendship clears the cache for exactly the users whose counts can change.   • Cache the ranking, never the filtered answer: a filtered answer goes stale the moment someone blocks or hides. Day 5 §2 measured how often a stale list shows a current friend: 1.1% of users after 1.25% graph growth.

WI-8Stretch: simulated sharding System Design DSA

What you build. shard.py: a consistent-hash ring with virtual nodes over a stable hash, and shards_touched. Measure how many shards one request reaches on average for 16, 64 and 256 shards, and what share of users move to a new home when a 17th shard joins, against simple mod-N placement. Then turn the fan-out into a tail: if any one call is slow 1% of the time, what share of requests waits on a slow one?

Why. At a billion users the friend lists do not fit on one machine (Day 5 §1). Every shard a request touches is another chance to be slow, and every reshuffle of users is data you have to move.

Done when PYMK_STRETCH=1 pytest -q -k stretch is green and your table is close to the reference: 10.0, 15.5 and 18.2 shards per request for 16, 64 and 256 shards; adding a 17th shard moves 0.062 of users (1/17 = 0.059), where mod-N moves 0.941.

  • Python's hash() of a string changes between processes. Use hashlib.blake2b, or your ring changes every time you restart.
  • Day 5 §3's ring, with its own hash, moved 0.0602 of users and mod-N 0.9442: any well-mixed hash lands near 1/17 and 16/17, so small differences from the reference are expected.
  • A request touches at most as many shards as you have friends; with 20 friends on average, adding shards stops adding calls quickly.
  • For the tail, use Day 5 §6: a request waiting on s calls is slow with probability \(1-(1-p)^s\).

Checks · Day 1 · Common friends
What a good answer covers

Candidates are friends of your friends who are not you and not already your friends: Esha 2 (Ben, Chloe), Dev 1, Farid 1, and Dev before Farid because the tie goes to the smaller id. Ben and Chloe are excluded by a rule, not a score.

The counting pass reads each friend's list once and adds one to everyone on it: for You, 4 + 4 = 8 entries. So the cost is \(\sum_{f \in N(u)} d_f\), set by your friends' lengths, not your own. Each list of length d is read once by each of its owner's d friends, so it costs \(d \times d\) over all users, and the total is \(\sum_v d_v^2\): 14,768,324 on the week's data, a mean of 1,477 per user, median 1,023, worst 51,806 (the user with the 1,480-friend hub). Intersecting with each friend of a friend instead reads 13.3 times more.

Back to the problem and Day 1 §7.

What a good answer covers

Hash set: probe the larger set with each id of the shorter list, \(O(a)\) lookups if the set exists, \(O(a+b)\) if you build it; a set costs about 22 times the memory of a sorted array (131,288 bytes against 5,920 for the 1,480-id list). Two-pointer merge on sorted lists: at most \(a+b\) steps, no extra memory. Binary search: \(a\log_2 b\). Galloping: about \(a\log_2(b/a)\), never far from the better of the two. Bitsets: \(n/64\) words whatever the sizes (157 for 10,000 users) at \(n/8\) bytes per list.

Measured operations, small against hub: hash 12.7, binary 138.9, galloping 157.8, merge 910.4 (the merge walks most of the hub's list, 5.8 times galloping). Hub against hub: bitset 157, hash 558.2, merge 1,474.9, galloping 2,050.6, binary 6,175.1. Sizes decide the winner, and operation counts, not pure-Python times, are the fair comparison.

Back to Day 1 §1–6.

What a good answer covers

One pair: ship the smaller list to the larger one and return a count. A median user against the 1,480-id hub: 15 × 4 + 4 = 64 bytes against 5,920, 92 times fewer.

All pairs: map each user's list to every pair on it with a 1, shuffle by pair, reduce by summing; the sum for (x, y) is their mutual-friend count. A list of length d emits \(d(d-1)/2\) records: 7,284,162 in all. The 1,480-friend list alone emits 1,094,460 (15.0%), and the 7 users with 500+ friends emit 49.1%; their mapper is 10,423 times the median one, a straggler. Reducers are fine when routed by a hash of the pair (1.018 times the average load). Fixes, measured: skip lists over 500, 50.9% of the shuffle and 96.5% of the top-10 value kept; sample hub friends; or emit only for users you will serve (10% of users: 20.3% of the shuffle, exact for them).

Back to Day 1 §8–9.

What a good answer covers

In a random order of all users, the first member of \(A \cup B\) is equally likely to be any of them; the two "first friends" agree exactly when it lies in \(A \cap B\): probability \(|A\cap B|/|A\cup B| = J\). With k orders the share of agreements estimates J with error \(\sqrt{J(1-J)/k}\): measured 0.0191 against 0.0197 predicted at k = 64 on typical pairs. But the mean J of two-step pairs is only 0.0262, so the error is most of the signal on this data.

LSH: cut the signature into b bands of r rows; a pair becomes a candidate with probability \(1-(1-J^r)^b\), an S-curve whose step sits near \((1/b)^{1/r}\). Place the step at the similarity you want: r = 2, b = 50 puts it at 0.141 and predicts recall 0.481 on pairs with \(J \ge 0.1\); measured 0.480. At the same step more rows cost less: (2, 100) emits 2.6 times fewer pairs than (1, 10). Candidates are not answers: even the best setting has only 1.3% of candidates at \(J \ge 0.1\), so check each one exactly.

Checks · Day 2 · The top k
What a good answer covers

Sort: \(O(n\log n)\), 9.41 comparisons per candidate. Size-k min-heap with the worst kept on top: \(O(n\log k)\), but most candidates lose to the root in one comparison: 1.17 per candidate; the heap changes about \(k(1+\ln(n/k))\) times (77.2 predicted, 78.1 measured for 8,271 candidates). Quickselect: \(O(n)\) on average, 2.18 per candidate. Bucket counting: 1.06 steps, 5.19 times faster than sorting, because mutual-friend counts are small whole numbers (86.6% equal 1); it disappears once the score is a model's probability, and the heap survives every change of score.

Ties: 92.7% of users with more than ten candidates tie between their 10th and 11th. Without a rule in the key, tests fail at random, cached lists reshuffle on refresh, and A/B arms get noise. Put it in every key: (count, −id) in the heap.

What a good answer covers

Exact upkeep: Dev–Chloe on the six people makes six count changes and You's top 2 becomes Dev, Esha. A count that rises costs one comparison with the worst of the ten; a candidate that becomes a friend and was in the ten forces a recompute. Replaying all 100,000 friendships: 143 count changes per friendship, only 5.2% of them reach anyone's top 10, and 16,795 recomputes.

In the last hour (20,000 arrivals) the leader gained 142, so \(m \ge 141\) before it is safe; at m = 500 both find the whole top 10, Space-Saving within 1.6 on average, Misra–Gries still 37.4 under. Checks · Day 3 · It is a graph
What a good answer covers

The walk reads every friend's whole list, so it costs the sum of your friends' degrees, and friendships lead to popular people more often. Summed over everyone each user of degree d causes \(d^2\) reads: \(\sum_v \deg(v)^2 = 14{,}768{,}324\), the top 1% of users cause 69% of the work, and the 1,480-friend hub alone 14.8%.

Proof: follow a random line to one of its ends. A user with \(d_v\) friends is reached with probability \(d_v/\sum_w d_w\), so the expected degree there is \(\mathbb{E}[d^2]/\mathbb{E}[d]\). Subtract the mean: \((\mathbb{E}[d^2]-\mathbb{E}[d]^2)/\mathbb{E}[d] = \operatorname{Var}(d)/\mathbb{E}[d] \ge 0\), with equality only when everyone has the same degree. Measured: 73.8 against 20.

Skipping the 27 users with more than 200 friends cuts mean work from 1,477 to 597 (60%) and p99 from 7,284 to 2,909, while recall@10 on the time split goes from 0.0524 to 0.0533: a hub is an expensive mutual friend and a weak one.

Back to Day 3 §3.

What a good answer covers

Triadic closure: friends introduce friends. Average clustering is 0.1104 against about 0.0020 for a random graph, and the share of new friendships that closed a triangle rose from 13.8% in the first tenth of the history to 63.4% in the last.

Recall@10 (train on the first 80,000 friendships, test on the next 20,000): Jaccard 0.0287, mutual friends 0.0524, resource allocation 0.0616, Adamic–Adar 0.0638. Adamic–Adar and resource allocation weight a mutual friend by \(1/\ln\deg\) and \(1/\deg\), so a hub votes less. Jaccard divides by the union and pushes sociable candidates down, though sociable people make most new friendships: the median degree of suggested people is 86 for the count, 10 for Jaccard. The ceiling: only 48.4% of a user's future friends are two steps away.

Personalised PageRank (walk with restart, α = 0.3) reaches 0.0674, mostly by ranking two-step candidates better: only 13 of its 2,292 hits are further away. Its error after t rounds is at most \(2(1-\alpha)^t\), and 30 rounds cost 4,800,000 updates per user, so it runs offline.

Back to Day 3 §4–6.

What a good answer covers

Union-find: each person points towards a root; union by rank and path compression keep trees short (within \(\log_2 n\) with rank alone). Feeding the week's friendships in order, exactly 9,999 joined two islands and 90,001 closed a cycle. It cannot delete, so unfriending needs a periodic rebuild. Uses: merging duplicate accounts and contact-import clusters.

Partitioning: hashing puts a friend elsewhere with probability \((p-1)/p\): 87.2% of two-step reads are remote at 8 machines. Locality placement trims that to 76.7% on this graph. Copying the top 1% of lists to every machine cuts it to 27.1%, because hubs cause most reads; the price is storage (seven extra copies add 78% at p = 8). BFS and PageRank then run as vertex-centric rounds whose cost is one message per list entry: BFS from user 8604 takes 4 rounds and 200,000 messages, 87.6% of them crossing machines under hashing.

Checks · Day 4 · Learn what engagement means
What a good answer covers

Five countable events: impression, click, request sent, accepted, interaction later. Clicks reward curiosity; interaction is truest but late and rare. Target: request sent and accepted within a fixed window, two-sided and fast enough to learn from. The week's graph has no impressions, so offline the label is "became friends after the cut", with features only from before it.

Log loss punishes confident wrongness (−log 0.9 ≈ 0.11, −log 0.1 = 2.30), is smooth, and its gradient for logistic regression is \((p-y)\,x\), a convex problem. It is a proper scoring rule: if pairs connect 20% of the time, reporting 0.1, 0.2, 0.3 costs 0.5448, 0.5004, 0.5261, so honesty wins; absolute error would reward reporting 0. Accuracy is a staircase with no gradient, and "predict no" scores 99.73% on the test pairs.

Weights (standardised): Adamic–Adar +1.573, mutual friends −0.813. With Adamic–Adar held fixed, more mutual friends means they are bigger hubs, so the model discounts hubs more steeply than \(1/\log\) does. Boosted trees add +0.0011 [−0.0013, +0.0033] recall@10 over the line: not established.

What a good answer covers

With one positive in 364, ROC-AUC flatters. PR-AUC is 0.0214 against a random baseline of 0.0027, and the top of the list is what users see: recall@10 0.0718 [0.0686, 0.0753] and precision@10 0.0258, against 0.0524 for the mutual-friends rule and 0.0638 for Adamic–Adar. Report paired differences over the same users: +0.0080 [+0.0053, +0.0108] over Adamic–Adar. Bootstrap over users, not pairs: one user's candidates share that user's friends. Slice: users with one to five friends gain +0.0429 over the rule.

Calibration: training kept each negative with probability r = 0.05, which multiplies the odds by 1/r, so add \(\log r = -3.00\) to every score. The calibration error falls from 0.05908 to 0.00137. What the shift cannot fix is drift: the model still predicts 0.411% on average against 0.274% observed, because the graph grew between the windows. Ceiling: only 53.2% of future friends were candidates, so no friends-of-friends ranker can exceed 0.484.

What a good answer covers

Rates 0.1819 and 0.2249; pooled rate 1,903 / 9,357 = 0.2034; standard error \(\sqrt{0.2034 \times 0.7966 \times (1/4689 + 1/4668)} = 0.00832\); z = 0.0430 / 0.00832 = 5.17; two-sided p = 2.3 × 10⁻⁷. Power: with 4,689 users per arm at a 17.51% baseline, the minimum detectable effect is 2.20 points (12.6% relative); a +5% relative lift needs 30,168 users per arm. Interference: friendships cross arms, so a user-level test saw 64% of the true lift; cluster randomisation saw 87% at nearly twice the spread (0.0151 against 0.0085). What a good answer covers

You only have labels for pairs the old system showed: the simulated log of 93,511 impressions holds 1,764 of the 21,290 friendships that formed (8.3%). Any pair the new ranker likes and the old one never showed scores zero, so the log can only confirm the old system: −27.6% on the log, +30.6% in truth.

Inverse propensity weighting: weight each logged outcome by \(1/\pi\), its chance of being shown. It is unbiased (old system 0.1987 against a truth of 0.1972; new system 0.2663 averaged over 20 logs against 0.2576) but noisy, a standard deviation of 0.1150 for one log, because one exploration slot among about 800 candidates has tiny propensities. Defences: log the propensity, keep an exploration bucket, treat never-shown as unlabelled rather than negative (training that way cost −0.0034 recall@10), and correct for position.

What a good answer covers

A one-hot id gives one weight per person (10,000 columns here, a billion at scale), learned only from that person's few rows, and "u likes v" would need a column per pair: \(10^8\) here. Ids do not generalise; hand features do. Checks · Day 5 · A billion users
What a good answer covers

Label every input. \(10^9 \times 200 / 2\) = 100 billion friendships; 200 billion ids × 8 bytes = 1.6 TB, seven 256 GB servers: storage is not the problem. 500 million active users × 2 loads = \(10^9\) requests a day, 11,574 per second, 34,722 at a peak of three times the average. Two hops read \(200 \times 200 \times 3.69 = 147{,}600\) ids, where 3.69 is the measured \(\mathbb{E}[d^2]/\mathbb{E}[d]^2\), from about 181 of 1,000 shards: 41.0 GB/s at peak. Reads break first.

So precompute every user's list in batch into a key-value store (1.2 TB for 100 suggestions each), read one key per request, and compute online only for new users and users whose friendships just changed. Staleness, replayed: after 1.25% graph growth 25.8% of top 10s differ but only 3.4% are materially stale; 1.1% show a current friend, which the request-time filter removes.

Back to Day 5 §1–2.

What a good answer covers

Mod N: key k stays only if \(k \bmod N = k \bmod (N+1)\). Over a cycle of \(N(N+1)\) keys that holds for exactly N of them, a share \(1/(N+1)\), so \(N/(N+1)\) move: 0.9442 measured for 16 → 17 against 16/17 = 0.9412. On a ring a new server takes only the arcs just before its points, so about \(1/(N+1)\) move, all to it: 6.02% measured against 5.88%. Virtual nodes even out the shares; their spread falls like \(1/\sqrt{V}\) (0.104 at 100 per server, 0.031 at 1,000).

A friend list is read once per friend and returns the whole list, so bytes follow degree squared: the top 1% of lists get 69.1% of ids read, and adding shards does not help a key that lives on one. Replicate the hot set: the fullest of 16 shards falls from 3.08 to 1.06 times the mean with a copy on every shard. Precomputing removes hub reads from the request path altogether (the suggestion store's fullest shard is 1.22).

Back to Day 5 §3.

What a good answer covers

Adding u–v changes exactly two friend lists: v joins u's and u joins v's. For a pair (w, x) with neither equal to u or v, both lists are unchanged, so their count is unchanged. For a friend x of u, the shared set of x and v gains u; for a friend x of v, the shared set of x and u gains v; and u and v stop being candidates for each other. So the users touched are u, v and their friends, nobody else: exactly the set the lab's add_friendship invalidates. Replayed: 127.0 users and 126.2 pairs per friendship on average (a hub friendship touched 1,747 users), and only 3.0% of count changes move a top 10, so write to the store only when the list moves.

The cache (an LRU: hash map plus recency list, \(O(1)\) per operation) stores the ranking, never the filtered answer. The ranking was computed earlier; since then someone may have blocked, hidden themselves, sent a request or become a friend. Those rules are absolute, so they are checked at read time, on every response. Batches publish as a new version with one pointer flip, so a reader never sees half of one and rollback is one flip back.

Back to Day 5 §4–5 and §9.

What a good answer covers

A request is fast only if all s calls are: \(0.99^s\), so \(1-0.99^s\) wait on a slow one: 0.096 at s = 10, 0.634 at 100, 0.838 at 181; the simulation measured 0.094, 0.633 and 0.837. At 100 shards even the median request takes 51.72 ms. Hedging (resend at the p95 time, take the first answer) makes a call slow only if both copies stall, about \(p^2\): at 100 shards the slow share falls to 0.0098 and the p99 from 54.00 ms to 8.93 ms for 5.0% extra calls; at 181 shards it is still 1.83%, so past a point you must cut s. The levers: cut s, cut p, or stop waiting (and never skip the filter).

Little's law, \(L = \lambda W\): 34,722 requests per second × 0.050 s = 1,736 in flight; fanned out to 181 shards that is 314,216 outstanding calls, against 1,736 when each request reads one precomputed key.

What a good answer covers

Training–serving skew: the model sees inputs at serving time it never saw in training. On the replay, 2.4% of mutual-friend feature values differ after 1.25% growth, 18.6% after 12.5%. Fixes: one feature code path for batch and server, log the features actually served, train the next model on the log (which also removes the future leak Day 4 warned about), and monitor the gap daily.

Fair exposure: the top 1% of users by degree take 50.1% of shown slots but are only 10.8% of the endpoints of future friendships. A cap of 20 showings per refresh gives them 2.2% and shows 92.4% of people to someone (76.8% without), at a measured cost: the share of shown slots that became friendships falls from 0.0185 to 0.0099. That offline number understates the value of the data exposure buys. Cold start: of the 129 future friendships of the 71 users with at most 2 friends, two hops find 18.6%, a shared group 58.9%, either 70.5%: onboarding signals carry new users.

Say it out loud, as in the room

Three kinds of round, in the order of the week. For each, answer first, out loud and against the clock you would face, then open what a strong answer covers. The ★ problems are the ones to be able to do live.

DSA · coding, by day

Day 1 · Common friends

  • ★ 349. Intersection of Two Arrays: hash set over the distinct values; then the sorted, huge and tight-memory follow-ups.
  • 350. Intersection of Two Arrays II: repeats count, so a count map, or a merge that emits once per matched pair.
  • 2540. Minimum Common Value: the merge, stopped at the first match.
  • 2248. Intersection of Multiple Arrays: count how many lists each value appears in, the counting pass of Day 1 §7.
  • 2215. Find the Difference of Two Arrays: the other side of intersection, with two sets.
  • 1002. Find Common Characters: per-letter counts with a running minimum over a fixed alphabet.
  • 160. Intersection of Two Linked Lists: two pointers on lists of different lengths, \(O(1)\) memory.
  • 986. Interval List Intersections: the merge over sorted intervals; advance whichever ends first.
What a strong answer covers · ★349
  • Clarify: each value once in the output, any order; are the arrays sorted; how different are their sizes; do they fit in memory?
  • Say the plan and its cost before coding: a set from one array, probe with the distinct values of the other: \(O(a+b)\) time, \(O(\min(a,b))\) extra space if the set is built from the shorter one.
  • Test aloud: repeats, no overlap, one empty side.
  • Follow-ups: sorted, two pointers with \(O(1)\) extra space, skipping a value equal to the last one written; one side huge, binary search or galloping, \(a\log_2(b/a)\); memory tight, sorted arrays (a set is about 22 times an array); too big for one machine, ship the smaller side and return the answer.

Day 2 · The top k

  • ★ 347. Top K Frequent Elements: count, then bucket by count; a count never exceeds n.
  • ★ 703. Kth Largest Element in a Stream: a min-heap of size k; its root is the answer.
  • 215. Kth Largest Element in an Array: quickselect, with a three-way split for repeats.
  • 692. Top K Frequent Words: 347 with a tie rule; put (−count, word) in the key.
  • 973. K Closest Points to Origin: a size-k max-heap of squared distances.
  • 295. Find Median from Data Stream: two heaps; change the ratio for any percentile.
  • 23. Merge k Sorted Lists: a heap of heads, \(O(N\log k)\).
  • 373. Find K Pairs with Smallest Sums: a k-way merge of lists that are never built.
What a strong answer covers · ★347 and ★703
  • 347: count with a hash map, then name the three ways to pick k (sort \(O(n\log n)\), size-k heap \(O(n\log k)\), buckets \(O(n)\)) and code buckets, which meets "better than \(O(n\log n)\)" because a count is at most n. Say the tie rule even though the problem accepts any order. Follow-up "the input is a stream": the heap.
  • 703: the heap must be a min-heap of size k, because its root is both the answer and what a newcomer must beat. \(O(\log k)\) per add, \(O(k)\) memory, however long the stream. Edge case: fewer than k numbers at the start.
  • Both: the comparison count argument (about one comparison per item for the heap, 1.17 measured) is what separates a strong answer from a correct one.

Day 3 · It is a graph

  • ★ 1971. Find if Path Exists in Graph: build adjacency lists from the edge list, then BFS.
  • 997. Find the Town Judge: in-degree minus out-degree equals n − 1.
  • 841. Keys and Rooms: BFS on a directed graph; did everyone get a label?
  • 547. Number of Provinces: union-find or BFS over a matrix, \(O(n^2)\).
  • 684. Redundant Connection: the first edge whose ends already share a root.
  • 785. Is Graph Bipartite?: colour BFS layers; a triangle makes it impossible.
  • 133. Clone Graph: BFS whose "seen" map holds the copies.
  • 1761. Minimum Degree of a Connected Trio in a Graph: list each triangle once, upward order; subtract 6 from the three degrees.
What a strong answer covers · ★1971
  • Clarify: undirected, n up to large, edges may repeat, source may equal destination.
  • Adjacency lists from the edge list, then BFS from the source with a queue, marking on discovery, not on removal, or a vertex enters the queue many times. \(O(n+m)\) time and memory.
  • Alternatives and why: DFS reaches the same vertices (watch Python's recursion limit); union-find answers "same component?" without building lists, and is the right tool if edges keep arriving.
  • Tie it back: friends of friends is BFS's second ring; a depth limit reads only the rings you need.

Day 4 · Sampling with known probabilities

  • 528. Random Pick with Weight: prefix sums and binary search; each pick's propensity is its weight over the total.
  • 382. Linked List Random Node: reservoir sampling, one uniform pick from a stream of unknown length.
  • 398. Random Pick Index: the same reservoir, over the items that match.
  • 470. Implement Rand10() Using Rand7(): rejection sampling.
What a strong answer covers
  • 528: the prefix sums split [0, total) into one interval per index of length \(w_i\); a uniform draw lands in interval i with probability \(w_i/\text{total}\). \(O(n)\) build, \(O(\log n)\) per pick.
  • 382 and 398: keep the i-th item seen with probability \(1/i\). It survives each later step j with probability \((j-1)/j\); the product telescopes to \(1/n\). \(O(1)\) memory.
  • 470: two calls give 49 equally likely outcomes; use the first 40 (four copies of each digit), reject the other 9. The expected number of rand7 calls is \(2 \times 49/40 = 2.45\).
  • Tie it back to Day 4 §11: an exploration slot is only useful offline if each pick's probability is known and logged.

Day 5 · Caches and versioned stores

  • ★ 146. LRU Cache: hash map plus doubly linked list with sentinels.
  • 460. LFU Cache: frequency buckets and the current minimum frequency.
  • 981. Time Based Key-Value Store: a sorted history per key, binary search "as of t".
  • 1146. Snapshot Array: record only the indices that change, per snapshot id.
  • 380. Insert Delete GetRandom O(1): an array plus an index map; delete by swapping with the last.
  • 355. Design Twitter: follows, and a feed merged from many lists with a heap.
What a strong answer covers · ★146
  • State the contract: \(O(1)\) get and put, evict the least recently used when full, and a get counts as a use.
  • Data structure: a hash map from key to node, and a doubly linked list in order of use with head and tail sentinels so no operation needs a special case. The map stores the node, not the value, because moving to the front needs the node's place.
  • Edge cases: capacity 1; put on an existing key updates the value and moves it to the front without evicting.
  • Mention OrderedDict with move_to_end as the short version, then write the linked list if asked.
  • Follow-up "when does LRU lose?": on independent requests with fixed popularity a pinned hot set beats it (at 1% cache size, 0.107 against 0.030 on Day 5's stream); with reloads LRU serves 51.6%. Combine: pin the hottest, LRU for the rest.
ML · design, from Day 4

1 · "Design the ranker for People You May Know."

What a strong answer covers
  • Business first: the win is a request sent and accepted within a window; say why not clicks and not 30-day interaction.
  • The function: \(f(u,v) \to P(\text{accepted} \mid \text{shown})\), a calibrated probability, and the four reasons a probability beats a score.
  • Baseline: mutual friends, shipped first and kept as the fallback; Adamic–Adar as the sharpened rule.
  • Data: one row per impression, point-in-time features, label in a fixed window, dismissals as true negatives; negatives sampled with a known rate and the \(\log r\) correction.
  • Features by group (graph, shared world, receptiveness, behaviour, negative, context), with the claim that pair features matter more than either person's.
  • Model ladder: logistic regression, then boosted trees or crosses, then learned representations, each only if it beats the last offline and online. Split into candidate generation (high recall) and ranking (high precision).
  • Evaluation and the loop: PR-AUC and recall@K on a time split, sliced, against the baseline; an A/B test; exposure bias and exploration.

2 · "Our new model's ROC-AUC went from 0.805 to 0.832. Ship it?"

What a strong answer covers
  • Not on ROC-AUC alone: with one positive in 364 it rewards ordering the easy middle. Ask for PR-AUC (0.0214 against a 0.0027 baseline) and recall@10 or precision@10, which match the ten slots.
  • Ask for intervals over users and a paired difference against the old model (+0.0080 [+0.0053, +0.0108] recall@10 over Adamic–Adar on the week's graph), and slices: the new-user slice can move the other way.
  • Two models can even disagree by metric: trees beat the line on ROC-AUC (0.837) and lose on PR-AUC (0.0192 against 0.0214).
  • Check the evaluation itself: time split, no future in the features, and whether the test labels came from the old model's impressions (exposure bias).
  • Then an A/B test with a fixed sample size, guardrails and the ship rule.

3 · "Design the online experiment for the new ranker."

What a strong answer covers
  • Unit: users by a sticky hash of (experiment, user id), so a person stays in one arm on every device and experiments split independently.
  • Before starting: one primary metric (accepted connections per user), guardrails (dismissals, reports and blocks, notification volume, latency p99), sample size from the baseline and the minimum effect worth shipping, and the decision rule.
  • Read it: a two-proportion z-test, the p-value as surprise under "no effect", the interval as the size.
  • Pitfalls: peeking (24.7% false positives at 20 looks), novelty (plot the effect by days since first exposure), and network interference, which dilutes the lift (64% of the truth per user); cluster randomisation for a big launch, at a higher variance.

4 · "Retraining on last month's logs made things worse. Why?"

What a strong answer covers
  • The logs were written by the old model: only shown pairs have labels, so the new model learns the old one's blind spots; treating never-shown as negative teaches it they were bad.
  • Evaluation on those logs can reverse a verdict (−27.6% on the log against +30.6% in truth on Day 4's simulation).
  • Fixes: log propensities, inverse propensity weighting for training and counterfactual evaluation (unbiased but noisy), an exploration bucket, position as a training-only feature.
  • Also check training–serving skew: were features recomputed after the fact instead of logged as served (Day 5 §7)?

5 · "A user signs up with no friends. What do they see?"

What a strong answer covers
  • Two hops find nothing with no friends and little with few. Onboarding signals carry it: groups and schools chosen at sign-up, consented contacts, city. On the week's graph, for users with at most 2 friends, a shared group finds 58.9% of their future friendships against 18.6% for two hops.
  • Compute these users online (their scan is tiny) until the next batch includes them.
  • Learned vectors help sparse users (+0.0238 recall@10 for users with one to five friends); two towers give a vector from features alone for a brand-new user.
  • Respect the rules: contact-import consent, minors, private reasons.
System Design · from Day 5, with the scale parts of Days 1–3

1 · "Design People You May Know for a billion users. Suggestions within 200 ms at p99; a new friendship should change the list within the hour."

What a strong answer covers
  1. Clarify and estimate, with labelled inputs: 1.6 TB of lists, 34,722 requests per second at peak, about 147,600 ids per two-hop scan. Reads per request break first.
  2. The rules that hold on every response (self, friends, blocks both ways, pending requests, minors, consent), run last on fresh data.
  3. The funnel: candidates (two hops, groups, contacts), features, a calibrated ranker, the filter.
  4. Batch and online: nightly precompute into a versioned key-value store; online only for new users; incremental updates from the friendship stream for the one-hour freshness, writing only when a top 10 moves.
  5. Scale mechanics: consistent hashing with virtual nodes; hub lists as hot keys, replicated; an LRU cache; hedging for the remaining fan-out; Little's law to size the fleet.
  6. Measurement: recall on a time split, an A/B test with guardrails, training–serving skew handled by logging served features.
  7. Product risks: privacy of reasons, notification fatigue, fair exposure, cold start.

2 · "Compute every user's mutual-friend counts overnight."

What a strong answer covers
  • Map each friend list to every pair on it with a 1; shuffle by pair; reduce by summing; drop pairs already friends; keep top k per user in the reducer.
  • The shuffle is \(\sum d(d-1)/2\): the heavy tail, not the mean, sets the cost; one hub's mapper is a straggler (7 users emit 49.1% on the week's graph).
  • Fixes with their cost: cap or sample hub fan-out, emit only for users to be served, route reducers by a hash of the pair.

3 · "Counts are spread over shards. Return each user's exact top 10."

What a strong answer covers
  • Ask how the data is split. If each candidate lives on one shard, local top k plus a heap-of-heads merge is exact.
  • If each count is a sum of partial counts, local top k misses winners (the Esha 2 + 2 counterexample); aggregate by candidate first, or prune with TPUT, and say when TPUT pays (large winning scores relative to shard count).
  • Keep the request path off this: do the aggregation in batch, store each user's list on one shard, because fan-out makes a request as slow as its slowest shard.

4 · "Partition the friend graph across machines."

What a strong answer covers
  • Hash partitioning: even load, no directory, but \((p-1)/p\) of two-step reads are remote.
  • Locality placement (label propagation within a balance limit) helps more on real graphs with schools and towns than on the synthetic one.
  • Replicate the hub lists: they dominate reads, so copying 1% of lists cut remote reads to 27.1% at 8 machines, at a storage cost.
  • Iterative algorithms (BFS, PageRank) as vertex-centric rounds; combine messages per machine; few rounds.

5 · "Every backend meets its p99, but the page's p99 is terrible."

What a strong answer covers
  • Fan-out: a request waiting on s calls is slow with probability \(1-(1-p)^s\); at 100 calls, 63% of requests hit a 1% tail, so a request's p99 is set by its backends' p99.99.
  • Levers: cut s (precompute, batch by shard), cut p (hedge at the p95 time, about 5% extra calls), or stop waiting (deadline, rank with what arrived, never skip the filter).
  • Size concurrency with Little's law; a slower tail raises W and the in-flight count.

6 · "Now do it for a follow graph, a professional network, or people to work with inside a company."

What a strong answer covers
  • Keep the skeleton (candidates, features, ranker, rules; precomputed and patched) and change four things: target, candidate sources, signals, rules.
  • Follow graph: no acceptance step, so "follows and keeps following"; celebrity hot keys and rich-get-richer are sharper.
  • Professional network: shared employers and dates; recruiter traffic skews the labels; never reveal a job search.
  • Inside a company: org chart and collaboration graph; the main risk is surveillance, so suggestions must not reveal who meets whom.
