Ship pymk on the week's graph

Where we are. Over five days the answer to "which ten people should we suggest?" grew from a count of mutual friends into a small system: an exact baseline you can trust, a reason it gets slow, a cheaper way to find almost the same people, a model that learns who actually connects, and a service that never shows someone it must not. This weekend you build all of it yourself, in the same order, as one tested Python package.

The package lives in the lab folder week_01/: stubs in pymk/, a SPEC.md to fill in, and acceptance tests in tests/test_acceptance.py. The graph is the one every page measured: 10,000 users and 100,000 friendships, rebuilt by data/make_graph.py with seed 0.

spec + failing tests→exact baseline→workload→budgeted candidates→LSH source→ranker→serving: filter + cache→stretch: shards
One box per work item, left to right. Each box has its own section of tests in tests/test_acceptance.py.

WI-1Spec and failing tests Delivery

What you build. The You write sections of SPEC.md: who uses the feature and how it fails them, what you will not build, your estimates (before any code), your decisions and your risks. Then tests/test_mine.py: at least one test per module, each named after the bug it catches.

Why. Writing down what "right" means before the code exists is what lets you change the code later without fear. The estimates are there so that WI-3 and WI-7 can show you how far off your intuition was.

Done when a commit exists in which every row of section 6 of SPEC.md has an estimate, and every test in test_mine.py and test_acceptance.py fails.

  • Start from the six-person graph of the week reading: You, your friends Ben and Chloe, and Dev, Esha and Farid. Write the expected answer by hand (Esha first with two mutual friends, then Dev before Farid on the tie) before you read the acceptance test.
  • Good test names say the bug: test_friend_never_suggested, test_tie_goes_to_smaller_id, test_block_applies_to_cached_ranking.
  • For a differential test, the slow version that is obviously right is the matrix square from Day 1: squaring the friendship matrix counts the paths of length two between every pair.

WI-2The exact baseline and its properties DSA Delivery

What you build. graph.py (load the data, build friend lists, split by time, measure work) and exact.py (mutual_counts, top_k, suggest). Then three property tests with Hypothesis in your own test file: the count for (u, v) equals the count for (v, u); nothing ineligible is ever returned; the counts equal the matrix square on random small graphs.

Why. Every later module is measured against this one. Recall@10 in WI-4 and WI-5 means "how much of the exact answer did we keep", so the exact answer has to be right first.

Done when pytest -q -k "load or adjacency or split or work or golden or ties or never_self or mutual or top_k or suggest" is green and your three properties pass on at least 200 random graphs each.

  • Count with a dict while you walk your friends' lists. Do not build the candidate set first and then intersect: that reads every list twice.
  • For top_k, keep a heap of size k instead of sorting every candidate (Day 1 compares the costs). Order by "more mutual friends first, then smaller id": that one sort key is also the tie-break rule.
  • temporal_split must sort by time. A test shuffles the edges first, so cutting the list by position fails it.

WI-3Workload report System Design ML

What you build. A short script that measures, for every user, how many friend-list entries the exact algorithm reads (the work W, the sum of your friends' friend counts), plots its distribution in buckets, and times exact.suggest for every user, reporting p50, p95 and p99 with metrics.percentile. Find the slowest user and explain their time from the friend counts of their friends.

Why. Day 2 showed that popular people make some requests far more expensive than the average. This report is where you see it on your own code, and it is the number that justifies everything in WI-4.

Done when your W numbers match the week's exactly (they are deterministic: mean 1,477, median 1,023, p99 7,284, max 51,806), your report plots time against W, and the SPEC row "p50/p95/p99 time" has a measured value next to your estimate, with the gap explained.

  • Time one call with time.perf_counter(), run each user once first to warm up, and keep every sample: a percentile needs the samples, not a running mean.
  • One acceptance test checks that the total work over all users equals the sum of every user's friend count squared. Convince yourself first, as on Day 2: each person is read once by each of their friends.

WI-4Hub capping and sampled candidates ML DSA

What you build. sample.py: sample_counts, which reads only some of your friends and scales the count back up so it is right on average (Day 3), and budgeted_candidates, which stops reading at a fixed budget and can skip any friend with too many friends of their own (the hub cap, Day 2). Then draw the curve: mean recall@10 against the exact top 10, for read budgets from 250 to 8,000, with no cap and with a cap of 200, each point with a bootstrap 95% interval over users.

Why. A fixed budget turns the long tail from WI-3 into a flat cost per request. The curve tells you what that costs in quality, and where extra reads stop paying off.

Done when the tests in the sample.py section pass and your curve is close to the one below (500 random users, seed 0), with one paragraph on why the capped curve stops rising.

WI-5A MinHash and LSH candidate source DSA

What you build. minhash.py. Give every user a short MinHash signature of their friend list, put the signatures in an LSH index split into b bands of r rows, and query it with your own friend list. For at least three settings of (b, r), report the share of the exact top 10 that LSH returns and how many candidates it returns on average.

Why. LSH finds people whose whole friend lists look like yours without reading anyone's list at query time. Whether that matches "has mutual friends with you" is exactly what this table answers.

Done when the tests in the minhash.py section pass, your table covers at least three settings, and you explain your numbers with the S-curve from Day 3 (the chance that two lists with a given similarity share at least one band).

200 users. Over the exact top-10 pairs, the median Jaccard similarity of the two friend lists is 0.017 (mean 0.031): two or three mutual friends out of a combined list of dozens.

  • Hash with all num_perm functions at once: an outer product of the coefficients and the items, then a minimum along one axis.
  • Test with scattered ids, not range(100): a linear hash on consecutive integers behaves nothing like a random shuffle.
  • Read the table as a finding about the data: LSH finds lists that are similar as wholes, and mutual-friend candidates share only a sliver of their lists. Say where a similarity-based source would still help, for example a new user with no mutual friends yet.

WI-6The ranker, with error bars ML AI

What you build. rank.py: the eight pair features, build_dataset, the logistic loss and its gradient, and LogisticRegression in plain NumPy. Train on features from the first 60% of friendships with labels from the next 20%; test on features from the first 80% with labels from the last 20%. Report AUC for each single feature and for the model, with bootstrap 95% intervals, and recall@10 of each user's future friends for common neighbours and for the model.

Why. Mutual-friend count is a guess about who will connect. Day 4 replaced the guess with a model trained on what people actually did, and the time split is what keeps that model honest: it predicts the future from the past, as the app must.

Done when the tests in the rank.py section pass and your numbers are close to the ones below, including the paired difference in recall@10 with its interval.

Two ways to compute AUC

AUC is the chance that the model scores a real future friendship above a pair that did not form. What changes the number is which pairs get compared. build_dataset pairs each future friend of a user with one other candidate of the same user: a friend of a friend who did not connect. Every user therefore brings as many negatives as positives, and the question is "among this person's own candidates, who will they connect with?". Day 4 pools all candidate pairs of all users into one set, so a future friend of one user is often compared with a non-friend of another user.

The within-user comparison is harder. Two candidates of the same person share that person's friends, friend count and city, so the score can only win on what differs between the candidates. In a pool, the score can also win on differences between the users themselves. That is why the same features score lower here: common neighbours 0.590 against 0.620 pooled on Day 4, Adamic–Adar 0.721 against 0.805, logistic regression 0.742 against 0.832. The within-user number is the closer match to the app's job, which is ordering one person's list. Whichever you compute, say which pairs it compares.

  • Check your gradient against finite differences before you train anything.
  • Open make_graph.py and work out the chance that one triadic-closure step from u lands on v. Compare it with the resource-allocation score.

WI-7Serving: the final filter and the cache System Design Delivery

What you build. serve.py: an LRUCache and a PYMKService. The cache stores each user's ranking. The eligibility filter (not yourself, not current friends, nobody blocked in either direction, nobody who has hidden themselves) runs on every response, cached or not. add_friendship clears the cache for exactly the users whose counts can change. Plug in your ranker with rank.make_scorer, then report p50 and p95 of recommend with a cold cache and a warm one.

Why. The cache is what makes the service cheap; the filter is what makes it trustworthy. A cached ranking was computed earlier, and a block must still take effect on the very next screen.

Done when the tests in the serve.py section pass, your own tests show that a block, a hide and a new friendship each take effect on the very next response, and your report gives cold and warm percentiles. In the reference run the warm p95 was 489 times lower than the cold one (0.004 ms against 1.82 ms).

  • Cache the ranking, never the filtered answer: a filtered answer goes stale the moment someone blocks or hides.
  • When u and v become friends, the users whose suggestions can change are u, v, and the friends of each (Day 5). Prove it before you code it; learning check 13 walks through it.
  • Copy the graph in the constructor. A service that changes its caller's friend lists breaks every measurement that follows.

WI-8Stretch: simulated sharding System Design DSA

What you build. shard.py: a consistent-hash ring with virtual nodes over a stable hash, and shards_touched. Measure how many shards one request reaches on average for 16, 64 and 256 shards, and what share of users move to a new home when a 17th shard joins, against simple mod-N placement. Then turn the fan-out into a tail: if any one call is slow 1% of the time, what share of requests waits on a slow one?

Why. At a billion users the friend lists do not fit on one machine (Day 5). Every shard a request touches is another chance to be slow, and every reshuffle of users is data you have to move.

Done when PYMK_STRETCH=1 pytest -q -k stretch is green and your table is close to the reference: 10.0, 15.5 and 18.2 shards per request for 16, 64 and 256 shards; adding a 17th shard moves 0.062 of users (1/17 = 0.059), where mod-N moves 0.941.

  • Python's hash() of a string changes between processes. Use hashlib.blake2b, or your ring changes every time you restart.
  • A request touches at most as many shards as you have friends; with 20 friends on average, adding shards stops adding calls quickly.
Work items
LEARNING CHECKS

Can you answer these without looking?

Answer each one aloud or on paper first, then open the answers. Each answer ends with the day that teaches it; if an answer surprises you, go back there.

Learning checks
Answers
  1. A candidate is a friend of one of your friends who is not you and not already your friend. Your mutual count with them is the number of friends you share. Write the friendships as a 0/1 matrix A. The entry of \(A^2\) for (u, v) is \(\sum_w A_{uw}A_{wv}\), and a term is 1 exactly when w is a friend of both, so the sum counts the shared friends. Back to the reading and Day 1.
  2. Hash: put the larger list in a hash set and probe it with each element of the smaller one; expected cost proportional to the smaller list. Best when the sets are already in memory as hash sets. Sorted merge: walk both sorted lists with two pointers; cost proportional to both lengths added. Best when both are sorted and of similar size. Galloping: for each element of the smaller sorted list, search forward in the larger one by doubling and then binary search; cost \(O(a\log(b/a))\) for lengths \(a\le b\). Best when one list is far shorter, for example a user with 20 friends against the top hub with 1,480. Back to Day 1.
  3. Triadic closure: two people with a friend in common are more likely to become friends, so new friendships close triangles. On the time split, 10,645 of the 20,000 future friendships (53.2%) have at least one mutual friend in the past graph. A candidate source that only proposes friends of friends can therefore find at most 53.2% of future friendships, however good the ranker behind it. The rest need other sources: shared groups, similar friend lists, random walks. Back to Day 1.
  4. The exact algorithm reads the friend list of each of your friends, so it touches \(W(u)=\sum_{f\in N(u)}\deg(f)\) entries; your own friend count only sets how many lists there are. You reach friends by following friendships, and friendships lead to popular people more often: on the week's graph the average friend has 73.8 friends, against an average of 20. Measured work: mean 1,477, p99 7,284, max 51,806. With the size-k heap, one request costs \(O(W(u)+|C(u)|\log k)\). Back to Day 2.
  5. Pick a friendship uniformly at random, then one of its two ends. A user v with \(d_v\) friends is picked with probability \(d_v/\sum_w d_w=d_v/(n\,\mathbb{E}[d])\). So the expected friend count of the picked person is \(\sum_v d_v\cdot d_v/(n\,\mathbb{E}[d])=\mathbb{E}[d^2]/\mathbb{E}[d]\). Subtract the average: \(\mathbb{E}[d^2]/\mathbb{E}[d]-\mathbb{E}[d]=(\mathbb{E}[d^2]-\mathbb{E}[d]^2)/\mathbb{E}[d]=\operatorname{Var}(d)/\mathbb{E}[d]\ge 0\), with equality exactly when everyone has the same number of friends. Measured: 73.8 against 20. Back to Day 2.
  6. The mean is dominated by the many cheap requests and hides the few users who wait a long time; with a heavy-tailed workload those few are the ones who complain, and a page that fans out sees the slow calls often. The p99 is the time that 99% of requests beat. From n timing samples, sort them and take the one at rank about 0.99n. To put an interval on it, resample the n samples with replacement many times, compute the p99 of each resample, and take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of those values. The interval is wide, because only about 1% of the samples sit near the p99. Back to Day 2.
  7. Let m be the true mutual count and d your friend count. Sampling s friends without replacement, each friend is in the sample with probability s/d, so each of the m shared friends adds s/d to the expected count: \(\mathbb{E}[\text{count}]=m\,s/d\), and scaling by d/s gives m. The count is hypergeometric, with variance \(s\,\tfrac{m}{d}(1-\tfrac{m}{d})\tfrac{d-s}{d-1}\), and the scaled estimate has \((d/s)^2\) times that. For d = 10, s = 3, m = 4: 0.560 and 6.22, as the acceptance test checks. The factor \((d-s)/(d-1)\) is zero when you read every friend: no sampling, no noise. Back to Day 3.
  8. Shuffle all items at random. The first item of \(A\cup B\) in the shuffle is equally likely to be any of its elements, and the two MinHash values agree exactly when that item lies in \(A\cap B\): probability \(|A\cap B|/|A\cup B|\), the Jaccard similarity J. Mutual-friend candidates have tiny J (median 0.017 over exact top-10 pairs), so with r = 2 rows a band matches with probability about \(J^2\), and even 64 bands rarely fire. A Bloom filter stores bits, not elements: it answers "is x in the set?" with false positives and no false negatives, at rate \((1-e^{-kn/m})^k\). It cannot list the elements of a set, so it cannot intersect two of them; use it for "already friends", "already shown" or "blocked" checks. Back to Day 3.
  9. A mutual friend w with \(\deg(w)\) friends is shared by about \(\deg(w)^2/2\) pairs of people, so being one of them says little about any one pair. Adamic–Adar weights w by \(1/\log\deg(w)\); resource allocation by \(1/\deg(w)\). In this graph a triadic-closure step from u picks a friend f with probability \(1/d_u\) and then v with probability \(1/d_f\), so it lands on v with probability \(\sum_{f}1/(d_u d_f)\) over shared friends f: resource allocation divided by \(d_u\), which is why it is the best single feature (AUC 0.726 against 0.590 for common neighbours). For the logistic loss \(L=-[y\log\sigma(z)+(1-y)\log(1-\sigma(z))]\) with \(z=w^\top x+b\), the derivative \(\sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))\) gives \(\partial L/\partial z=\sigma(z)-y\), so the gradient is \((\sigma(z)-y)\,x\). The loss is convex, so gradient descent reaches the global minimum. Back to Day 4.
  10. A random split puts friendships formed after the one being predicted into the feature graph. They create the very mutual friends that "predict" it, so the offline AUC comes out too high. The app predicts the future from the past, so the test must too. Feedback loop: people connect mostly with whom the app shows them, so the next training set is full of pairs the old model chose; the new model learns to copy the old one and never sees the pairs it hid. Keep a little exploration, and log what was shown, not only what was accepted. Back to Day 4.
  11. A request is fast only if all n calls are fast: \(0.99^n\). So the share that waits on a slow one is \(1-0.99^n\): 0.096 for n = 10, 0.165 for n = 18 (about the mean fan-out at 256 shards), 0.634 for n = 100. The slow tail of one server becomes the typical experience of a request with large fan-out. Back to Day 5.
  12. With mod-N, key k stays put only if \(k \bmod N = k \bmod (N+1)\). Over a full cycle of \(N(N+1)\) keys that holds for exactly N keys, a share of \(1/(N+1)\), so \(N/(N+1)\) of keys move (0.941 measured at N = 16). On a consistent-hash ring, a new node takes over only the arcs just before its own points, and every other key keeps its owner. With enough virtual nodes the new node owns about \(1/(N+1)\) of the ring, so about that share of keys move, all of them to the new node (0.062 measured, against 0.059). Back to Day 5.
  13. Adding u–v changes exactly two friend lists: v joins u's and u joins v's. For a pair (w, x) where neither is u or v, both lists are unchanged, so their count is unchanged. For w = u, the shared set of u and x gains v exactly when x is a friend of v; for w = v, it gains u exactly when x is a friend of u. So the users whose suggestions change are u and v themselves (and they stop being candidates for each other), every friend of v (who gained a mutual friend with u), and every friend of u: u, v and the friends of each, nobody else. The filter still runs on every response because the cache holds a ranking computed earlier; since then someone may have blocked, hidden themselves or become a friend. Those rules are absolute, so they are checked at read time, at a cost of k plus the skipped entries. Approximate candidates may miss good people; the filter guarantees that nobody ineligible is ever shown. Back to Day 1 (the rules) and Day 5 (caching and updates).
