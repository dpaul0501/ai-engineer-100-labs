DAY 2 · GREEDY AND THE OBJECTIVEMLDSASys

When the best next step is the best plan, and when it isn't

BPE training has an objective: with a budget of k merges, make the training corpus as short as possible. It chases that objective greedily, one most-frequent pair at a time. Today asks two questions about this. Is the objective the right one for a model that has to learn from the tokens (ML)? And does the greedy method actually reach the best plan (Algorithms)? Answering the second means learning greedy algorithms as a subject: how to prove one correct, the theory of when greedy always works, and what to do when it doesn't.

ML · length vs learnability

The question

A model gives every token a vector (its embedding) and learns that vector from the places where the token appears. If a token appears 5 times in the training data and another appears 5,000 times, how much worse is the first one's vector? The answer decides how large a vocabulary a corpus can support.

First attempt: let the objective choose

The tokenizer's own objective cannot choose the vocabulary size. Each merge replaces at least two adjacent tokens with one, so on the training corpus every extra merge makes the text shorter, or at worst no longer. "Fewest tokens" always says "more merges". Something else has to push back, and that something is learnability.

The key idea, derived: a vector learned from n occurrences has noise ∝ 1/√n

Which gradients reach a token's row. The embedding table E has one row per token. Looking up token t is the same as multiplying a one-hot vector by E, so the gradient of the loss with respect to E is zero on every row except the rows of tokens that were looked up. Row et therefore receives exactly one gradient term per occurrence of t: if t appears n times, its row is shaped by n terms and by nothing else.

A one-dimensional toy. Suppose each occurrence i pulls the vector towards a target \(y_i = \mu + \varepsilon_i\): \(\mu\) is the "true meaning" all contexts agree on, and \(\varepsilon_i\) is what is particular to that one context, with mean 0 and variance \(\sigma^2\), independent across occurrences. With the squared loss, the best vector is the one that minimizes

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} \tfrac12 (e - y_i)^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{d}{de} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (e - y_i) = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \hat e = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^{n} y_i . \]

The learned vector is the average of the n targets. Its error is the average of the noise terms, and the variance of an average of n independent terms is

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\hat e - \mu) = \operatorname{Var}\!\Big(\frac1n \sum_i \varepsilon_i\Big) = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_i \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}, \qquad \mathrm{SE}(\hat e) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}. \]

The general case. A real loss is not quadratic, but near the ideal vector \(e^{*}\) it is close to one. Write each occurrence's gradient as \(g_i(e) \approx H(e - e^{*}) + \xi_i\), where H is the curvature of the expected loss and \(\xi_i\) is that occurrence's zero-mean noise with covariance \(\Sigma\). Training drives the sum of gradients to zero:

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(H(\hat e - e^{*}) + \xi_i\big) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \hat e - e^{*} = -H^{-1}\,\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i \;\Rightarrow\; \operatorname{Cov}(\hat e) = \frac{H^{-1}\Sigma H^{-1}}{n}. \]

It is the same algebra with matrices: the error is a fixed matrix times an average of n noise terms, so every coordinate's standard deviation falls like \(1/\sqrt n\). The assumption doing the work is that occurrences are roughly independent. Repeated near-identical contexts count for less than their number.

\[ \frac{\mathrm{SE}(n=100)}{\mathrm{SE}(n=10{,}000)} = \sqrt{\frac{10{,}000}{100}} = 10 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{halving the noise costs } 4\times \text{ the occurrences.} \]

Vocabulary size as model selection

Model selection means choosing a setting that controls a model's capacity, such as polynomial degree, tree depth or here the vocabulary size V, by its performance on data the model did not train on. It is needed whenever the training fit improves with capacity no matter what, which is exactly the situation above.

The bias–variance view makes the trade-off concrete. A small V has high bias: its units are generic (bytes, short pieces), sequences are long, a fixed context window covers less text, and the model must rebuild every word from parts. A large V has high variance: it adds V·d parameters and splits a fixed amount of text among more entries, so each is seen fewer times. If the corpus has N tokens and token frequencies follow Zipf's law (Day 1), the token of rank r has probability \(1/(r H_V)\) with \(H_V = \sum_{r\le V} 1/r \approx \ln V + 0.58\), so the rarest token in the vocabulary is seen about

\[ n_V \approx \frac{N}{V\,H_V} \quad\text{times, with noise} \;\propto\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{n_V}} = \sqrt{\frac{V H_V}{N}} . \]

Bias falls as V grows; variance rises. Held-out quality is the sum of both effects, so it improves, flattens and eventually gets worse. The best V sits near the knee, and the knee moves right as N grows. As a rough rule, if every token must be seen at least τ times, then \(V H_V \le N/\tau\). This is an estimate: BPE tokens are only roughly Zipfian.

The curve has the predicted shape. The first 744 new tokens buy 2.9× compression; the next 2,208 buy only 27% more, while most of them are seen fewer than 10 times and the train–test gap grows. The trade-off has a name: generalization (handle any input with pieces seen many times) versus identity (give each meaningful unit its own token). Too much identity gives rare, under-trained tokens; too much generalization gives long sequences. The vocabulary size sets the dial; the training corpus decides whose words get identity.

Leakage: split before training the tokenizer

Training a tokenizer is fitting a model to data: the merges are its parameters. So the held-out text must be set aside before the tokenizer is trained. If it is not, the held-out documents' own words become tokens and the held-out score measures memorization. Measured on Alice: a tokenizer trained on all documents, test set included, scores 3.66 bytes per token on the test set at V = 2,000, against 3.45 for the clean one, 6.0% better for no real reason. At V = 1,000 the inflation is only 1.5% (2.98 vs 2.94). It grows with V because the later merges are the rare, document-specific ones, the same merges the variance argument warns about.

Cost. One sweep needs only one training run. The trainer is deterministic and adds merges one at a time, so the first V − 256 merges of a long run are exactly the merges a shorter run would learn. Train once at the largest size and slice the merge list.

Where BPE uses it. This is why the vocabulary size is a hyperparameter you pick on held-out text, and why BPE's training objective is only a proxy. Fewer tokens is good only up to the point where the tokens stop being learnable.

Algorithms · greedy, in full

1 · The question: BPE's objective, and its greedy rule

Given a corpus and a budget of k merges, choose the merges so that the corpus has as few tokens as possible. BPE answers with one rule, repeated k times: merge the most frequent adjacent pair.

The gain identity. Replacing a non-overlapping occurrence of a b with a new token z turns two tokens into one and changes nothing else. So a merge that makes r replacements shortens the corpus by exactly r. When no pair overlaps itself, r is the pair's count, and merging the most frequent pair is the exact best single step.

The overlap caveat. In aaaaa the pair aa is counted 4 times, but merging scans left to right and fits only 2 replacements (aa aa a). With aaaaa ×1 and bc ×3, BPE's count picks aa (count 4, saves 2) over bc (count 3, saves 3). Runs of one repeated symbol are the only case where count and gain differ.

Is "best single step, repeated" the best plan? To answer, we need to know when that kind of reasoning is ever correct.

2 · What a greedy algorithm is

A greedy algorithm builds a solution by a sequence of choices. Each choice is the best one by a fixed local rule, made using only what has been decided so far, and it is never undone. The rule does not look ahead at how the choice will affect later choices.

A tiny example: pay 63 cents with the fewest coins, using US coins 1, 5, 10, 25. Local rule: take the largest coin that still fits.

6325largest coin ≤ 63 · 38 left
382525largest coin ≤ 38 · 13 left
13252510largest coin ≤ 13 · 3 left
32525101116 coins. Each choice is final.
greedy.py · change-making by the largest coin
def greedy_change(coins, amount):
    used = []
    for c in sorted(coins, reverse=True):      # local rule: biggest coin that fits
        while amount >= c:
            used.append(c); amount -= c         # never undone
    return used

Greedy algorithms are attractive because they are simple and fast: one pass, often after a sort, so O(n log n) or better. The catch is that a locally best choice can rule out a better plan. The same code with coins {1, 3, 4} pays 6 as 4 + 1 + 1, three coins, when 3 + 3 uses two. A greedy algorithm is correct only when we can prove it is, and the proof always has the same two ingredients.

3 · The two properties, and why you need both

Think of a problem as a family of instances, where making one choice turns an instance into a smaller instance of the same family (pay 63 → pay 38).

  • Greedy-choice property. For every instance, some optimal solution contains the greedy first choice c. It need not be the only optimal solution; there just has to be one that agrees with greedy's first move.
  • Optimal substructure. After choosing c, what remains is an instance P′ of the same problem, and (i) if S′ is any feasible solution of P′, then c ∪ S′ is feasible for P with value \(w(c) + w(S')\); (ii) if O is a feasible solution of P that contains c, then O − c is feasible for P′.

Theorem. If both properties hold for every instance, the greedy algorithm returns an optimal solution.

Proof, by induction on the instance size. An empty instance has only the empty solution, so greedy is optimal there. Now take an instance P and assume greedy is optimal on every smaller instance. Greedy picks c and then solves P′, returning G′; by the induction hypothesis G′ is optimal for P′. By the greedy-choice property there is an optimal solution O of P that contains c. By (ii), O − c is feasible for P′, so \(w(G') \ge w(O - c)\). By (i), greedy's answer c ∪ G′ is feasible for P and

\[ w(c \cup G') = w(c) + w(G') \;\ge\; w(c) + w(O - c) = w(O). \]

So greedy's answer is at least as good as an optimal one, hence optimal. ∎ (For a minimization problem, flip the inequalities.)

Why both are needed. Optimal substructure alone is common: 0/1 knapsack and coin change with {1, 3, 4} both have it, which is why dynamic programming solves them (§9). But without the greedy-choice property, the first step of the induction has no optimal O to compare with, and greedy can commit to a first choice that no optimal solution makes. The greedy-choice property alone is not enough either: knowing the first move is safe says nothing about the rest unless the rest is a smaller copy of the same problem that greedy also solves. In practice, the greedy-choice property is the hard part to prove. The next two sections show the two standard ways.

4 · Proof technique 1: the exchange argument, on interval scheduling

The question. Given n intervals [s, f), choose as many as possible so that no two overlap. (Talks to schedule in one room; touching at an endpoint is allowed.)

First attempts, and why they fail. Two natural local rules are wrong, and each has a three-interval counterexample:

RuleCounterexampleRule picksBest
Shortest first[1,5) [4,7) [6,10)[4,7), which blocks both others: 1[1,5) [6,10): 2
Earliest start first[1,10) [2,3) [4,5)[1,10): 1[2,3) [4,5): 2

The key idea. Choose the interval that finishes first, discard everything that overlaps it, and repeat. Finishing first leaves the most room for everything after it. By hand on [1,5) [4,7) [6,10): sorted by finish the order is [1,5), [4,7), [6,10). Take [1,5). [4,7) starts at 4 < 5, so skip it. [6,10) starts at 6 ≥ 5, so take it. Answer: 2.

Why it is correct: the exchange argument. An exchange argument takes an arbitrary optimal solution and swaps one of its parts for greedy's choice, showing the result is still feasible and no worse. Then an optimal solution containing greedy's choice exists, which is the greedy-choice property.

  • Greedy-choice property. Let g be the interval with the earliest finish. Let O be any optimal solution and o its interval with the earliest finish. Because g finishes earliest of all, \(f(g) \le f(o)\). Every other interval in O is disjoint from o and finishes after it, so it starts at or after \(f(o) \ge f(g)\). So O′ = O − o + g has no overlaps and the same size as O: it is an optimal solution that contains g.
  • Optimal substructure. After taking g, the remaining instance is the set of intervals that start at or after \(f(g)\). Any disjoint set of those, plus g, is disjoint; and any optimal solution containing g, minus g, is a disjoint set of those.

The theorem in §3 now finishes the proof. Notice why the swap was harmless: the rest of O lives entirely to the right of o, and g ends no later than o, so swapping cannot disturb anything else in O. Keep this in mind for BPE.

Cost. O(n log n) time for the sort by finish time, then O(n) for one scan that keeps only the finish time of the last chosen interval. O(1) extra space beyond the sort (O(n) if the sort copies).

Two LeetCode problems that are this problem. 435 Non-overlapping Intervals asks for the fewest intervals to remove so the rest are disjoint: that is n minus the maximum disjoint set. 452 Minimum Number of Arrows to Burst Balloons asks for the fewest points that hit every closed interval [s, f]. Its answer equals the maximum number of pairwise disjoint balloons, by a two-sided argument. Lower bound: one arrow cannot hit two disjoint balloons, so any answer needs at least as many arrows as there are disjoint balloons. Upper bound: run earliest-finish-first and shoot an arrow at the right end of each balloon it chooses. A balloon that was skipped starts no later than the last arrow x and, because balloons are sorted by finish, ends no earlier than x, so that arrow hits it. Greedy's arrows match the lower bound exactly.

intervals.py · earliest finish first, 435, 452
def max_disjoint(intervals):                    # half-open [s, f): touching is fine
    chosen, end = [], float("-inf")
    for s, f in sorted(intervals, key=lambda iv: iv[1]):   # by finish time
        if s >= end:                            # compatible with everything chosen
            chosen.append((s, f)); end = f
    return chosen

def eraseOverlapIntervals(intervals):           # 435: remove fewest to make disjoint
    return len(intervals) - len(max_disjoint(intervals))

def findMinArrowShots(points):                  # 452: closed balloons [s, f]
    arrows, x = 0, float("-inf")
    for s, f in sorted(points, key=lambda p: p[1]):
        if s > x:                               # not burst by the last arrow
            arrows += 1; x = f                  # shoot at its right end
    return arrows

5 · Proof technique 2: greedy stays ahead

A greedy-stays-ahead proof compares greedy with an optimal solution step by step and shows, by induction, that after each step greedy is at least as far along by some measure. Then it shows that being ahead at every step means greedy cannot finish with a worse answer.

Interval scheduling again. Let greedy's intervals be \(g_1, \dots, g_k\) and an optimal solution's be \(o_1, \dots, o_m\), both in order of finish time. Claim: \(f(g_j) \le f(o_j)\) for every \(j \le \min(k, m)\). For \(j = 1\) this holds because \(g_1\) finishes earliest of all. If it holds for \(j - 1\), then \(o_j\) starts at or after \(f(o_{j-1}) \ge f(g_{j-1})\), so \(o_j\) was still available when greedy chose \(g_j\); greedy chose the earliest-finishing available interval, so \(f(g_j) \le f(o_j)\). Conclusion: suppose \(m > k\). Then \(o_{k+1}\) starts after \(f(o_k) \ge f(g_k)\), so it was still available after greedy's last choice, and greedy would not have stopped. So \(k \ge m\). ∎

Jump game. 55 Jump Game: from index i you may jump forward any distance from 1 to nums[i]; can you reach the last index? 45 Jump Game II: with the fewest jumps? A first attempt, "always jump as far as possible", fails: in [2, 3, 1, 1, 4] the longest first jump lands on index 2 (value 1), giving three jumps, while jumping to index 1 (value 3) reaches the end in two.

The key idea is to be greedy about reach, not about the landing spot. Let \(R_k\) be the farthest index reachable with at most k jumps. Everything up to \(R_k\) is also reachable (you may always jump shorter), so the reachable set is the prefix \([0, R_k]\), and

\[ R_0 = 0, \qquad R_{k+1} = \max_{0 \le i \le R_k} \big(i + \texttt{nums}[i]\big). \]

Stays ahead. Any sequence of jumps is at position \(p_k \le R_k\) after k jumps. Induction: \(p_0 = 0 = R_0\), and \(p_{k+1} \le p_k + \texttt{nums}[p_k] \le R_{k+1}\) because \(p_k \le R_k\) is one of the indices in the max. So no strategy reaches the end in fewer jumps than the smallest k with \(R_k \ge n - 1\), and greedy achieves exactly that k. On [2, 3, 1, 1, 4]: \(R_0 = 0\), \(R_1 = 2\), \(R_2 = \max(0{+}2, 1{+}3, 2{+}1) = 4\), so 2 jumps. For 55 the same quantity with no jump count is enough: scan left to right, keep the farthest reach, and fail if an index lies beyond it.

Cost. One left-to-right pass: O(n) time, O(1) space. The layers \(R_0, R_1, \dots\) are breadth-first search levels computed without a queue.

jumps.py · 55 and 45, greedy stays ahead
def canJump(nums):                              # 55
    reach = 0
    for i, n in enumerate(nums):
        if i > reach:
            return False                        # i is past everything reachable
        reach = max(reach, i + n)
    return True

def jump(nums):                                 # 45: fewest jumps to the last index
    jumps, end, far = 0, 0, 0                   # end: reach with `jumps` jumps
    for i in range(len(nums) - 1):
        far = max(far, i + nums[i])             # reach with jumps + 1
        if i == end:                            # layer finished: must jump
            jumps += 1; end = far
    return jumps

6 · Fractional knapsack works; 0/1 knapsack does not

The question. A bag holds weight W. Item i has weight \(w_i\) and value \(v_i\). Maximize the value carried. In the fractional version you may take any fraction \(x_i \in [0, 1]\) of an item (gold dust); in the 0/1 version each \(x_i\) is 0 or 1 (gold bars).

Greedy rule. Sort by value per unit weight \(r_i = v_i / w_i\), highest first, and take as much of each item as fits.

Exchange proof for the fractional version. Number the items so \(r_1 \ge r_2 \ge \dots\). Greedy's solution is \(x^G = (1, \dots, 1, \text{partial}, 0, \dots, 0)\). Let \(x^{*}\) be an optimal solution that agrees with \(x^G\) on as many leading items as possible, and suppose they differ; let i be the first item where they do. Greedy took as much of item i as fit after the earlier items, so \(x^{*}_i < x^G_i\). The optimum fills the bag at least as much as greedy does (if there were spare room it could add more of item i), so some later item \(j > i\) has \(x^{*}_j > 0\). Move a small amount of weight \(\varepsilon > 0\) from item j to item i:

\[ \Delta\text{value} = \varepsilon\, r_i - \varepsilon\, r_j = \varepsilon\,(r_i - r_j) \;\ge\; 0 . \]

The total weight is unchanged and the value does not fall, so the result is still optimal. Take \(\varepsilon\) as large as possible: either \(x^{*}_i\) reaches \(x^G_i\), or \(x^{*}_j\) drops to 0. Repeating the swap ends at an optimal solution that agrees with greedy on item i too, contradicting the choice of \(x^{*}\). So greedy's solution is optimal. Cost: O(n log n) for the sort, O(n) for the fill.

The 0/1 version breaks it. Bag W = 4, items A (weight 3, value 5, ratio 1.67), B (2, 3, 1.5), C (2, 3, 1.5). Greedy takes A, then neither B nor C fits: value 5. Taking B and C gives 6. (Fractionally, greedy takes A and half of B for 6.5.) The proof failed at one step: it moved an arbitrarily small \(\varepsilon\) of weight. With whole items, the only swaps are lumps (A out, B and C in), and a lump of a high-ratio item can waste capacity that lower-ratio items would have filled. 0/1 knapsack still has optimal substructure, so dynamic programming solves it exactly in O(nW) time.

knapsack.py · fractional (greedy) vs 0/1 (DP)
def fractional_knapsack(items, cap):            # items: (weight, value)
    total = 0.0
    for w, v in sorted(items, key=lambda it: it[1] / it[0], reverse=True):
        take = min(w, cap)                      # all of it, or what still fits
        total += v * take / w; cap -= take
        if cap == 0:
            break
    return total

def knapsack01(items, cap):                     # exact: best[c] over items seen so far
    best = [0] * (cap + 1)
    for w, v in items:
        for c in range(cap, w - 1, -1):         # downwards: each item used once
            best[c] = max(best[c], best[c - w] + v)
    return best[cap]

7 · Matroids: the structures where greedy always works

The examples so far each needed their own proof. Is there a structural reason? For one large class of problems, yes, and it is exact.

Definitions. Take a finite set E of elements, each with a weight \(w(x) \ge 0\), and a family \(\mathcal I\) of subsets called independent sets (the feasible choices). The problem: find an independent set of maximum total weight. The greedy algorithm for it: sort elements by weight, heaviest first, and add each one if the set stays independent. The pair \((E, \mathcal I)\) is a matroid if

  • Hereditary: the empty set is independent, and every subset of an independent set is independent;
  • Exchange: if A and B are independent and \(|A| < |B|\), then some \(x \in B \setminus A\) can be added to A with \(A \cup \{x\}\) still independent.

Two examples. (1) Uniform matroid: any set of at most k elements is independent. Both properties are immediate, and greedy picks the k heaviest elements, which is plainly optimal. (2) Graphic matroid: E is the edges of a graph, and a set of edges is independent if it contains no cycle (it is a forest). Hereditary: removing edges cannot create a cycle. Exchange: a forest with e edges on n vertices has \(n - e\) connected pieces, so if \(|A| < |B|\), forest A has more pieces than B. If every edge of B stayed inside one piece of A, B would have at least as many pieces as A. So some edge of B joins two pieces of A, and adding it to A creates no cycle.

Theorem (Rado 1957, Edmonds 1971). Let \((E, \mathcal I)\) be hereditary. The greedy algorithm finds a maximum-weight independent set for every nonnegative weight function if and only if \((E, \mathcal I)\) is a matroid.

Proof that matroid ⇒ greedy is optimal (greedy stays ahead). Take positive weights for simplicity. Let greedy's set be \(g_1, \dots, g_k\) in the order chosen (so heaviest first) and let O = \(o_1, \dots, o_m\) be any independent set, heaviest first. First, \(k \ge m\): otherwise exchange gives \(x \in O\) with \(G \cup \{x\}\) independent; when greedy looked at x, its set then was a subset of G, so by heredity adding x was allowed and greedy would have taken it. Next, \(w(g_i) \ge w(o_i)\) for every \(i \le m\). Suppose not, and let i be the first failure: \(w(o_i) > w(g_i)\). Apply exchange to \(A = \{g_1, \dots, g_{i-1}\}\) and \(B = \{o_1, \dots, o_i\}\): some \(x \in B \setminus A\) has \(A \cup \{x\}\) independent, and \(w(x) \ge w(o_i) > w(g_i)\). So greedy looked at x before \(g_i\), at a moment when its set was a subset of A; by heredity x fit, so greedy took it, so \(x \in A\). That contradicts \(x \notin A\). Summing \(w(g_i) \ge w(o_i)\) over i gives \(w(G) \ge w(O)\). ∎

Proof that greedy always optimal ⇒ matroid. Suppose exchange fails for some independent A, B with \(|A| = p < |B|\). By heredity we may shrink B to \(p + 1\) elements and exchange still fails. Give elements of A weight \(p + 2\), elements of \(B \setminus A\) weight \(p + 1\), and everything else 0. Greedy takes all of A first (it is independent), then rejects every element of \(B \setminus A\), because exchange fails and adding elements to a superset of A cannot make it work. Greedy's weight is \(p(p+2) = p^2 + 2p\). But B weighs at least \((p+1)^2 = p^2 + 2p + 1\). So some weight function defeats greedy. ∎

Kruskal's algorithm is this theorem. A minimum spanning tree is a maximum-weight basis of the graphic matroid under weights \(C - w(e)\) for a large constant C, so "heaviest first" becomes "cheapest edge first". Kruskal sorts the edges by cost and adds an edge whenever it joins two different pieces, which is exactly "add it if the set stays a forest". A union-find structure answers "same piece?" in nearly constant time, so the cost is O(E log E) for the sort plus about O(E) for the scan.

kruskal.py · greedy on the graphic matroid
def kruskal(n, edges):                          # edges: (weight, u, v); returns MST weight
    parent = list(range(n))
    def find(x):
        while parent[x] != x:
            parent[x] = parent[parent[x]]       # path halving
            x = parent[x]
        return x
    total, used = 0, 0
    for w, u, v in sorted(edges):               # cheapest edge first
        ru, rv = find(u), find(v)
        if ru != rv:                            # still a forest: independent
            parent[ru] = rv; total += w; used += 1
    return total if used == n - 1 else None

What this says about BPE. The matroid theorem needs each element to have a fixed weight, independent of what else is chosen. A BPE merge has no fixed weight: its gain depends on which merges came before it and in which order. So BPE is not a matroid problem, and the theorem gives no guarantee. §8 shows the guarantee really is missing.

8 · When greedy fails

Coin change with {1, 3, 4}, amount 6. Greedy takes 4, then 1, then 1: three coins. Optimal is 3 + 3: two coins. The greedy-choice property fails: no optimal way to pay 6 uses a 4.

Canonical coin systems. A coin set is canonical if greedy is optimal for every amount. This is a property of the coin set, not of the algorithm, and it can be proved by exchange. For US coins {1, 5, 10, 25}, an optimal payment never contains 5 pennies (swap for a nickel), 2 nickels (swap for a dime), 3 dimes (swap for a quarter and a nickel) or 2 dimes and a nickel (swap for a quarter): each swap uses fewer coins. So the coins below 25 in an optimal payment add up to at most 2 dimes + 4 pennies = 24. An optimal payment of amount a therefore uses exactly \(\lfloor a/25 \rfloor\) quarters, which is greedy's choice. The same argument on the remainder fixes the dimes (the rest is at most 5 + 4 = 9), then the nickels. For {1, 3, 4} the argument has nothing to swap: two 3s cannot be replaced by a 4 plus fewer coins. Checked by computer for every amount up to 1,000: US coins never fail, {1, 3, 4} fails first at 6, and {1, 10, 25} fails first at 30 (greedy 25 + 5×1 is six coins; 10 + 10 + 10 is three). You do not have to check every amount: Kozen and Zaks (1994) proved that if a coin system is not canonical, its smallest counterexample is below the sum of its two largest coins, so a finite check decides it.

BPE fails too. Corpus nan ×2, nab ×2, anan ×3 (24 characters), budget 2 merges. Counts: an 8, na 7, ab 2.

Exactly where the exchange argument breaks. Try to prove the greedy-choice property the way §4 did: take the optimal plan O = (n+a, na+n), swap its first merge for greedy's a+n, and keep the rest.

swapn+aa+nthenna+nNot even a plan. na+n joins the token na, which no longer gets created. Later merges are built from earlier ones.
repaira+nthenn+a14 tokens. After a+n, na occurs only in nab: count 2, down from 7.
besta+nthenan+an13 tokens. The best second merge after a+n gains 3. Every plan that starts with a+n loses to 12.
No optimal plan starts with greedy's choice, so BPE has no greedy-choice property.

Two different interactions break the swap, and interval scheduling had neither:

  • Competition for characters. an and na overlap: in n a n you can merge the first two letters or the last two, not both. Merging an used up 5 of the 7 na occurrences (both nan and the middle of each anan). In interval scheduling, the rest of O lived to the right of the swapped interval and was untouched; in BPE, the rest of the plan lives on the same characters as the swapped merge.
  • Dependence. A later merge can use a token an earlier merge made (na+n needs na). Removing an early merge does not just lower a later merge's gain; it can delete the later merge.

Optimal substructure still holds: after any first merge, the best remaining plan is the best (k − 1)-merge plan for the rewritten corpus. It is the greedy-choice property that fails. So BPE is like 0/1 knapsack, not like interval scheduling: an exact method would have to look at more than one first move.

Confirm by measuring. How often does this happen? Over 2,000 random tiny corpora (three words of 3–5 letters from {a, b, c}, frequencies 1–3), greedy BPE was beaten by the best plan in 200 corpora (10.0%) with 2 merges and 208 (10.4%) with 3 merges. In the worst case found, greedy's total gain was 0.667 of the best plan's.

9 · When greedy fails, (a): exact answers by dynamic programming

Dynamic programming uses optimal substructure without the greedy-choice property: instead of trusting one first choice, it tries every first choice and looks up the best way to finish each one in a table of smaller answers. It works when the number of distinct subproblems, the state space, is small.

Coin change, 322. Let dp[a] be the fewest coins that pay a. The last coin of an optimal payment is some coin c, and the rest must be an optimal payment of a − c (if it were not, replacing it would beat the optimum). So

\[ dp[0] = 0, \qquad dp[a] = 1 + \min_{c \le a} dp[a - c]. \]

Correctness is induction on a: if every dp below a is right, the min tries every possible last coin, including the optimal one. By hand for {1, 3, 4}: dp[0..6] = 0, 1, 2, 1, 1, 2, 2; the last entry comes from dp[3] + 1 with coin 3. Cost: amount states × |coins| choices = O(amount · |coins|) time and O(amount) space. The state is one integer, which is why this is cheap.

Word break, 139, and the fewest-token split. Can string s be cut into dictionary words? Let ok[i] say whether the first i characters can be cut; ok[i] is true if some j < i has ok[j] and s[j:i] in the dictionary. Replace "is there a split" by "fewest pieces" and the same table gives the shortest segmentation of a chunk for a fixed vocabulary: dp[i] = 1 + min dp[j], with back-pointers to recover the pieces. With longest token length L, there are n + 1 states and at most L choices each: O(nL) substring lookups, O(n) space.

This answers a real question. BPE encodes by replaying its merges in training order, which is itself a greedy procedure. With a 2,000-token vocabulary trained on Alice, 97 of 3,218 chunks have a shorter split than replay gives. spoken encodes as sp|o|ken (3 tokens) though spoke|n (2) uses tokens in the same vocabulary. Replay is still the right choice for a model: it is the split the model saw during training.

dp.py · 322, 139, min_tokens
def coinChange(coins, amount):                  # 322: dp[a] = fewest coins for a
    dp = [0] + [amount + 1] * amount
    for a in range(1, amount + 1):
        for c in coins:
            if c <= a:
                dp[a] = min(dp[a], dp[a - c] + 1)
    return dp[amount] if dp[amount] <= amount else -1

def wordBreak(s, wordDict):                     # 139: ok[i] = s[:i] can be split
    words, L = set(wordDict), max(map(len, wordDict))
    ok = [True] + [False] * len(s)
    for i in range(1, len(s) + 1):
        ok[i] = any(ok[j] and s[j:i] in words for j in range(max(0, i - L), i))
    return ok[len(s)]

def min_tokens(s, vocab):                       # fewest tokens; vocab must cover s
    L, INF = max(map(len, vocab)), float("inf")
    dp, back = [0] + [INF] * len(s), [-1] * (len(s) + 1)
    for i in range(1, len(s) + 1):
        for j in range(max(0, i - L), i):
            if dp[j] + 1 < dp[i] and s[j:i] in vocab:
                dp[i], back[i] = dp[j] + 1, j
    out, i = [], len(s)
    while i:
        out.append(s[back[i]:i]); i = back[i]
    return out[::-1]

Why the merge-plan problem has no small DP. For coin change the state after a choice is one number. For BPE the state after j merges is the whole corpus as it is now segmented, because which merges are possible and how much each gains depends on every word's current tokens. Two plans that reach different segmentations cannot share table entries, and the number of segmentations is exponential in the corpus size. What is left is exhaustive search: try every pair at every step. With p candidate pairs per step, that is about \(p^k\) plans. On all of Alice there are 665 distinct byte pairs at step 0, so even 2 merges means about 442,000 plans and 10 merges about \(1.7 \times 10^{28}\). The brute force below is only for tiny corpora, which is the point: it is how you find and check counterexamples.

bpe_plans.py · greedy vs every plan
def apply_merge(words, pair, tok):              # words: Counter{tuple of symbols: freq}
    out = Counter()
    for w, f in words.items():
        out[tuple(merge(list(w), pair, tok))] += f
    return out

def length(words):
    return sum(len(w) * f for w, f in words.items())

def greedy_plan(words, k):
    ws, plan = Counter({tuple(w): f for w, f in words.items()}), []
    for step in range(k):
        counts = Counter()
        for w, f in ws.items():
            for p in zip(w, w[1:]):
                counts[p] += f
        if not counts:
            break
        p = min(counts, key=lambda q: (-counts[q], q))   # most frequent, ties: smallest
        ws = apply_merge(ws, p, f"<{step}>"); plan.append(p)
    return length(ws), plan

def best_plan(words, k):                        # exhaustive search over k-merge plans
    best = None
    def rec(ws, plan):
        nonlocal best
        pairs = sorted({p for w in ws for p in zip(w, w[1:])})
        if len(plan) == k or not pairs:
            if best is None or length(ws) < best[0]:
                best = (length(ws), plan)
            return
        for p in pairs:
            rec(apply_merge(ws, p, f"<{len(plan)}>"), plan + [p])
    rec(Counter({tuple(w): f for w, f in words.items()}), [])
    return best

apply_merge uses merge from the week reading (R.6); new tokens are named <0>, <1>, …, so best_plan returns (12, [('n', 'a'), ('<0>', 'n')]) and greedy_plan returns (13, [('a', 'n'), ('<0>', '<0>')]).

10 · When greedy fails, (b): approximation algorithms

When an exact answer is too expensive, the next best thing is a fast algorithm with a proven worst case. An algorithm has approximation ratio \(\rho\) if on every instance its answer is within a factor \(\rho\) of optimal: \(\mathrm{ALG} \ge \rho \cdot \mathrm{OPT}\) with \(\rho \le 1\) for a maximization problem, \(\mathrm{ALG} \le \rho \cdot \mathrm{OPT}\) with \(\rho \ge 1\) for a minimization problem.

Greedy set cover. Given a universe U of n elements and a list of subsets, choose the fewest subsets whose union is U. Greedy: repeatedly choose the subset that covers the most still-uncovered elements. It is not optimal: with U = {1, …, 6}, A = {1, 2, 3, 4}, B = {1, 3, 5}, C = {2, 4, 6}, greedy takes A (4 new), then B (1 new), then C (1 new), three sets, while B and C alone cover everything.

Theorem. Greedy uses at most \(H(n) \cdot \mathrm{OPT}\) sets, where \(H(n) = 1 + \tfrac12 + \dots + \tfrac1n \le \ln n + 1\).

Proof (charging). Each time greedy picks a set that covers t new elements, charge each of those elements 1/t. Each pick hands out a total charge of exactly 1, so the number of sets greedy picks equals the total charge. Now bound the charge per element. Suppose r elements are uncovered when greedy makes a pick. The optimal cover covers those r elements with OPT sets, so one of its sets covers at least r/OPT of them; greedy's pick covers at least that many, so \(t \ge r/\mathrm{OPT}\) and each newly covered element is charged at most OPT/r. List the elements in the order they get covered. When the j-th one is covered, at least \(n - j + 1\) elements were uncovered at the start of that pick, so its charge is at most \(\mathrm{OPT}/(n - j + 1)\). Adding up:

\[ \#\text{greedy sets} \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{n} \text{charge}_j \;\le\; \mathrm{OPT}\Big(\frac1n + \frac1{n-1} + \dots + \frac11\Big) = H(n)\cdot \mathrm{OPT}. \]

∎

This is close to the best possible: no polynomial-time algorithm achieves \((1 - \varepsilon)\ln n\) unless P = NP (Dinur and Steurer, 2014). Cost: with a straightforward implementation, each pick scans all subsets, so O(m · n · picks) for m subsets; bucket queues make it linear in the total size of the subsets.

set_cover.py · greedy set cover
def greedy_set_cover(universe, sets):
    left, picked = set(universe), []
    while left:
        best = max(sets, key=lambda name: len(sets[name] & left))
        if not sets[best] & left:
            return None                         # some element is in no set
        picked.append(best); left -= sets[best]
    return picked

BPE. Kozma and Voderholzer (2024) studied the problem "choose k merges to maximize the compression gain" and proved it APX-complete. APX is the class of problems with some constant-ratio polynomial-time approximation; APX-complete means that, unless P = NP, there is a ratio no polynomial algorithm can beat, so you cannot get arbitrarily close to optimal in polynomial time. They also bounded greedy BPE's worst-case ratio: its gain is always at least 0.333 of the optimal gain, and there are inputs where it gets no more than 0.625 of it. Zouhar et al. (2023) took another route, treating the gain of a merge sequence as a function with diminishing returns, and proved a guarantee that depends on a measured curvature quantity; on real data that guarantee comes out at about 0.37. These are worst-case floors. On our counterexample greedy got 11 of 12 (0.917), and the worst ratio in the random tiny corpora above was 0.667. On Zipf-shaped natural text the top pairs are far more frequent than the rest, so the early merges barely interfere with one another, and greedy does much better than its worst case.

11 · When greedy fails, (c): why BPE uses greedy anyway

  • It is simple and deterministic. One rule plus a tie-break (the smaller pair wins) fixes the result completely: same corpus, same merges, in every implementation. That is what lets Day 4 test a fast trainer against the simple one merge by merge.
  • It is fast. Each merge is one pass, and Day 4 makes it nearly linear in the corpus by counting once and updating locally. Exact search grows like \(p^k\).
  • It is good enough. The guarantee is weak in theory, but on natural text greedy's gain is close to the best plan's, and the ML block showed that the length objective is only a proxy anyway: past the knee, extra compression produces tokens the model cannot learn.
  • Encoding replays its order. The tokenizer is the ordered merge list. Encoding new text applies those merges in the same order, so the training procedure and the encoder are the same algorithm, and any text is split the same way it would have been split in training. An optimal plan found by search would be a list too, but greedy's list comes with this consistency for free.

cookies.py · 455
def findContentChildren(g, s):                  # g: greed, s: cookie sizes
    g, s = sorted(g), sorted(s)
    child = 0
    for size in s:                              # smallest cookie first
        if child < len(g) and size >= g[child]:
            child += 1                          # least greedy unfed child gets it
    return child

455 by exchange. Let g be the least greedy child and c the smallest cookie with \(c \ge g\). Cookies smaller than c satisfy nobody, since every child is at least as greedy as g. Take any optimal assignment. If g is unfed, give g the cookie c (if another child had it, that child loses it): the count does not drop. If g has some other cookie c′ (necessarily c′ ≥ c), give g the cookie c instead, and if a child h had c, give h the cookie c′: h was satisfied by c, so it is satisfied by the larger c′. Either way an optimal assignment pairs g with c, and the rest is the same problem on fewer children and cookies. The code walks cookies from smallest up and gives each one to the least greedy unfed child if it fits, which is exactly this rule. Cost: two sorts, O(n log n + m log m), then one pass.

Intervals are not a matroid. A = {[0, 10)} and B = {[0, 5), [5, 10)} are both disjoint sets and \(|A| < |B|\), but neither interval of B can be added to A. Greedy still works because the problem has its own exchange argument (§4); matroids are a sufficient structure for "greedy works for every weight function", and interval scheduling only needs it for counting.

Overlap. Count non-overlapping occurrences by scanning each word left to right and skipping one position after each match. On aaaaa ×1, bc ×3 this gives aa 2 and bc 3, so greedy by real gain merges bc and reaches 8 tokens, the optimum for one merge, where greedy by count stops at 9.

Coins. {1, 10, 25} fails at 30; the bound says any counterexample is below 25 + 10 = 35, so checking 1–34 decides it. Like nan, the big first choice (25) leaves a remainder (5) that the other coins cover badly, while a smaller first choice (10) leaves a remainder (20) they cover well.

Design · choosing the vocabulary budget

The question. You are building a 1 B-parameter model. How large should its vocabulary be?

First attempt. "As large as the data supports, since longer tokens mean shorter sequences." This ignores what the vocabulary costs inside the model, and a small model feels that cost most.

The cost, derived. The vocabulary is paid for twice. The input table holds V × d parameters, one row of width d per token. The output layer scores all V tokens at every position, which is a d × V matrix-vector product, about 2Vd operations per token. With tied embeddings the two share one table; untied, there are two.

Share of parameters spent on the vocabulary
GPT-2 small · 50,257 × 768, tied31%
Llama 3 8B · 128,256 × 4,096, ×2 untied13%
38.6 M of 124 M parameters for GPT-2 small; 1.05 B of 8 B for Llama 3 8B. Small models pay most for a big vocabulary.

So the budget is a design decision with three inputs: how much text you have (how many tokens it can support at τ occurrences each, from the ML block), the model's size (what share of parameters you can spend), and the languages and domains you must serve.

Where BPE uses it. The budget is the k in "choose k merges": V = 256 + k for byte-level BPE, plus any special tokens.
