DAY 2 · SCALE AND SKEWMLDSASystem Design

Why a few users cost most of the work

Where we are in the design. Day 1 built the exact baseline: for user u, walk to every friend, read every friend's friend list, count mutual friends, keep the top 10. That is the candidate generation box of the architecture (R.7). Today asks what that box costs, on average and in the worst case, once degrees are as uneven as they are in real social networks. The answer explains most of what the rest of the week builds: precomputation and caching (Day 3), sampling and hub capping (Day 4), and the serving budget (Day 5).

Three facts carry the day. Your friends have more friends than you do, by a margin you can compute from the degree distribution. The cost of counting mutual friends for everyone is \(\sum_v \deg(v)^2\), which a few hubs dominate. And a request that waits for many calls is slow far more often than any single call.

ML · heavy tails and the friendship paradox

1 · The question

How much work does one PYMK request do? The exact scan reads the friend list of each of u's friends, so the number of entries it reads is

\[ W(u) \;=\; \sum_{f \in N(u)} \deg(f). \]

Here N(u) is the set of u's friends and \(\deg(f) = |N(f)|\) is the number of friends f has (its degree). To size the servers we need the typical value of W and its worst values.

2 · First attempt: plug in the average

The week's graph has 10,000 users and 100,000 friendships, so the mean degree is 2 · 100,000 / 10,000 = 20. "A user has 20 friends, each with 20 friends, so a request reads 20 × 20 = 400 entries." Measured, the mean of W is 1,477, 3.7 times more. The estimate is wrong because it assumes that a friend is a typical user. A friend is not: you reach a user by following a friendship, and a user with 1,000 friendships is 1,000 times easier to reach that way than a user with one. The rest of this block makes that precise.

3 · The degree distribution, and why averages mislead

Definitions. The degree distribution is \(p_k\) = the fraction of users with exactly k friends. Its mean is \(E[d] = \sum_k k\,p_k\) and its second moment is \(E[d^2] = \sum_k k^2 p_k\). The variance is \(\operatorname{Var}(d) = E[d^2] - E[d]^2\). The complementary cumulative distribution (CCDF) is \(P(D \ge k)\), the fraction of users with at least k friends. A distribution is heavy-tailed when its CCDF falls much more slowly than an exponential, for example like a power \(P(D \ge k) \approx c\,k^{-\alpha}\). On log-log axes a power law is a straight line with slope −α.

Degrees span three orders of magnitude, so we bucket them by powers of two. For each bucket the chart shows two shares: of users, and of friend ends. Each friendship has two ends, one at each user, so user v owns \(\deg(v)\) of the 200,000 ends.

Four numbers show why the mean misleads. The mean is 20, the median 15, and 69% of users are below the mean. The standard deviation, 32.8, is larger than the mean. The 99th percentile is 100 and the maximum is 1,480, 74 times the mean. On the tail the CCDF is close to a straight line on log-log axes: \(P(D \ge 100) = 1.0\%\), \(P(D \ge 200) = 0.27\%\), \(P(D \ge 500) = 0.07\%\), and a least-squares line through \(\log P(D \ge k)\) against \(\log k\) for k from 20 to 480 has slope −1.90. Treat that as a rough description. Fitting power laws properly is harder than drawing a line (Clauset, Shalizi and Newman, below).

Why the tail matters more than the middle. With \(P(D \ge k) \approx c\,k^{-\alpha}\), the second moment is dominated by the largest degrees whenever \(\alpha \le 2\): the users with degree near k number about \(n\,c\,k^{-\alpha}\), each contributes \(k^2\), so the contribution \(n c k^{2-\alpha}\) does not shrink as k grows. Then \(E[d^2]\) is set by the few largest hubs, and it keeps growing as the network grows and its largest hub gets larger. Every cost on this page is a second moment.

4 · Where heavy tails come from: preferential attachment, derived

The question. Why do social graphs have hubs at all? If people made friends uniformly at random, would the maximum degree be 74 times the mean?

Model. Users arrive one at a time. User number t arrives at time t and makes m friendships with existing users. Two rules for choosing whom:

  • Uniform attachment: each existing user is equally likely.
  • Preferential attachment ("rich get richer"): existing user i is chosen with probability proportional to its current degree \(k_i\). This is the Barabási–Albert model.

Derivation for preferential attachment. At time t there are about mt friendships, so the degrees sum to 2mt. Each of the m new friendships lands on i with probability \(k_i / 2mt\), so i's degree grows on average at rate

\[ \frac{dk_i}{dt} = m \cdot \frac{k_i}{2mt} = \frac{k_i}{2t}. \]

Treat k as continuous (the mean-field approximation) and separate variables. User i arrived at time \(t_i\) with degree m:

\[ \int_m^{k_i} \frac{dk}{k} = \int_{t_i}^{t} \frac{ds}{2s} \;\Rightarrow\; \ln\frac{k_i}{m} = \frac12 \ln\frac{t}{t_i} \;\Rightarrow\; k_i(t) = m\sqrt{t / t_i}. \]

Early arrivals grow fastest. To get the distribution, note that arrival times are uniform on \([0, t]\), so \(P(t_i \le x) = x/t\). Then

\[ P(k_i \ge k) = P\!\Big(m\sqrt{t/t_i} \ge k\Big) = P\!\Big(t_i \le \frac{m^2 t}{k^2}\Big) = \frac{m^2}{k^2}, \qquad p_k = -\frac{d}{dk}\frac{m^2}{k^2} = \frac{2m^2}{k^3}. \]

A power law with CCDF exponent 2 (density exponent 3). The oldest user's degree grows like \(m\sqrt{t}\) without limit.

Uniform attachment, for contrast. Now each new friendship lands on i with probability 1/t, so \(dk_i/dt = m/t\) and \(k_i(t) = m\,(1 + \ln(t/t_i))\). Then \(P(k_i \ge k) = P\big(t_i \le t\,e^{-(k-m)/m}\big) = e^{-(k-m)/m}\): an exponential tail. Degrees well above the mean are exponentially rare, and the maximum grows only like \(m \ln t\).

And triadic closure is preferential attachment in disguise: picking a friend of a friend reaches user v through any of its \(\deg(v)\) friendships, so well-connected users are reached more often.

5 · The friendship paradox, proved

The question. Pick a friendship at random and look at one of its two ends. How many friends does that person have, on average? This is "the mean degree of a friend", and it is exactly what the two-hop scan pays for.

Definitions. Let n be the number of users and m the number of friendships, so \(\sum_v \deg(v) = 2m\) (each friendship adds 1 to two degrees). Choose one of the 2m friend ends uniformly. User v owns \(\deg(v)\) of them, so the end belongs to v with probability \(\deg(v)/2m\). This is size-biased sampling: each user is weighted by its degree.

Theorem (Feld, 1991). The mean degree of a friend is \(E[d^2]/E[d]\), and it is at least the mean degree \(E[d]\), with equality only when every user has the same degree.

Proof. Using the probabilities above,

\[ E[\deg \text{ of a friend}] = \sum_v \deg(v)\cdot\frac{\deg(v)}{2m} = \frac{\sum_v \deg(v)^2}{\sum_v \deg(v)} = \frac{\tfrac1n\sum_v \deg(v)^2}{\tfrac1n\sum_v \deg(v)} = \frac{E[d^2]}{E[d]}. \]

Substitute \(E[d^2] = E[d]^2 + \operatorname{Var}(d)\):

\[ \frac{E[d^2]}{E[d]} = E[d] + \frac{\operatorname{Var}(d)}{E[d]} \;\ge\; E[d], \]

because a variance is never negative. The gap is \(\operatorname{Var}(d)/E[d]\), which is 0 exactly when all degrees are equal. ∎

By hand on a star. One centre with 4 friends, each of whom has only the centre. Degrees 4, 1, 1, 1, 1: \(E[d] = 8/5 = 1.6\). The 8 friend ends are 4 at the centre (degree 4) and 4 at the leaves (degree 1), so the mean degree of a friend is \((4\cdot4 + 4\cdot1)/8 = 2.5\). Check: \(E[d^2] = (16+4)/5 = 4\), and \(4/1.6 = 2.5\).

The per-user version. A different question: for each user, average the degrees of their friends, then average over users. Is this also at least \(E[d]\)? Yes. Write the sum over users as a sum over friendships; friendship {u, v} contributes \(\deg(v)/\deg(u)\) to u's average and \(\deg(u)/\deg(v)\) to v's:

\[ \sum_u \frac{1}{\deg u}\sum_{f\in N(u)} \deg f \;=\; \sum_{\{u,v\}\in E}\Big(\frac{\deg v}{\deg u} + \frac{\deg u}{\deg v}\Big) \;\ge\; \sum_{\{u,v\}\in E} 2 \;=\; 2m, \]

using \(x/y + y/x \ge 2\) for positive x, y (it is \((x-y)^2 \ge 0\) divided by xy). Divide by n: the average over users is at least \(2m/n = E[d]\). (With isolated users, average over the others; the bound only gets stronger.) On the star it is \((1 + 4\cdot4)/5 = 3.4\).

Measured. On the week's graph \(E[d] = 20.00\), \(E[d^2] = 1{,}476.8\), \(\operatorname{Var}(d) = 1{,}076.8\), so

\[ \frac{E[d^2]}{E[d]} = 20 + \frac{1076.8}{20} = 73.84 . \]

The per-user version is 76.22. And 96.5% of users have fewer friends than their friends have on average; 73.8% are below the median degree of their friends, so the paradox is not only an effect of a few hubs pulling averages up.

6 · Expected work per request

Claim 1 (exact). Averaged over users, \(E_u[W(u)] = E[d^2]\).

Proof, by double counting. The total \(\sum_u W(u) = \sum_u \sum_{f\in N(u)} \deg(f)\) adds \(\deg(f)\) once for every pair (u, f) with f a friend of u. A fixed f appears in exactly \(\deg(f)\) such pairs, once per friend. So

\[ \sum_u W(u) = \sum_f \deg(f)\cdot\deg(f) = \sum_f \deg(f)^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad E_u[W] = \frac1n \sum_f \deg(f)^2 = E[d^2]. \;∎ \]

That is why the "20 × 20 = 400" estimate fails: it computes \(E[d]^2\) where the truth is \(E[d^2] = E[d]^2 + \operatorname{Var}(d)\). Measured: mean W = 1,476.8 = \(E[d^2]\) to the last digit.

Claim 2 (a model). If the degrees of u's friends behave like independent size-biased draws, unrelated to \(\deg(u)\), then

\[ E[W(u) \mid \deg u = d] = d \cdot \frac{E[d^2]}{E[d]} \approx 73.8\, d . \]

Proof. W is a sum of d terms, each with mean \(E[d^2]/E[d]\) by the theorem; the mean of a sum is the sum of the means. ∎ The assumption is that the graph has no degree correlation: whom you are friends with does not depend on how many friends you have. The random graph with a given degree sequence (the configuration model) has this property in the limit of large graphs. The week's graph almost does: the correlation between the degrees at the two ends of a friendship (the assortativity) is −0.037.

skew.py · the friendship paradox and the work per request
def friend_degree_means(adj):
    """Mean degree, and two ways to ask 'how many friends do my friends have?'."""
    deg = [len(a) for a in adj]
    n, two_m = len(adj), sum(deg)
    mean_d = two_m / n                                   # E[d]
    edge_end = sum(d * d for d in deg) / two_m           # E[d^2]/E[d]: end of a random friendship
    users = [u for u in range(n) if deg[u]]
    per_user = sum(sum(deg[f] for f in adj[u]) / deg[u] for u in users) / len(users)
    return mean_d, edge_end, per_user

def work(adj, u):
    """Adjacency entries the exact two-hop scan reads for user u: W(u) = sum of friends' degrees."""
    return sum(len(adj[f]) for f in adj[u])

Cost. Degrees cost O(n + m) to compute and O(n) to store. friend_degree_means touches each user once and each friend end once: O(n + m). work(adj, u) is O(\(\deg u\)) given stored degrees, which is why a server can predict a request's cost before running it.

7 · Where PYMK uses it

Every capacity estimate for candidate generation must use \(E[d^2]\), not \(E[d]^2\), and the worst requests come from hubs. Because W(u) is cheap to predict, the server can route or cap expensive requests before doing them (the Algorithms block and Day 4). The friendship paradox also shapes ranking: the mutual friend you share with a stranger is very likely a hub, because hubs are where friendships lead, and a hub is weak evidence that two of its 1,000 friends know each other (Adamic–Adar weighting, Day 5).

Solution
attach.py · preferential attachment in O(n·m)
def preferential_attachment(n, m, seed=0):
    """Each new user befriends m existing users, chosen with probability proportional to degree."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    adj = [set() for _ in range(n)]
    ends = []                                  # user v appears here deg(v) times
    for u in range(m + 1):                     # start from a small clique
        for v in range(u):
            adj[u].add(v); adj[v].add(u); ends += [u, v]
    for u in range(m + 1, n):
        while len(adj[u]) < m:
            v = rng.choice(ends)               # uniform over ends = proportional to degree
            if v not in adj[u]:
                adj[u].add(v); adj[v].add(u)
        ends += [x for v in adj[u] for x in (u, v)]
    return adj

The list ends holds one entry per friend end, so a uniform choice from it is a degree-proportional choice of user, in O(1). Uniform attachment replaces the choice with rng.randrange(u). The degree-preserving shuffle keeps \(\sum_v \deg(v)^2\), so \(E_u[W]\) is unchanged by Claim 1, but it destroys degree correlations, so the bucket table moves towards the model line. For the star with n users: \(E[d] = 2(n-1)/n \to 2\); \(E[d^2]/E[d] = ((n-1)^2 + (n-1)) / (2(n-1)) = n/2\); the per-user version is \(((n-1)\cdot(n-1) + 1)/n \approx n\). For the bound: \(E[d^2] \ge E[d]\) because \(d^2 \ge d\) for integer \(d \ge 1\), and \(E[d^2] \ge E[d]^2\) because the variance is non-negative.

Algorithms · complexity under skew

1 · The question: mutual counts for everyone at once

A common design precomputes every user's candidates in a nightly batch job (Day 3 compares this with online serving). The batch needs \(m(u, v) = |N(u) \cap N(v)|\) for every pair of non-friends u, v with at least one mutual friend. How much work is that, and who causes it?

2 · First attempt: every pair, one intersection each

Loop over all \(\binom{n}{2}\) pairs and intersect their friend lists. For 10,000 users that is 49,995,000 intersections, each costing at least \(O(\min(\deg u, \deg v))\) with hashing (Day 1). Most are wasted: only 11.9% of ordered pairs of users have a mutual friend. At a billion users, \(\binom{n}{2} \approx 5 \times 10^{17}\) pairs rules this out before any intersection is done. The work must be driven by pairs that actually share a friend.

3 · The key idea: count through the middle friend

Definitions. The adjacency matrix A has \(A_{uv} = 1\) if u and v are friends and 0 otherwise. A wedge (or path of length 2) is a triple u–w–v with u ≠ v, both friends of the middle w.

Lemma. \((A^2)_{uv} = m(u, v)\) for u ≠ v, and \((A^2)_{uu} = \deg(u)\).

Proof. By the definition of matrix product, \((A^2)_{uv} = \sum_w A_{uw}A_{wv}\). A term is 1 exactly when w is a friend of both, and 0 otherwise, so the sum counts common friends. For u = v it counts \(A_{uw}^2 = A_{uw}\), the friends of u. ∎

So "all mutual counts" is one sparse matrix product. The useful way to compute it turns the sum around: instead of asking each pair for its mutual friends, let each middle friend w announce itself to every pair of its friends. User w with friends {a, b, c, d} adds 1 to m(a,b), m(a,c), m(a,d), m(b,c), m(b,d), m(c,d): \(\binom{4}{2} = 6\) unordered pairs, 12 ordered ones. Only pairs that share a friend are ever touched.

allpairs.py · all mutual counts, one middle friend at a time
def all_pairs_mutual(adj):
    """m(u, v) for every non-friend pair u < v with a mutual friend, by looping over the middle friend w."""
    m = Counter()
    for w in range(len(adj)):
        nb = sorted(adj[w])
        for i, u in enumerate(nb):             # each unordered pair of w's friends, once
            for v in nb[i + 1:]:
                if v not in adj[u]:
                    m[u, v] += 1               # w is one mutual friend of u and v
    return m

4 · Cost: Σ deg², derived two ways

By the middle friend. User w generates one update per ordered pair of distinct friends: \(\deg(w)(\deg(w) - 1)\) of them. The total is \(\sum_w \deg(w)(\deg(w)-1) = \sum_w \deg(w)^2 - 2m\), and the code above does half of that by visiting unordered pairs.

By rows. Row u of \(A^2\) is the sum of the rows of u's friends, which is exactly Day 1's suggest(adj, u): it reads \(W(u) = \sum_{f\in N(u)} \deg(f)\) entries. Over all users, by the double-counting proof in the ML block, \(\sum_u W(u) = \sum_f \deg(f)^2\). (This row-by-row order is Gustavson's algorithm for sparse matrix products.)

Both orders do \(\Theta(\sum_v \deg(v)^2)\) work, plus the output. Since \(\sum_v \deg(v)^2 = n\,E[d^2] = n\,(E[d]^2 + \operatorname{Var}(d))\), two graphs with the same number of friendships can differ enormously in cost: the variance of the degrees decides. A graph where all 10,000 users have exactly 20 friends would cost 4,000,000; the week's graph costs 14,768,324, 3.7 times more. Space is the output: one counter per ordered candidate pair.

Confirm by measuring. On the week's graph \(\sum_v \deg(v)^2 = 14{,}768{,}324\), equal to \(\sum_u W(u)\) as proved. The output has 11,908,610 ordered candidate pairs holding 14,136,468 counted wedges, so each pair is touched 1.24 times on average. (Check: of the 14,568,324 ordered wedges, the other 431,856 = 6 × 71,976 close a triangle, so their endpoints are already friends.) Who causes the work:

Squaring turns a mild concentration of friendships into a severe concentration of work. At a billion users the same shape is far worse. Assumption, for a back-of-envelope: mean degree 200 and the same ratio \(E[d^2]/E[d]^2 = 3.69\) as the week's graph. Then \(E[d^2] \approx 147{,}700\) and \(\sum_v \deg(v)^2 \approx 1.5 \times 10^{14}\) wedge updates per full recompute, half of them, by the measured shape, from a tiny set of hubs. In a distributed job (MapReduce-style, keyed by the middle friend), each hub's pairs land on one worker, which then finishes long after the others. This is the "curse of the last reducer" (Suri and Vassilvitskii, below).

5 · Triangle counting and the degree-ordering trick

The question. Count the triangles: sets of three users who are all friends with each other. Triangles measure triadic closure (the global clustering coefficient is 3 × triangles / wedges; Day 1), and triangle counting is the standard example of an algorithm whose cost depends on skew. LeetCode 1761 is the same loop with a different aggregate.

First attempts. Checking every triple is \(\binom{n}{3} = 1.67 \times 10^{11}\) checks for 10,000 users. The node iterator is better: for each user v, check every pair of its friends for a friendship. That is \(\sum_v \binom{\deg v}{2}\) checks, 7,284,162 on the week's graph. It counts each triangle three times (once at each corner) and pays \(\binom{1480}{2} = 1{,}094{,}460\) checks at the top hub alone. The cost is again a sum of squared degrees.

The key idea. Count each triangle only at its "smallest" corner, where smallest means fewest friends. A hub is rarely the smallest corner of a triangle, so it does almost no work.

  • Order. Rank users by (degree, id): u ≺ v if \(\deg u < \deg v\), or the degrees are equal and u has the smaller id. This is a total order: any two distinct users are comparable, with no ties.
  • Orient. Point each friendship from its lower end to its higher end. Let \(N^+(v)\) be the out-neighbours of v (friends ranked above it), with size \(d^+(v)\), and \(d^-(v) = \deg v - d^+(v)\) the in-degree.
  • Count. For each oriented edge u → v, count the users w in \(N^+(v)\) that are also in \(N^+(u)\).

By hand. Six users: hub 0 is friends with 1, 2, 3, 4, 5; also 1–2 and 2–3. Two triangles: {0, 1, 2} and {0, 2, 3}. Degrees: 0 has 5, 2 has 3, 1 and 3 have 2, 4 and 5 have 1. The order is 4 ≺ 5 ≺ 1 ≺ 3 ≺ 2 ≺ 0, and every arrow points up the order:

Arrows point from lower to higher (degree, id). The hub receives five arrows and sends none, so it starts no work.

Edges out of 1: {2, 0}. Following 1 → 2, \(N^+(2) = \{0\}\), and 0 is in \(N^+(1)\): triangle {1, 2, 0}. Following 1 → 0, \(N^+(0)\) is empty. From 3 the same steps find {3, 2, 0}. Users 4, 5 and 2 point only at 0, which points nowhere. Total: 2 membership checks and 2 triangles, against 15 pair checks for the node iterator (10 of them at the hub).

Why each triangle is counted exactly once. Take a triangle and name its corners in order, a ≺ b ≺ c. Its edges are oriented a → b, a → c, b → c. When the loop handles the edge a → b, it finds c in \(N^+(b)\) and in \(N^+(a)\): counted. No other edge counts it: handling a → c would need b ∈ \(N^+(c)\), impossible since b ≺ c; handling b → c would need a ∈ \(N^+(c)\), impossible since a ≺ c. Conversely, anything counted at u → v with w is a triangle, since all three friendships exist. ∎

Lemma: no user has more than \(\sqrt{2m}\) out-neighbours. Let \(k = d^+(v)\). Every out-neighbour w of v ranks above v, so \(\deg w \ge \deg v \ge d^+(v) = k\). Those k users therefore have degrees adding up to at least \(k \cdot k\). All degrees add up to 2m, so \(k^2 \le 2m\) and \(k \le \sqrt{2m}\). ∎

Cost. With the out-neighbour sets stored as hash sets, handling edge u → v costs \(d^+(v)\) expected-O(1) lookups. Each user v is the head of \(d^-(v)\) edges, so the total is

\[ \sum_{u \to v} d^+(v) \;=\; \sum_v d^-(v)\, d^+(v) \;\le\; \sqrt{2m}\sum_v d^-(v) \;=\; \sqrt{2m}\cdot m \;=\; O(m^{1.5}), \]

plus O(n log n + m) to sort and orient. Space is O(n + m). The bound holds for every graph, however skewed; the node iterator's \(\sum_v \binom{\deg v}{2}\) can be as large as \(\Theta(n^2)\) for a star with only \(n - 1\) friendships. The result goes back to Chiba and Nishizeki (1985).

triangles.py · degree-ordered triangle counting
def count_triangles(adj):
    """Each triangle counted once, at its lowest vertex in (degree, id) order. O(m^1.5) work."""
    n = len(adj)
    pos = [0] * n
    for i, v in enumerate(sorted(range(n), key=lambda v: (len(adj[v]), v))):
        pos[v] = i
    out = [[w for w in adj[v] if pos[w] > pos[v]] for v in range(n)]   # orient low -> high
    outset = [set(o) for o in out]
    t = 0
    for u in range(n):
        for v in out[u]:                       # u before v
            t += sum(1 for w in out[v] if w in outset[u])   # v before w, and u-w is an edge
    return t

6 · Capping hubs

The question. Bound the work of one PYMK request, whatever the graph.

The idea. Skip any friend with more than C friends. Then \(W_C(u) = \sum_{f\in N(u),\,\deg f \le C} \deg f \le C \cdot \deg u\). The skipped lists are the most expensive (they are the hubs, which by the paradox are most of what a scan reads) and the least informative (a mutual friend with 1,000 friends says little about whether two of them know each other; Day 5 derives the Adamic–Adar weight \(1/\log \deg f\)). The price is that m(u, v) undercounts by the number of skipped hubs they share; Day 4 measures what that does to recall.

capped.py · two-hop scan that skips hubs
def suggest_capped(adj, u, k=10, cap=200):
    """The exact two-hop scan, except a friend with more than `cap` friends is skipped."""
    direct = adj[u]
    count = Counter()
    for f in direct:
        if len(adj[f]) > cap:
            continue                           # a hub: its list costs most and says least
        for v in adj[f]:
            if v != u and v not in direct:
                count[v] += 1
    return heapq.nsmallest(k, count, key=lambda v: (-count[v], v)), count

Solution
lc_997.py · Find the Town Judge, O(n + |trust|)
def findJudge(n, trust):
    score = [0] * (n + 1)
    for a, b in trust:
        score[a] -= 1                          # trusting someone disqualifies a
        score[b] += 1                          # being trusted counts for b
    for p in range(1, n + 1):
        if score[p] == n - 1:                  # possible only if in = n-1 and out = 0
            return p
    return -1

The judge has in-degree n − 1 and out-degree 0. Since in-degree ≤ n − 1 and out-degree ≥ 0, in − out = n − 1 happens only in that case, so one score per person suffices.

lc_1615.py · Maximal Network Rank, O(n² + m)
def maximalNetworkRank(n, roads):
    deg = [0] * n
    road = set()
    for a, b in roads:
        deg[a] += 1; deg[b] += 1
        road.add((min(a, b), max(a, b)))
    best = 0
    for a in range(n):
        for b in range(a + 1, n):
            best = max(best, deg[a] + deg[b] - ((a, b) in road))   # a shared road counts once
    return best

The rank of a pair is the number of roads touching either city: \(\deg a + \deg b\), minus 1 if the road a–b was counted twice.

lc_1761.py · Minimum Degree of a Connected Trio, O(m^1.5)
def minTrioDegree(n, edges):
    adj = [set() for _ in range(n + 1)]
    for a, b in edges:
        adj[a].add(b); adj[b].add(a)
    deg = [len(s) for s in adj]
    out = [[w for w in adj[v] if (deg[w], w) > (deg[v], v)] for v in range(n + 1)]
    best = math.inf
    for u in range(1, n + 1):
        for v in out[u]:
            for w in out[v]:
                if w in adj[u]:                # u, v, w is a trio, found once
                    best = min(best, deg[u] + deg[v] + deg[w] - 6)
    return -1 if best == math.inf else best

The same orientation as count_triangles, so each trio is found once and the loops do \(O(m^{1.5})\) work. A trio's degree is the number of edges leaving it: the three degrees minus the 6 ends of its own three edges. For the id-only bad case, take a star whose centre has id 0 plus a few edges among the leaves: every leaf edge forces a scan of the centre's out-list. For the hub-chunking variation: the top user has 1,480 · 1,479 = 2,188,920 ordered pairs, so 22 chunks of at most 100,000.

Design · estimating load and the tail

1 · Back-of-envelope estimation as a skill

The question. If every PYMK load ran the exact two-hop scan online, how much reading would the system do at a billion users?

First attempt. "A billion users, 200 friends each, so 200 reads per request." That is the number of friend lists read, but it ignores how long each list is, which the ML block showed is the expensive part, and it gives no rate.

The method. A back-of-envelope estimate has three steps. (1) Write every assumption down and label it, so a reader can change one and redo the arithmetic. (2) Compute with round numbers and powers of ten: a day is 86,400 s, about \(10^5\). (3) Sanity-check the result against something known, and say which assumption it is most sensitive to.

2 · Latency percentiles, defined

The question. How do you state "how fast" a service is, when requests differ in cost by a factor of 1,000?

Definition. For a sample of N latencies sorted as \(x_{(1)} \le \dots \le x_{(N)}\), the q-th percentile (nearest-rank) is \(x_{(\lceil qN/100 \rceil)}\): the smallest value with at least q% of the sample at or below it. p50 is the median (half the requests are faster), p95 and p99 bound all but the slowest 5% and 1%. A service-level objective is usually stated as a percentile: "p99 under 100 ms".

Why not the mean. The mean is one number for a whole distribution and is pulled by the tail it hides. On the week's graph the work per request has mean 1,477 but median 1,023, p95 3,870, p99 7,284 and max 51,806. A user who opens the app many times a day meets the p99 often: at 100 requests, the chance that none of them is in the slowest 1% is \(0.99^{100} = 37\%\). Day 3 shows how to estimate a p99 from samples and how uncertain it is.

tail.py · percentiles and fan-out
def percentile(xs, q):
    """Nearest-rank percentile: the smallest sample value with at least q% of the sample at or below it."""
    s = sorted(xs)
    return s[max(0, math.ceil(q / 100 * len(s)) - 1)]


def p_any_slow(p, n):
    """A request waits for n independent calls, each slow with probability p."""
    return 1 - (1 - p) ** n

3 · The tail at scale: fan-out

The question. A PYMK request reads n friend lists in parallel from storage and must wait for all of them. Each read is slow (say, it hits a garbage-collection pause or a busy disk) with probability p = 1%. How often is the request slow?

First attempt. "1%, since each read is slow 1% of the time." That is right only for n = 1. A request that waits for all of its calls is as slow as its slowest call.

Derivation. Assume the reads are slow independently. The request is fast only if all n reads are fast, so

\[ P(\text{request fast}) = (1-p)^n, \qquad P(\text{request slow}) = 1 - (1-p)^n . \]

Two approximations make it easy to use. For small np, \(1 - (1-p)^n \le np\) by the union bound (the probability that at least one of n events happens is at most the sum of their probabilities), and it is close to np. In general, \(\ln(1-p) \approx -p\) gives \(1 - (1-p)^n \approx 1 - e^{-np}\): once np is about 1, most requests are slow.

Turned around. For the request to be fast 99% of the time, each call must be fast with probability \(0.99^{1/n}\): at n = 100 that is 0.9999, so the request's p99 is set by each call's p99.99. A backend that looks healthy at its own p99 can make every user-facing request slow.

On the week's graph. A request fans out to \(\deg(u)\) friend lists: median 15, p99 100, max 1,480. At p = 1%, averaging \(1-(1-p)^{\deg u}\) over users gives 16.6% of requests slow: 14.0% for a median user and essentially 100% for the top hub. At p = 0.1%, 1.9%. A simulation with an assumed read latency (1 ms, or 50 ms with probability 1%, independently) and ten requests per user agrees: 16.8% of requests take 50 ms, so the p50 is 1 ms while the p95 and p99 are both 50 ms.

What to do about it. The derivation names the levers: make n smaller, make p smaller, or stop waiting for the last call.

  • Fewer calls. Batch reads by storage shard, so a request makes one call per shard rather than one per friend (Day 3 measures shards touched). Cap fan-out (hub capping, sampling on Day 4).
  • Hedged requests. If a read has not returned after, say, the p95 delay, send a second copy to another replica and take whichever answers first. If the copies are slow independently, a read is now slow with probability about \(p^2\): at p = 1% and n = 100, the request is slow 1.0% of the time instead of 63.4%. In the simulation (both copies sent at once, a simplification), the share of slow requests falls from 16.8% to 0.21%. The cost is the extra reads, roughly the share of calls that get a second copy.
  • Partial results. Give the fan-out a deadline and rank with the lists that arrived. Mutual counts are approximate anyway; the final eligibility check (blocks, privacy, already friends) is not, so it must never be skipped (Day 3).

4 · Hot keys

The question. Which friend lists does storage serve most, and how unevenly?

Derivation. If every user makes one request, list f is read once for each friend of f: \(\deg(f)\) reads. Each read returns \(\deg(f)\) ids, so f's list costs \(\deg(f)^2\) ids of bandwidth. Read counts follow the degrees; bytes follow the squared degrees. Measured: reads per list have median 15 and maximum 1,480. The top 1% of lists receive 11.1% of the reads but 69.1% of the bytes. The hottest list alone serves 1,480 × 1,480 × 8 B = 17.5 MB per round of requests, against 1.8 KB for a median list. A key that receives far more traffic than average is a hot key; the shard that holds it saturates while the others idle, and adding shards does not help, because the key lives on one shard.

Mitigations. Replicate hot lists on several shards and spread reads across the copies; cache them close to the servers that compute candidates (Day 3's cache); or skip them (hub capping). All three need the hot set to be known, and the degree tells you which it is before any traffic arrives.

5 · Little's law

The question. At 34,722 peak QPS and 50 ms per request, how many requests are in progress at once? That number sizes thread pools, connection pools and memory.

Statement. In any stable system, the average number of items inside, L, equals the arrival rate, λ, times the average time each item spends inside, W: \(L = \lambda W\). It needs no assumption about arrival patterns or service times.

Why it is true (the area argument). Watch the system for a long time T and let N(t) be the number of items inside at time t. Compute the area under N(t) two ways. Over time, the area is \(\int_0^T N(t)\,dt = L \cdot T\), by the definition of the time-average L. Over items, each item adds 1 to N for exactly as long as it stays, so the area is the sum of the stay times, \(\sum_i W_i = (\lambda T)\cdot W\), with \(\lambda T\) items arriving. Equate the two and divide by T: \(L = \lambda W\). (Items still inside at the edges of the window make a bounded error that vanishes as T grows.) ∎

Confirm by measuring. A simulated single-server queue with random (Poisson) arrivals at rate 0.8 and random (exponential) service at rate 1, over 200,000 arrivals: measured \(\lambda = 0.8004\), mean time inside \(W = 4.938\), \(\lambda W = 3.952\), and the time-average number inside, measured directly from N(t), \(L = 3.952\). (Queueing theory predicts W = 5 and L = 4 for this queue. The sample's L and W are both a little below that by chance, but they still satisfy \(L = \lambda W\): the law holds for each sample path, up to the edge effects in the proof.)

Worked example. 34,722 requests/s × 0.050 s = 1,736 requests in flight at peak. If each issues its 200 friend-list reads in parallel, storage sees 1,736 × 200 ≈ 347,000 outstanding reads. If one server can hold 100 requests in flight, the fleet needs at least 18 servers before any headroom, and a slower p99 directly raises W and therefore the concurrency the fleet must absorb.

Where PYMK uses it

The estimate says the exact scan cannot run online for every load at a billion users without a very large read budget; that is why the design precomputes (Day 3), caches hot lists (Day 3), and caps and samples (Day 4). Percentiles are how the latency budget is stated, fan-out is why the tail dominates it, and Little's law turns the budget into a fleet size.
