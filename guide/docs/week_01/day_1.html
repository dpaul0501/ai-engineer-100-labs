The exact baseline: count shared friends, and know why it is right

Where we are in the design. Today builds the first box of the map in R.7, candidate generation and scoring, exact version: for a user u, find everyone two hops away, count the friends each shares with u, drop the ineligible, and return the best 10. Nothing later in the week replaces this box without being measured against it. Day 4 approximates it and reports recall against it, Day 3 shards it and must return the same answer, and Day 5 adds a learned ranker on top of the candidates it produces. So it has to be correct, its cost has to be understood term by term, and it needs tests that say so.

The ML block asks whether friends of friends are a good bet at all, and measures it on the week's graph. The Algorithms block teaches the five subjects the exact algorithm is made of: how to store a graph, how to traverse it, how to count with a hash map, how to intersect two sets, and how to take the top k. The Delivery block writes the contract and the tests.

ML · triadic closure

1 · Do friendships close triangles? The clustering coefficient

The question. If B is your friend and also E's friend, are you and E more likely to be friends than two people picked at random? If yes, the graph is full of triangles, and "a friend of a friend" is a good first guess for a future friend. That guess is the whole premise of PYMK, so we measure it before building on it.

First attempt: count triangles. A triangle is three users who are all friends with each other. The week's graph has 71,976 of them. Is that a lot? The count alone cannot say. Add more friendships and the count grows by chance alone. A number means something only next to the number of chances to form a triangle, and next to what a graph without any tendency to close triangles would give.

The key idea, derived. Write \(N(v)\) for the set of v's friends and \(d_v = |N(v)|\) for v's degree.

  • A wedge (or connected triple) centred at v is a pair of distinct friends {x, y} of v: the path x – v – y. Choosing 2 of v's \(d_v\) friends gives \(\binom{d_v}{2} = d_v(d_v-1)/2\) wedges.
  • The wedge is closed if x and y are also friends. Let \(T_v\) be the number of closed wedges at v. A closed wedge at v is a triangle through v, so \(T_v\) is also the number of triangles that contain v.
  • The local clustering coefficient of a user with \(d_v \ge 2\) is the fraction of their wedges that are closed. Equivalently, it is the probability that two of v's friends, picked at random, are friends with each other:
    \[ C_v = \frac{T_v}{\binom{d_v}{2}} \in [0, 1]. \]
  • Two ways to summarise a whole graph. The average local clustering is the mean of \(C_v\) over users with \(d_v \ge 2\). The global clustering, or transitivity, pools all wedges first: \(\;\mathcal{T} = \sum_v T_v \big/ \sum_v \binom{d_v}{2}\).

Worked by hand. User 0 has four friends, 1 to 4, and only 1 and 2 know each other.

One triangle {0, 1, 2}. Wedges: 6 at 0, 1 at 1, 1 at 2, so 8 in total, 3 of them closed. Transitivity = 3/8 = 0.375. Average local = (1/6 + 1 + 1)/3 ≈ 0.72.

The two summaries disagree by a factor of two on five users. The average gives every user one vote. Transitivity gives every wedge one vote, and the hub, user 0, owns 6 of the 8 wedges. On a heavy-tailed graph like ours (Day 2), hubs own most wedges, so transitivity mostly describes hubs.

Why it is correct. Two facts, both used in the code.

(1) \(\sum_v T_v = 3 \times\) (number of triangles). Take a triangle {x, y, z}. It is a closed wedge at x (the pair {y, z}), at y and at z: three closed wedges. Conversely, a closed wedge at v with ends x, y belongs to exactly one triangle, {v, x, y}. So closed wedges and (triangle, corner) pairs are in one-to-one correspondence, and there are three corners per triangle. Hence \(\mathcal{T} = 3T / (\text{number of wedges})\).

(2) Transitivity is a weighted average of the local coefficients. Put \(w_v = \binom{d_v}{2}\). Then \(T_v = w_v C_v\), so

\[ \mathcal{T} = \frac{\sum_v T_v}{\sum_v w_v} = \frac{\sum_v w_v\, C_v}{\sum_v w_v}, \]

a weighted mean with weights growing like \(d_v^2\). That is the precise sense in which it "mostly describes hubs".

The baseline: a graph with no reason to close triangles. In the random graph \(G(n, p)\) every one of the \(\binom{n}{2}\) pairs is a friendship independently with probability p. Take any wedge x – v – y. Whether x–y is an edge is independent of the two edges we already know about, so it is closed with probability exactly p. Summing over v's wedges, \(E[T_v \mid d_v] = p \binom{d_v}{2}\), so \(E[C_v] = p\) for every degree. To compare fairly, match the density of our graph:

\[ p = \frac{|E|}{\binom{n}{2}} = \frac{2 \cdot 100{,}000}{10{,}000 \cdot 9{,}999} \approx 0.0020 . \]

\(G(n,p)\) also gets the degrees wrong: its degrees are concentrated near the mean, ours are heavy-tailed. A second baseline keeps every user's degree and wires the friendships at random: give each user \(d_v\) "stubs", shuffle all stubs, and pair them off (the configuration model). Hubs then share some friends by chance, so this baseline is the stricter one. We measure it rather than derive it.

Cost. For each user v and each friend x, \(|N(v) \cap N(x)|\) counts the triangles on edge v–x. With hash sets that costs \(\min(d_v, d_x)\) (part 4 of the Algorithms block), so the total is \(\sum_v \sum_{x \in N(v)} \min(d_v, d_x) = 2\sum_{\{v,x\} \in E} \min(d_v, d_x)\), and \(T_v\) counts each friend-friend edge twice, once from each end, hence the // 2. Day 2 analyses triangle counting properly. Space: \(O(n)\) for the results.

pymk/closure.py · clustering
def clustering(adj):
    """Local clustering C_v for every v with degree >= 2, and the global transitivity."""
    local, closed, wedges = {}, 0, 0
    for v, nv in enumerate(adj):
        d = len(nv)
        if d < 2:
            continue                                   # no pair of friends, C_v undefined
        t = sum(len(nv & adj[x]) for x in nv) // 2     # each friend-friend edge is seen from both ends
        local[v] = t / (d * (d - 1) / 2)
        closed += t                                    # closed wedges centred at v
        wedges += d * (d - 1) // 2                     # all wedges centred at v
    return local, closed / wedges

2 · Does a mutual friend predict a future friendship?

The question. Clustering is a snapshot. PYMK predicts the future: given the graph today, which non-friends will become friends? Put a number on it. For each count m of mutual friends, what is

\[ p_m = P\big(\text{a non-friend pair becomes friends later} \;\big|\; \text{it has } m \text{ mutual friends now}\big)\;? \]

First attempt: count future friendships by m. Use the temporal split of R.2: the first 80,000 friendships are the past graph, the last 20,000 are the future. Of the 20,000 future friendships, 9,355 had m = 0 in the past graph, 7,057 had m = 1, and only 2,042 had m = 2. Read naively, zero mutual friends is the best sign of all. That is wrong, because the number of pairs that could have formed a friendship differs by orders of magnitude between buckets: 45.9 million pairs have m = 0, only 343,961 have m = 2. A count of successes means nothing without the count of trials.

The key idea, derived: successes over trials. For each m, let \(N_m\) be the number of non-friend pairs in the past graph with exactly m mutual friends (the trials), and \(F_m\) the number of those that are friends in the future (the successes). Model each trial as a coin flip: every pair in bucket m becomes a friendship with the same unknown probability \(p_m\), independently. The probability of seeing exactly the outcomes we saw is the likelihood:

\[ L(p) = p^{F}(1-p)^{N-F}, \qquad \ell(p) = \log L(p) = F\log p + (N-F)\log(1-p). \]

The maximum-likelihood estimate is the p that makes the data most probable. Set the derivative to zero:

\[ \ell'(p) = \frac{F}{p} - \frac{N-F}{1-p} = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; F(1-p) = (N-F)\,p \;\Longrightarrow\; \hat p = \frac{F}{N}. \]

It is a maximum because \(\ell''(p) = -F/p^2 - (N-F)/(1-p)^2 < 0\). So the estimate is the obvious one, successes over trials, and now we know why.

Why it is correct, and how sure we are. \(F\) is a sum of N independent 0/1 variables with mean p, so \(E[F] = Np\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(F) = Np(1-p)\). Therefore

\[ E[\hat p] = \frac{E[F]}{N} = p, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(\hat p) = \frac{\mathrm{Var}(F)}{N^2} = \frac{p(1-p)}{N}, \qquad \mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/N}. \]

The estimate is unbiased, and its error shrinks like \(1/\sqrt{N}\). By the central limit theorem \(\hat p\) is close to normal when both F and N − F are large (here F ≥ 24 in every bucket), so \(\hat p \pm 1.96\,\mathrm{SE}\) is an approximate 95% confidence interval.

Cost. Enumerating every pair would be \(\binom{10{,}000}{2} \approx 50\) million pairs. Instead, run the two-hop count from every user u, keeping only v > u so each pair is seen once. That touches exactly the pairs with m ≥ 1, at a total cost of \(\sum_u W(u)\) counter updates (Day 2 shows this equals \(\sum_f d_f^2\)). The bucket m = 0 is never enumerated; it is everything else, by subtraction: \(N_0 = \binom{n}{2} - |E_{\text{past}}| - \sum_{m \ge 1} N_m\). Measured: 1.8 s for the whole curve.

pymk/closure.py · the closure curve
from collections import Counter

def closure_curve(n, past, future):
    """N[m]: non-friend pairs with m mutual friends in the past graph.
    F[m]: how many of those pairs became friends in the future."""
    adj = [set() for _ in range(n)]
    for u, v in past:
        adj[u].add(v); adj[v].add(u)
    fut = {(min(u, v), max(u, v)) for u, v in future}
    N, F = Counter(), Counter()
    for u in range(n):
        count = Counter()
        for f in adj[u]:
            for v in adj[f]:
                if v > u and v not in adj[u]:          # each unordered pair once, non-friends only
                    count[v] += 1
        for v, m in count.items():
            N[m] += 1
            F[m] += (u, v) in fut
    N[0] = n * (n - 1) // 2 - len(past) - sum(N.values())   # every other non-friend pair has m = 0
    F[0] = len(fut) - sum(F.values())
    return N, F

3 · Why ranking by mutual friends is a sensible first score

The question. The product goal (R.1) is the expected number of accepted suggestions among the 10 shown. Sorting candidates by m is a proxy. When is the proxy exactly right?

First attempt. "More mutual friends is better, so sort by m." That is a slogan, not an argument. It says nothing about when it fails.

The key idea, derived. Suppose the chance that u accepts a suggestion v depends only on m = m(u, v), through some function g that never decreases: \(P(\text{accept } v) = g(m_v)\) with \(m \le m' \Rightarrow g(m) \le g(m')\). The table above is evidence that, on average, this holds. For a set S of k suggestions, let \(X_v\) be 1 if v is accepted and 0 otherwise. By linearity of expectation (which needs no independence),

\[ E\Big[\sum_{v \in S} X_v\Big] = \sum_{v \in S} E[X_v] = \sum_{v \in S} g(m_v). \]

Why it is correct: an exchange argument. Claim: the k candidates with the largest m maximise this sum. Take any set S of size k that is not a top-k set. Then some \(v \in S\) and \(w \notin S\) have \(m_w > m_v\). Swap them: the sum changes by \(g(m_w) - g(m_v) \ge 0\), so it does not decrease. Each swap raises the number of top-k members of S by one, so after at most k swaps S is a top-k set, and its value is at least the value we started with. So no set beats a top-k set. Ties in m give equal g, so any tie-break is equally optimal under the model; the contract picks one for determinism (Delivery block).

Where the assumption fails, each one a later day's work:

  • g depends on more than m. The table above shows a 25× spread inside m = 1 alone. Day 5 learns a better score from features.
  • Candidates with m = 0 are invisible: 46.8% of future friendships. Day 4 adds other sources.
  • Acceptances are not a fixed property of the pair. What we show changes what people do, and what they do becomes next month's training data (feedback loops, Day 5).

Where PYMK uses it. This is the ranking rule of the exact baseline. The proof tells you what a better ranker must beat and why: it must estimate g more finely than "m alone".

Solution

Six-person graph. Its edges are 0–1, 0–2, 1–2, 1–3, 1–4, 2–4, 2–5, with two triangles, {0, 1, 2} and {1, 2, 4}. \(C_0 = 1\) (one wedge, closed), \(C_1 = 2/6\) (closed: 0–1–2 and 2–1–4), \(C_2 = 2/6\), \(C_4 = 1\), and 3 and 5 have degree 1. Wedges: 1 + 6 + 6 + 1 = 14, closed 1 + 2 + 2 + 1 = 6 = 3 × 2 triangles, so \(\mathcal{T} = 6/14 \approx 0.43\). The average local is (1 + 1/3 + 1/3 + 1)/4 ≈ 0.67.

\(\mathcal{T} = 1\). If every wedge is closed and x, y are in the same component at distance 2, the wedge between them is closed, so their distance is 1. Hence no two vertices are at distance 2, so none are at distance ≥ 2 (a shortest path of length ≥ 2 starts with a wedge at distance 2), and each component is complete. The converse is immediate.

Wilson interval. Squaring gives a quadratic in p: \((1 + z^2/N)p^2 - (2\hat p + z^2/N)p + \hat p^2 = 0\) with z = 1.96. Its two roots are the interval. It never leaves [0, 1], which the normal interval can.

MLE without logs. \(L'(p) = p^{F-1}(1-p)^{N-F-1}\,[F(1-p) - (N-F)p]\). For \(0 < p < 1\) the first two factors are positive, so \(L'\) has the sign of \(F - Np\): positive below F/N, negative above. The maximum is at \(\hat p = F/N\).

The code for both measurements is in the two blocks above. Both are tested against brute force: clustering on hand-checked graphs, closure_curve against direct pair enumeration on 30 random graphs.

1 · Storing a graph: adjacency matrix, adjacency list, CSR

The question. The baseline needs two operations, fast: neighbors(u), "list u's friends", called once for u and once for every friend; and is_friend(u, v), used by the filters. Which layout serves them on 10,000 users and 100,000 friendships, and which would survive 1 billion users?

First attempt: the adjacency matrix. An n × n table A with \(A_{uv} = 1\) if u and v are friends, else 0. is_friend is a single read, O(1). But neighbors(u) must scan the whole row, n = 10,000 reads to find about 20 friends. And memory is \(n^2\) entries whatever the number of friendships: \(10{,}000^2 = 10^8\) entries, 12.5 MB at one bit each, 100 MB at one byte. The fraction of ones is \(2|E|/n^2 = 0.2\%\): 99.8% of the table stores "not friends". At \(n = 10^9\) it is \(10^{18}\) bits = 125 PB.

The key idea, derived: store only the ones. An adjacency list keeps, for each user, a collection of their friends. Each friendship {u, v} appears twice, as v in u's list and as u in v's, so there are \(\sum_u d_u = 2|E|\) entries in total (the handshake lemma: each edge adds 1 to two degrees). Memory is \(\Theta(n + |E|)\) instead of \(\Theta(n^2)\). What is each list?

  • A hash set: is_friend in O(1) expected time, but every entry carries a hash slot and, in Python, a separate integer object.
  • A sorted array: compact, contiguous, and is_friend by binary search in O(log d).

Compressed sparse row (CSR) takes the sorted-array choice to its end: concatenate all the sorted lists into one array indices of length \(2|E|\), and record where each user's run begins in an array indptr of length \(n+1\). User u's friends are indices[indptr[u] : indptr[u+1]]. Since the runs are laid out in user order, the start of u's run is the total length of all earlier runs, a prefix sum of degrees:

\[ \texttt{indptr}[0] = 0, \qquad \texttt{indptr}[u+1] = \texttt{indptr}[u] + d_u, \qquad \texttt{indptr}[n] = \textstyle\sum_u d_u = 2|E| . \]

Why the construction is correct. build_csr is a counting sort. After the prefix sums, set a cursor fill[u] = indptr[u]. Each edge (u, v) writes v at fill[u] and advances it, and writes u at fill[v] and advances that. Row u receives exactly one write per edge at u, that is \(d_u\) writes, landing at positions indptr[u], …, indptr[u] + d_u − 1 = indptr[u+1] − 1. They fill row u exactly and never spill into row u+1. Sorting each row afterwards makes binary search valid.

pymk/graph.py · CSR
from bisect import bisect_left

def build_csr(n, edges):
    """Compressed sparse row form of an undirected graph:
    the friends of u are indices[indptr[u]:indptr[u + 1]], sorted ascending."""
    deg = [0] * n
    for u, v in edges:
        deg[u] += 1; deg[v] += 1
    indptr = [0] * (n + 1)
    for u in range(n):
        indptr[u + 1] = indptr[u] + deg[u]             # prefix sums: where each row starts
    fill = indptr[:-1]                                 # a copy: next free slot in each row
    indices = [0] * indptr[n]
    for u, v in edges:                                 # each edge is written into both rows
        indices[fill[u]] = v; fill[u] += 1
        indices[fill[v]] = u; fill[v] += 1
    for u in range(n):
        indices[indptr[u]:indptr[u + 1]] = sorted(indices[indptr[u]:indptr[u + 1]])
    return indptr, indices

def neighbors(indptr, indices, u):                     # O(1) to find, O(deg u) to read
    return indices[indptr[u]:indptr[u + 1]]

def is_friend(indptr, indices, u, v):                  # binary search in u's row: O(log deg u)
    lo, hi = indptr[u], indptr[u + 1]
    i = bisect_left(indices, v, lo, hi)
    return i < hi and indices[i] == v

2 · Traversal: breadth-first search and depth-limited search

The question. Who is within two hops of u? Is everyone reachable from everyone? In the six-person graph, from 0: one hop reaches {1, 2}, two hops reach {3, 4, 5}.

First attempt: follow every path. Start at u, step to every friend, from each step to every friend, and so on. A sequence of steps along edges is a walk, and the number of walks multiplies by a degree at every step. From user 0 there are 8 walks of length 2 (0→1→{0, 2, 3, 4}, 0→2→{0, 1, 4, 5}) but only 3 new users at distance 2: 4 is reached twice, and 0, 1 and 2 are walked back to. On the week's graph a user has on average 1,477 length-2 walks; the walks go on forever around cycles, so "follow everything" never even terminates on a full traversal. We need to remember who we have seen.

The key idea, derived. A path is a walk with no repeated vertex; the distance \(\mathrm{dist}(s, v)\) is the number of edges on the shortest path from s to v. Breadth-first search (BFS) visits vertices in order of distance. It keeps a first-in, first-out queue and a label for every vertex it has discovered: start with s labelled 0 in the queue; repeatedly take the front vertex x and, for each neighbour y without a label, label it \(\ell(x) + 1\) and append it. Each vertex is labelled once, at discovery, so it enters the queue once.

pop 23450, 1, 4 labelled (4 is not discovered twice); discover 5

Why it is correct. Three steps.

(a) The queue is ordered. Invariant: the labels in the queue, front to back, never decrease, and the last is at most the first plus 1. It holds for the single vertex at the start. A step removes the front label ℓ and appends labels ℓ + 1. The remaining labels are still ≥ ℓ, the old back label was ≤ ℓ + 1, so the order is kept, and the spread is still at most 1. Consequence: vertices leave the queue in nondecreasing label order.

(b) Labels are never too small. Each label is the length of a real walk from s (the chain of discoveries), so \(\ell(v) \ge \mathrm{dist}(s, v)\).

(c) Labels are never too large. Suppose some vertex has \(\ell(v) > \mathrm{dist}(s, v)\); among all such vertices take one with the smallest distance k. Let x be the vertex before v on a shortest path, so \(\mathrm{dist}(s,x) = k-1\), and by the choice of v, \(\ell(x) = k-1\). When x left the queue, either v had no label, and then it got \(\ell(v) = k\); or v had been discovered by some x′ that left the queue earlier, so by (a) \(\ell(x') \le \ell(x)\) and \(\ell(v) = \ell(x') + 1 \le k\). Both contradict \(\ell(v) > k\). With (b), \(\ell(v) = \mathrm{dist}(s, v)\) for every reachable v. Unreachable vertices are never discovered, so BFS from every unlabelled vertex in turn finds the connected components.

Cost. Each vertex enters and leaves the queue once: O(V). When x leaves, its list is read once, and the lists add up to \(\sum_x d_x = 2|E|\) entries: O(E). Total O(V + E) time, O(V) space for labels and queue. A depth-limited search to depth L expands only vertices with label < L, so its cost is the sum of their degrees. For L = 2 from u:

\[ \text{work} = d_u + \sum_{f \in N(u)} d_f = d_u + W(u). \]

That is exactly the work of the baseline: two-hop counting is a depth-2 BFS that counts how often it reaches each vertex instead of marking it once. Replacing the queue by a stack gives depth-first search: the same O(V + E) and the same reachable set, but no distances (LeetCode 841 and 547 only need reachability).

from collections import deque

def bfs(adj, s, max_depth=None):
    """dist[v] = fewest hops from s to v, for every v within max_depth hops of s."""
    dist = {s: 0}
    queue = deque([s])
    while queue:
        x = queue.popleft()
        if max_depth is not None and dist[x] == max_depth:
            continue                                   # reached the limit: do not expand x
        for y in adj[x]:
            if y not in dist:                          # mark on discovery: y enters the queue once
                dist[y] = dist[x] + 1
                queue.append(y)
    return dist

def components(adj):
    """Number of connected components and a component label for every vertex."""
    label, c = [-1] * len(adj), 0
    for s in range(len(adj)):
        if label[s] == -1:
            for v in bfs(adj, s):
                label[v] = c
            c += 1
    return c, label

3 · Counting with a hash map

The question. The depth-2 walk from u reaches each candidate v once per mutual friend. How do we turn those arrivals into counts?

First attempt. Two obvious ways, both wasteful. (i) Collect every arrival in a list, sort, and count runs: O(W log W) for W arrivals. (ii) An array count of length n: each arrival is O(1), but the array must be allocated or cleared, and then scanned for non-zeros, O(n) per request, which at \(n = 10^9\) is hopeless for 1,477 arrivals.

The key idea: a hash table. A hash table stores keys in an array of M slots, putting key x at slot h(x) for a hash function h; keys that collide share a chain (or, in open addressing, as in Python's dict, probe for the next free slot). Its load factor is α = (keys stored)/M. Model h as sending each key to a uniformly random slot, independently. Then a lookup for x inspects x's slot plus the other keys that landed there, and the expected number of those is

\[ \sum_{y \ne x} P\big(h(y) = h(x)\big) = \frac{K - 1}{M} < \alpha \quad \text{for } K \text{ keys}, \]

so a lookup or insert costs O(1 + α) expected. The table keeps α below a constant by doubling M when it fills. Copying on a doubling is expensive, but the copies after K inserts cost at most K + K/2 + K/4 + … < 2K in total, so each insert is O(1) amortized. Space is proportional to the keys actually touched.

Why the count is right. The loops perform one count[v] += 1 for each pair (f, v) with \(f \in N(u)\) and \(v \in N(f)\). So afterwards \(\texttt{count}[v] = |\{f \in N(u) : v \in N(f)\}|\). In an undirected graph \(v \in N(f) \iff f \in N(v)\), hence

\[ \texttt{count}[v] = |\{ f : f \in N(u),\ f \in N(v) \}| = |N(u) \cap N(v)| = m(u, v) = (A^2)_{uv}. \]

On the six-person graph, user 0's loop makes \(d_1 + d_2 = 4 + 4 = 8\) updates: count[0] = 2 (0 is reached back through both friends), count[1] = count[2] = 1 (each through the other), count[3] = 1, count[4] = 2, count[5] = 1. The filter then removes 0, 1 and 2.

Cost. Exactly W(u) = \(\sum_{f \in N(u)} d_f\) updates at O(1) expected each, plus \(d_u\) to read u's list: O(\(d_u + W(u)\)) time. Space O(number of distinct keys), at most min(W(u), n).

def mutual_counts(adj, u):

    (u itself and u's friends can appear; the caller filters them.)"""
    count = Counter()

    return count

4 · Set intersection, three ways

The question. User u opens v's profile and sees "12 mutual friends". That is one pair, not a whole candidate list: compute \(|N(u) \cap N(v)|\) for lists of sizes \(a \le b\). Example: A = [3, 9, 14, 20], B = [1, 3, 4, 7, 9, 12, 15, 20, 22, 30]; the answer is {3, 9, 20}.

First attempt: nested loops. For each x in A, scan B: \(a \cdot b\) comparisons. A 20-friend user against a 1,480-friend hub is 29,600 comparisons to find a handful of matches.

Way 1 · Hash: probe the larger set. If B is stored as a hash set, test each element of the smaller set: [x for x in A if x in B]. Correct, because x is output exactly when x ∈ A and x ∈ B. Cost: a = min(a, b) lookups at O(1) expected each, so O(min(a, b)), provided the hash set already exists; building one costs O(b). Loop over the smaller set: looping over the larger would cost O(b).

Way 2 · Merge two sorted lists. Keep a pointer i into A and j into B. If \(A_i < B_j\), advance i; if \(A_i > B_j\), advance j; if equal, output it and advance both.

1–23vs1→3vs31 < 3: advance j. Then equal: output 3
3–59vs479skip 4 and 7 in B; equal: output 9
6–714vs1215skip 12; then 14 < 15, so 14 is not in B: advance i
8–920vs1520skip 15; equal: output 20. A is used up: stop after 9 steps (a + b = 14)

Why it is correct. Both lists are sorted with distinct elements. Invariant, before every step: (P) every element of \(A[{:}i]\) and \(B[{:}j]\) is smaller than both \(A_i\) and \(B_j\); (Q) the output so far is \(A[{:}i] \cap B[{:}j]\). Both hold at the start (empty prefixes). Suppose \(A_i < B_j\). Every element of \(B[{:}j]\) is below \(A_i\) by (P), and every element of \(B[j{:}]\) is at least \(B_j > A_i\), so \(A_i \notin B\): advancing i without output keeps (Q), and since \(A_i < B_j \le\) everything after, (P) still holds. The case \(A_i > B_j\) is symmetric. If \(A_i = B_j\), outputting it and advancing both keeps (Q), and (P) holds because the lists are strictly increasing. When the loop stops, say i = a: by (P) nothing in \(A[{:}a] = A\) is in \(B[j{:}]\), so \(A \cap B = A \cap B[{:}j]\), which is the output by (Q). Cost: every step advances i or j, so at most a + b steps, O(a + b) time and O(1) extra space. It reads both lists front to back, the friendliest possible memory pattern.

Way 3 · Galloping (exponential search). When b ≫ a, merging walks through almost all of B to find a elements. Binary-searching each x in all of B costs a log b, but wastes the fact that the positions found only move right. Galloping searches from the last position j: probe \(B_j, B_{j+1}, B_{j+3}, B_{j+7}, \dots\), doubling the stride until a probe is ≥ x, then binary-search the last stride. On the example, x = 9 starts at position 2: probes 4 (too small), 7 (too small), 12 (big enough), then a binary search between 7 and 12 finds 9 at position 4.

Why it costs O(a log(b/a + 1)). Let \(g_i\) be the number of positions of B that the search for the i-th element of A moves past. The doubling phase probes offsets 0, 1, 3, 7, … past the start; it makes one probe for each offset below \(g_i\) plus the one that stops it, so \(t = \lceil\log_2(g_i + 1)\rceil + 1\) probes. The binary search over the last stride, which is shorter than \(2^{t-1}\), costs at most \(t - 1\) more, and one comparison tests equality. So element i costs at most \(2t \le 2\log_2(g_i+1) + 4\) comparisons. The pointer only moves right, so \(\sum_i g_i \le b\). Because log is concave, Jensen's inequality bounds the sum by its value at the average:

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{a} \log_2(g_i + 1) \;\le\; a \log_2\!\Big(\frac{1}{a}\sum_i (g_i + 1)\Big) \;\le\; a \log_2\!\Big(\frac{b}{a} + 1\Big). \]

Total: at most \(2a\log_2(b/a + 1) + 4a\) comparisons. When \(b \approx a\) that is O(a), like merging; when b ≫ a it approaches a log b, like binary search; in between it beats both.

Why no comparison method can do much better. Consider inputs where A and B share no element. To answer "empty" correctly, an algorithm must know, for each x in A, two consecutive elements of B that it falls between; otherwise some input with the same comparison outcomes puts x equal to an element of B, and "empty" would be wrong. Knowing every element's gap determines the whole interleaving of A with B, and there are \(\binom{a+b}{a}\) interleavings. A comparison has two outcomes, so c comparisons distinguish at most \(2^c\) of them:

\[ c \;\ge\; \log_2 \binom{a+b}{a} \;\ge\; \log_2\Big(\frac{a+b}{a}\Big)^{a} \;\ge\; a\log_2\frac{b}{a}, \]

using \(\binom{N}{a} = \prod_{i=0}^{a-1}\frac{N-i}{a-i} \ge (N/a)^a\) because each factor is ≥ N/a. Galloping meets this bound up to a constant factor.

from bisect import bisect_left

def intersect_hash(a, b):
    """a, b: hash sets. Expected O(min(|a|, |b|)) probes."""
    if len(a) > len(b):
        a, b = b, a                                    # loop over the smaller set
    return [x for x in a if x in b]

def intersect_merge(a, b):
    """a, b: sorted lists of distinct ids. At most |a| + |b| steps."""
    i = j = 0
    out = []
    while i < len(a) and j < len(b):
        if a[i] < b[j]:
            i += 1                                     # a[i] is smaller than all of b[j:], so not in b
        elif a[i] > b[j]:
            j += 1
        else:
            out.append(a[i]); i += 1; j += 1
    return out

def gallop(b, x, lo):
    """Smallest i >= lo with b[i] >= x (len(b) if none), in O(log(i - lo + 2)) comparisons."""
    hi, step = lo, 1
    while hi < len(b) and b[hi] < x:
        lo = hi + 1                                    # the answer is past hi
        hi += step
        step *= 2                                      # probe lo, lo+1, lo+3, lo+7, ...
    return bisect_left(b, x, lo, min(hi, len(b)))      # binary search the last gap

def intersect_gallop(a, b):
    """a, b: sorted lists of distinct ids. O(a log(b/a + 1)) for a = the smaller size."""
    if len(a) > len(b):
        a, b = b, a
    out, j = [], 0
    for x in a:
        j = gallop(b, x, j)                            # b[:j] < x: never look there again
        if j == len(b):
            break
        if b[j] == x:
            out.append(x); j += 1
    return out

5 · Top-k, three ways

The question. User u has C candidates with counts (on the week's graph, mean 1,191, max 8,271). Return the best k = 10, ordered by m descending, then id ascending. Write key(v) = (−mv, v); smaller keys are better, and all keys are distinct because ids are.

First attempt: sort everything. Sort the C keys and keep the first 10. Correct by definition. A comparison sort must distinguish all \(C!\) orders, so it needs at least \(\log_2 C! \approx C\log_2 C - 1.44\,C\) comparisons (Stirling), which for the largest set is about 11.6 comparisons per candidate to put 8,261 candidates in an order nobody will see.

Way 1 · Sort: O(C log C) time, O(C) space. Simple, and the right choice when C is small or you need the whole order.

Way 2 · A size-k heap: O(C log k). A binary min-heap is a complete binary tree stored in an array, each node no larger than its children, so the smallest item is at the root; push and pop-root cost O(log size) because the tree has height ⌊log₂ size⌋. Keep the k best candidates seen so far in a min-heap ordered so that the worst kept candidate is at the root (the code stores (m, −v), the reverse of the key order). For each new candidate x: if the heap has fewer than k items, push x; otherwise, if x beats the root, replace the root with x.

Why it is correct. Invariant: after i candidates, the heap holds exactly the best min(i, k) of them. Suppose it holds and the heap is full. The root r is the k-th best of the first i. If x is worse than r, then k candidates beat x, so x is not in the top k of the first i + 1, and the heap is unchanged and still right. If x beats r, the top k of the first i + 1 is the old top k with r pushed out by x, which is what the replacement does.

Cost. C comparisons with the root, plus O(log k) per insertion: O(C log k) in the worst case, O(k) space, and it works on a stream. In a random order it is better than that. The i-th candidate is inserted exactly when it is among the best k of the first i, and in a random order each of those i is equally likely to be any rank, so for \(i > k\) that has probability k/i. The expected number of insertions is

\[ k + \sum_{i=k+1}^{C} \frac{k}{i} \;\approx\; k\Big(1 + \ln\frac{C}{k}\Big), \]

so the expected cost is \(O(C + k\log k \cdot \log(C/k))\): almost all candidates cost one comparison.

Way 3 · Quickselect: O(C) expected. Pick a random pivot p and split the keys into those better than p (less) and those worse. If less has at least k keys, all k answers are in it: continue there. Otherwise every key in less, and p itself, is an answer: keep them, and continue on the worse side for the remaining k − |less| − 1. Finally sort only the k answers.

Why it is correct. Invariant: best holds only true answers, and the remaining answers are exactly the best k (remaining) of keys. If |less| ≥ k, at least k keys beat p and everything worse, so the answers are all in less. Otherwise fewer than k keys beat p, so p and everything better are answers, and the rest of the answers are the best of what is worse than p.

Cost. One round over n keys costs at most 2n comparisons (the code makes two passes). Call a round good if the pivot's rank lands in the middle half, between n/4 and 3n/4. With a uniformly random pivot that happens with probability 1/2, and then whichever side we continue on has at most 3n/4 keys. The number of rounds until a good one is geometric with mean 2, so shrinking from n to at most 3n/4 keys costs at most 2 × 2n = 4n expected comparisons. Summing the shrinking sizes,

\[ E[\text{comparisons}] \;\le\; 4C\Big(1 + \tfrac34 + \big(\tfrac34\big)^2 + \cdots\Big) = 4C \cdot \frac{1}{1 - 3/4} = 16\,C, \]

plus O(k log k) for the final sort: O(C + k log k) expected. The worst case, bad pivots every round, is O(\(C^2\)), and random pivots make it vanishingly unlikely. It needs all C keys in memory and copies them.

import heapq
import random

def topk_sort(count, k):
    """O(C log C): sort every candidate, keep the first k."""
    return sorted(count, key=lambda v: (-count[v], v))[:k]

def topk_heap(count, k):
    """O(C log k) time, O(k) space: keep the k best seen so far in a min-heap."""
    heap = []                                          # items (m, -v); the root is the worst kept
    for v, m in count.items():
        item = (m, -v)
        if len(heap) < k:
            heapq.heappush(heap, item)
        elif item > heap[0]:                           # better than the worst kept: replace it
            heapq.heapreplace(heap, item)
    return [-nv for m, nv in sorted(heap, reverse=True)]

def topk_quickselect(count, k, seed=0):
    """O(C + k log k) expected: select the k best keys, then sort only those."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    keys = [(-m, v) for v, m in count.items()]         # distinct keys; smaller is better
    best = []
    while k > 0 and keys:
        p = rng.choice(keys)
        less = [x for x in keys if x < p]
        if len(less) >= k:
            keys = less                                # all k answers are below the pivot
        else:
            best += less + [p]                         # all of these are answers
            k -= len(less) + 1
            keys = [x for x in keys if x > p]
    return [v for _, v in sorted(best)]

        for y in x.neighbors:

    seen, islands = set(), 0
    for r in range(rows):
        for c in range(cols):
            if grid[r][c] == "1" and (r, c) not in seen:
                islands += 1
                seen.add((r, c)); queue = deque([(r, c)])
                while queue:
                    x, y = queue.popleft()
                    for nx, ny in ((x + 1, y), (x - 1, y), (x, y + 1), (x, y - 1)):
                        if 0 <= nx < rows and 0 <= ny < cols and grid[nx][ny] == "1" \
                                and (nx, ny) not in seen:
                            seen.add((nx, ny)); queue.append((nx, ny))
    return islands

def findCircleNum(isConnected):                        # 547: components of a matrix graph. O(n^2)
    n = len(isConnected)
    adj = [{j for j in range(n) if isConnected[i][j] and j != i} for i in range(n)]
    return components(adj)[0]

def canVisitAllRooms(rooms):                           # 841: reachability from room 0. O(V + E)
    seen, stack = {0}, [0]
    while stack:
        for key in rooms[stack.pop()]:
            if key not in seen:
                seen.add(key); stack.append(key)
    return len(seen) == len(rooms)

def validPath(n, edges, source, destination):          # 1971: BFS from source. O(V + E)
    adj = graph_from(n, edges)
    return destination in bfs(adj, source)

def intersection(nums1, nums2):                        # 349: hash sets. O(n1 + n2)
    return list(set(nums1) & set(nums2))

def intersect(nums1, nums2):                           # 350: multiset intersection with a counter
    if len(nums1) > len(nums2):
        nums1, nums2 = nums2, nums1                    # count the smaller array
    left = Counter(nums1)
    out = []
    for x in nums2:
        if left[x] > 0:
            out.append(x); left[x] -= 1
    return out

def topKFrequent(nums, k):                             # 347: bucket by count. O(n)
    count = Counter(nums)
    buckets = [[] for _ in range(len(nums) + 1)]       # a count is at most n
    for x, c in count.items():
        buckets[c].append(x)
    out = []
    for c in range(len(nums), 0, -1):
        for x in buckets[c]:
            out.append(x)
            if len(out) == k:
                return out
    return out

def findKthLargest(nums, k, seed=0):                   # 215: quickselect. O(n) expected
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    while True:
        p = rng.choice(nums)
        hi = [x for x in nums if x > p]
        eq = sum(1 for x in nums if x == p)
        if k <= len(hi):
            nums = hi
        elif k <= len(hi) + eq:
            return p
        else:
            k -= len(hi) + eq
            nums = [x for x in nums if x < p]

350's follow-ups. If both arrays are sorted, merge them (Way 2), O(a + b) and O(1) space. If nums1 is much smaller, count the smaller one (the code does this) or gallop through the larger. If nums2 is on disk and memory is small, sort both externally and merge: merging reads each list once, front to back.

Bucket top-k. Make \(d_u\) buckets; put each candidate in bucket m; walk buckets from \(d_u\) down and, inside a bucket, take ids in ascending order until k are taken. Distributing is O(C); the walk is O(\(d_u\)) plus sorting only the last bucket touched.

Many lists. Intersect the two smallest first, then intersect the (small) result with the next smallest, and so on. Each intermediate result is no larger than the smallest list, so every step is a small list against a large one, the case galloping is built for. Demaine, López-Ortiz and Munro analyse adaptive orders.

Bidirectional BFS. Expand the smaller frontier one level at a time from both ends; stop when a newly discovered vertex is labelled from the other side. With branching factor d each side explores about \(d^{L/2}\) vertices, so \(2d^{L/2}\) instead of \(d^{L}\). The distance is the sum of the two labels at the meeting vertex, minimised over all meeting vertices found in that level.

Directed graphs. Two CSRs: out-edges (whom u follows) and in-edges (who follows u), the second being the transpose. "Accounts followed by accounts you follow" uses out-edges twice; "accounts that follow the same people as you" uses out then in.

Delivery · the contract and its tests

The question

The baseline will be called by a service, re-implemented as a batch job, sharded (Day 3) and approximated (Day 4). Each of those versions must be compared with "the right answer". What exactly is the right answer, and how do we check, automatically, that a version gives it?

First attempt

Run it for one user and look at the output. Ten plausible ids with plausible counts look the same whether ties go to the smaller id or the larger, whether a blocked user was removed before or after taking the top 10, and whether an existing friend slipped in. Looking has no oracle: nothing tells you what the output should have been.

The key idea: a contract, then one test per kind of oracle

A contract states what a function promises for every valid input, so that every rule can become a check. For the exact baseline:

Inputadj: a list of sets, symmetric (\(v \in N(u) \iff u \in N(v)\)), no self-loops; u in range; k an int; blocked a set of idsThe count proof in part 3 uses symmetry

pymk/exact.py · the exact baseline
import heapq
from collections import Counter

def suggest(adj, u, k=10, blocked=frozenset()):
    """The exact baseline. Returns up to k pairs (v, m), best first, where
    m = |N(u) & N(v)| >= 1 and v is eligible: v != u, v not already u's friend,
    v not in blocked (users u blocked or who blocked u, and users who opted out).
    Order: m descending, then v ascending, so the output is deterministic."""
    if not 0 <= u < len(adj):
        raise KeyError(u)
    if k <= 0:
        return []
    direct = adj[u]
    count = Counter()
    for f in direct:
        for v in adj[f]:
            if v != u and v not in direct and v not in blocked:   # filter before ranking
                count[v] += 1
    best = heapq.nsmallest(k, count, key=lambda v: (-count[v], v))
    return [(v, count[v]) for v in best]

Tests differ by where their oracle comes from, and that decides which bugs each can see:

  • A golden test uses an answer computed by hand on a tiny input: the six-person graph, where user 0 must get [(4, 2), (3, 1), (5, 1)]. Precise, but it sees only what that one graph exercises.
  • A property test checks rules that must hold for every input, on hundreds of random graphs: eligibility, order, no duplicates, symmetry \(m(u,v) = m(v,u)\), top-k is a prefix of top-n, the same output after shuffling the edges. It needs no answers, only rules.
  • A differential test compares with an independent implementation of the definition: here the matrix product, \(m(u,v) = (A^2)_{uv}\), computed with NumPy on small random graphs.

import random
import numpy as np

# Each check takes the implementation under test: check_golden(suggest).
def graph_from(n, edges):
    adj = [set() for _ in range(n)]
    for u, v in edges:
        if u != v:
            adj[u].add(v); adj[v].add(u)
    return adj

GOLDEN = [(0, 1), (0, 2), (1, 2), (1, 3), (1, 4), (2, 4), (2, 5)]   # 0 = you; friends 1, 2; 4 shares both

def check_golden(suggest):
    adj = graph_from(6, GOLDEN)
    assert suggest(adj, 0, k=10) == [(4, 2), (3, 1), (5, 1)]
    assert suggest(adj, 0, k=2) == [(4, 2), (3, 1)]            # the tie 3 vs 5 goes to the smaller id
    assert suggest(adj, 0, k=1, blocked={4}) == [(3, 1)]       # blocked users do not use up a slot
    assert suggest(adj, 3, k=10) == [(0, 1), (2, 1), (4, 1)]
    assert suggest(adj, 1, k=10) == [(5, 1)]                   # 0, 2, 3, 4 are already friends
    assert suggest(adj, 0, k=0) == []

def random_graph(rng):
    n = rng.randint(2, 14)
    edges = [(rng.randrange(n), rng.randrange(n)) for _ in range(rng.randint(0, 3 * n))]
    return n, edges

def check_properties(suggest, trials=300, seed=1):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    for _ in range(trials):
        n, edges = random_graph(rng)
        adj = graph_from(n, edges)
        u = rng.randrange(n)
        blocked = {v for v in range(n) if rng.random() < 0.2}
        out = suggest(adj, u, k=n, blocked=blocked)
        vs = [v for v, _ in out]
        assert len(vs) == len(set(vs))                                   # no duplicates
        assert out == sorted(out, key=lambda p: (-p[1], p[0]))           # documented order
        for v, m in out:
            assert v != u and v not in adj[u] and v not in blocked      # eligibility
            assert m >= 1
            assert dict(suggest(adj, v, k=n)).get(u) == m               # symmetry m(u,v) = m(v,u)
        k = rng.randint(1, 4)
        assert suggest(adj, u, k=k, blocked=blocked) == out[:k]         # top-k is a prefix
        shuffled = edges[:]; rng.shuffle(shuffled)
        assert suggest(graph_from(n, shuffled), u, k=n, blocked=blocked) == out   # deterministic

def check_against_matrix(suggest, trials=200, seed=2):
    """Differential test: the oracle is the matrix definition m(u, v) = (A @ A)[u, v]."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    for _ in range(trials):
        n, edges = random_graph(rng)
        adj = graph_from(n, edges)
        A = np.zeros((n, n), dtype=np.int64)
        for x in range(n):
            for y in adj[x]:
                A[x, y] = 1
        M = A @ A
        for u in range(n):
            want = [(v, int(M[u, v])) for v in range(n)
                    if v != u and A[u, v] == 0 and M[u, v] > 0]
            want.sort(key=lambda p: (-p[1], p[0]))
            assert suggest(adj, u, k=n) == want

Why it works: plant bugs and see which tests fail

A test suite is only as good as the bugs it catches, so plant some (mutation testing) and run every check against each. Four realistic bugs, measured:

Two lessons, both measured. The differential test never passes a block list, so it cannot see the filtering bug; only the golden and property tests can. And the golden graph had to be chosen: without the edge 1–2, no friend of user 0 is also two hops from 0, and the golden test missed "keeps existing friends" (the measurement script runs that version too). Every oracle has blind spots, so use all three, and design the golden graph so that every rule has something to bite on.

Cost

The property check runs 300 random graphs of up to 14 users; the matrix check builds an n × n matrix and multiplies it, O(\(n^3\)), so it is only for small n. On the full graph the oracle is the definition itself: \(|N(u) \cap N(v)|\) for every v, which for 300 users took 2.4 s.

Practice
  • Add a property for updates: after adding the friendship u–v for a candidate v, v disappears from u's list, and the count of every other candidate w of u rises by 1 exactly when \(w \in N(v)\). Prove it before you test it.
  • Rewrite check_properties with Hypothesis, plant a bug, and look at the smallest failing graph it reports.
  • Invent a fifth bug that all three checks miss. Then add a check that catches it.
Problem variations
  • Fair ties. "Smaller id first" always favours early users (small ids). Design a tie-break that is still deterministic but does not favour anyone systematically.
  • A time budget. Extend the contract for a request that must answer within a fixed read budget. What may change in the output, and what must not?
  • Asymmetric input. What should suggest do if adj is not symmetric? Write the check that detects it, and decide where it runs.
Solution

The update property. After adding u–v, \(N'(u) = N(u) \cup \{v\}\), so v is a friend and ineligible. For another candidate w (not u, not v), \(N'(w)\) gains u only if \(w = v\), which it is not; so \(m'(u,w) = |N'(u) \cap N(w)| = m(u,w) + [v \in N(w)]\). That is the incremental update rule Day 3 builds on.

A bug all three miss. One example: returning m as a float (2.0 instead of 2). Python's == treats them as equal. A type check (all(type(m) is int …)) catches it. The lesson is that equality-based oracles inherit the blind spots of equality.

Fair ties. Break ties by a hash of the pair, for example sha256(f"{seed}:{u}:{v}"): deterministic for a given seed, unrelated to account age, and rotated by changing the seed. Do not use Python's built-in hash of strings, which is randomised per process unless PYTHONHASHSEED is set.

Time budget. The eligibility rules, the order rule on whatever is returned, and determinism for a given budget must not change; the counts and the set of candidates may be approximate, and the response should say so. Day 4 writes this as the candidate-budget contract.

Asymmetric input. Check \(v \in N(u) \Rightarrow u \in N(v)\) once, where the graph is built or loaded, not on every request: it costs O(E). On failure, refuse to load; a silently wrong count is worse than an outage of the batch job.
