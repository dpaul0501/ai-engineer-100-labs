DAY 1 · TEXT AS DATAMLDSAAI

What text looks like to a counter

BPE does one thing over and over: it counts, then picks the largest count. Today answers three questions about that. How are the counts of text distributed (ML)? How do you count, and pick the largest counts, fast (algorithms)? And what exactly is being counted: where does one piece of text end and the next begin (design)?

Throughout, a word means a maximal run of ASCII letters and apostrophes, lower-cased; the <|endoftext|> separators of alice.txt are removed first. By that rule Alice in Wonderland has 26,956 words. A type is a distinct word: there are 2,559 types. The frequency \(f\) of a type is how many times it occurs, and its rank \(r\) is its position when the types are sorted by frequency, most frequent first.

ML · Zipf's law, fitting it, and Heaps' law

1. How does frequency depend on rank?

The question. Sort the types of Alice by frequency. Is there a rule that gives the frequency of the type at rank \(r\)?

First attempt. The numbers fall fast, roughly like \(1/r\): the second word is about half as frequent as the first. If frequency were exactly \(C/r\), the product \(f \cdot r\) would be the constant \(C\). It is not: it climbs from 1,603 to 4,900 and falls back. A single guessed formula with no free parameter is too rigid. We need a family of curves with a parameter we can fit, and a way to see whether the family fits at all.

The key idea. A power law in rank is

\[ f(r) = C\, r^{-s}, \qquad C > 0,\ s > 0. \]

The exponent \(s\) says how fast frequency falls. Doubling the rank divides the frequency by \(2^s\): with \(s = 1\), the word at rank 20 is half as frequent as the word at rank 10; with \(s = 2\), a quarter. Zipf's law is the observation that word frequencies in natural text follow this family with \(s\) close to 1.

Why plot it on log–log axes? Take logarithms of both sides:

\[ \log f = \log C - s \log r. \]

This is a straight line in the variables \(x = \log r\), \(y = \log f\), with slope \(-s\) and intercept \(\log C\). The converse also holds: if \(\log f = a + b \log r\), then \(f = e^{a} r^{b}\), a power law with \(s = -b\). So "the points lie on a straight line on log–log axes" and "frequency is a power law in rank" are the same statement. Curvature on the log–log plot is direct evidence against a single power law.

What it predicts. If \(f(r) = C/r\) for \(r = 1, \dots, V\), the top \(k\) types cover a share \(\sum_{r \le k} 1/r \,\big/\, \sum_{r \le V} 1/r = H_k / H_V\) of the text, where \(H_k = 1 + \tfrac12 + \dots + \tfrac1k \approx \ln k + 0.577\) is the harmonic number. Coverage grows like \(\ln k\): every tenfold increase in \(k\) adds the same share. With \(V = 2{,}559\) this predicts 35%, 62% and 89% for \(k\) = 10, 100, 1,000. The book gives:

The head is flatter than \(1/r\) (the top 10 cover less than predicted; this is the rising \(f \cdot r\) in the table), and the tail is steeper. Meanwhile 1,105 types (43% of all types) occur exactly once: the long tail.

Why does it hold? Zipf's law is an empirical regularity, not a theorem; there is no derivation of it from the rules of English. Several simple processes produce it, which is part of why it appears so widely. In Simon's "rich get richer" model (1955), each new word is a fresh type with a small fixed probability, and otherwise repeats an earlier word chosen in proportion to how often it has already been used; the counts end up power-law distributed. Miller (1957) showed that even random typing, with the space bar as one key among many, gives "words" whose rank–frequency curve is close to a power law, because each extra letter makes a word both rarer and one of many more possible words. Mandelbrot's variant \(f \propto (r + b)^{-s}\) bends the head, which matches the flat start in the table above. For this course the law matters as a description, not an explanation: a few types carry most of the text, and most types are rare.

2. Fitting the exponent

The question. Given the counts, what is \(s\)? And how sure are we?

First attempt: least squares on the log–log points. Compute \((\log r, \log f)\) for every type and fit a straight line by least squares. The slope is the estimate of \(-s\). On Alice this gives −1.22 using all 2,559 points and −1.11 using only the top 1,000 ranks (zipf.py below). The two answers differ by more than the precision we would like, and that is the first sign of trouble. Least squares is the right tool when every point is an independent measurement with the same amount of Gaussian noise. These points break all three assumptions:

  • Unequal weight. Ranks are evenly spaced on the \(r\) axis, so on the \(\log r\) axis most points crowd into the far right. In Alice, 1,509 of the 2,559 points (59%) are types seen once or twice; they form flat steps (all types with \(f = 1\) share one \(y\) value over a long run of ranks). Least squares gives each point equal say, so the noisiest part of the data, the tail, decides the slope.
  • Unequal noise. A count is a random quantity. A type whose expected count is 1.4 shows up as 0, 1, 2 or 3; on the log scale those are wildly different values, while a count of 1,603 barely moves. The noise on \(\log f\) is small at the head and huge at the tail. And types whose count happened to be 0 are not in the data at all, which cuts the tail off from below.
  • Not independent. The ranks come from sorting the counts, so neighbouring points are tied together: one lucky word pushes every word below it down a rank.

Fitting a line to a histogram instead (log of the number of types with frequency \(x\), against \(\log x\)) is worse still: most frequencies above 30 occur for zero or one type, and empty bins cannot be logged at all.

The key idea: model the counts and maximize the likelihood. Ask a different question with the same content. Pick a type at random. What is the probability that its frequency is \(x\)? A power law in this frequency distribution says

\[ p(x) \propto x^{-\alpha}, \qquad x \ge x_{\min}. \]

Its exponent \(\alpha\) is related to the rank exponent \(s\), but it is not the same number. Here is the link. Suppose \(f(r) = C r^{-s}\). The number of types with frequency at least \(x\) is the rank at which the frequency drops to \(x\): solve \(C r^{-s} = x\) to get \(r(x) = (C/x)^{1/s}\). Dividing by the number of types \(V\) gives the fraction of types with frequency \(\ge x\):

\[ P(X \ge x) = \frac{r(x)}{V} \propto x^{-1/s}. \]

The probability density is minus the derivative of this, \(p(x) \propto x^{-1/s - 1}\). So

\[ \alpha = 1 + \frac{1}{s}, \qquad s = \frac{1}{\alpha - 1}. \]

Classic Zipf, \(s = 1\), is \(\alpha = 2\). A steeper rank curve (larger \(s\)) means a smaller \(\alpha\): more types pile up at low frequencies. The least-squares slope −1.22 corresponds to \(\alpha = 1 + 1/1.22 = 1.82\).

Now fit \(\alpha\) by maximum likelihood: choose the \(\alpha\) under which the observed frequencies \(x_1, \dots, x_n\) (one per type with \(x_i \ge x_{\min}\)) are most probable. Start with the continuous version, where the density with the right normalizing constant is

\[ p(x) = \frac{\alpha - 1}{x_{\min}} \left(\frac{x}{x_{\min}}\right)^{-\alpha}, \qquad x \ge x_{\min},\ \alpha > 1. \]

(Check: \(\int_{x_{\min}}^\infty p(x)\,dx = 1\).) The log-likelihood of independent observations is the sum of the log densities:

\[ \ell(\alpha) = n \ln(\alpha - 1) - n \ln x_{\min} - \alpha \sum_{i=1}^{n} \ln \frac{x_i}{x_{\min}}. \]

Set the derivative to zero: \(\ell'(\alpha) = \frac{n}{\alpha - 1} - \sum_i \ln(x_i / x_{\min}) = 0\), so

\[ \hat\alpha = 1 + n \left[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} \ln \frac{x_i}{x_{\min}} \right]^{-1}. \]

The second derivative is \(-n/(\alpha-1)^2 < 0\), so this is the maximum. The same second derivative gives the standard error: the Fisher information is \(n/(\alpha-1)^2\), so \(\sigma_{\hat\alpha} \approx (\hat\alpha - 1)/\sqrt{n}\). This is the estimator of Clauset, Shalizi and Newman (2009), Section 3. Notice what the estimator does: every type gets equal weight, through \(\ln(x_i/x_{\min})\), and there is no binning and no sorting.

Counts are integers. The formula above assumes \(x\) is continuous. Word counts are 1, 2, 3, …, and at \(x_{\min} = 1\) the difference matters: every type seen once adds \(\ln(1/1) = 0\) to the sum, as if it had no information. On Alice the continuous formula at \(x_{\min} = 1\) returns 2.00, which is wrong. The discrete power law is

\[ p(x) = \frac{x^{-\alpha}}{\zeta(\alpha, x_{\min})}, \qquad \zeta(\alpha, x_{\min}) = \sum_{k = x_{\min}}^{\infty} k^{-\alpha}, \]

with log-likelihood \(\ell(\alpha) = -n \ln \zeta(\alpha, x_{\min}) - \alpha \sum_i \ln x_i\). There is no closed form, but \(\ell\) is concave in \(\alpha\) (\(\ln \zeta\) is a log of a sum of exponentials \(e^{-\alpha \ln k}\), which is convex), so it has one maximum and a simple one-dimensional search finds it (alpha_discrete below).

Choosing \(x_{\min}\). Real data rarely follow a power law all the way down. Clauset et al. pick \(x_{\min}\) by fitting \(\alpha\) for each candidate and keeping the one where the fitted CDF is closest to the data's CDF on \(x \ge x_{\min}\), in Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance (the largest vertical gap between the two CDFs). Below \(x_{\min}\), the model makes no claim.

Confirm by measuring, part 1: a case where we know the answer. Draw 2,559 frequencies from a discrete power law with known \(\alpha\) (the same number of types as Alice), fit with each method, and repeat 100 times.

The histogram fit is off by more than a factor of two. The rank fit is biased: its error (0.04 to 0.08) is larger than its own spread, so averaging more books would not fix it. (It also has the theoretical guarantees: it is consistent, and asymptotically efficient, meaning that for large \(n\) its variance approaches the Cramér–Rao lower bound, the smallest any unbiased estimator can have.)

Two lessons. First, the estimate depends on \(x_{\min}\): the bottom of the distribution (the 1,105 types seen once) is steeper than the body, so Alice is not one power law from top to bottom. Second, once the tail is excluded by a principled rule, the maximum-likelihood exponent (\(s \approx 1.15\)) agrees with the least-squares slope on the top 1,000 ranks (1.11): the least-squares number was pulled down by the tail, as the argument above said it would be.

Cost. Counting is \(O(N)\) for \(N\) words with a hash map (next block). The continuous estimator is one pass over the \(V\) types, \(O(V)\). The discrete one evaluates \(\ell\) a fixed number of times (60 in the code), each costing \(O(V + K)\) for \(K\) terms of the zeta sum.

3. Heaps' law: does the vocabulary ever close?

The question. Read the book one word at a time. After \(n\) words, how many types \(V(n)\) have you seen? Does \(V(n)\) level off?

First attempt. Assume English has a fixed vocabulary. Then after enough text you would have seen all of it, and \(V(n)\) would flatten to a constant. Alice says otherwise:

Distinct words \(V(n)\) after reading \(n\) words of Alice
\(n\) = 1,000378
2,000593

10,0001,504
20,0002,169
26,956 (all)2,559
Doubling from 10,000 to 20,000 words still adds 665 new types. Growth slows but does not stop.

The key idea. Heaps' law says vocabulary grows as a power of text length,

\[ V(n) \approx K n^{\beta}, \qquad 0 < \beta < 1, \]

a straight line of slope \(\beta\) on log–log axes, for the same reason as before. Least squares on \((\log n, \log V(n))\) is a reasonable fit here, unlike for Zipf: each point is a large, smooth count, not a noisy small one. Fitting the points \(n = 1{,}000, 1{,}100, \dots\) gives \(\beta = 0.56\) and \(K = 8.4\) on Alice.

Where it comes from. Heaps' law follows from Zipf's law if words are drawn independently. Let the type at rank \(r\) have probability \(p_r = r^{-s}/Z\) with \(s > 1\). After \(n\) draws it has probably been seen once its expected count \(n p_r\) reaches about 1, that is for \(r \lesssim (n/Z)^{1/s}\). So \(V(n) \approx (n/Z)^{1/s}\), which is Heaps' law with \(\beta = 1/s\). We checked this on simulated text: independent draws from a pure Zipf law with \(s = 1.22\) give a fitted \(\beta = 0.81\) (prediction 0.82), and \(s = 1.47\) gives 0.67 (prediction 0.68). Alice's \(\beta = 0.56\) is lower than \(1/s\) for any of its fitted exponents. Shuffling the book's words (same counts, order destroyed) gives \(\beta = 0.53\), so word order is not the cause. The cause is the one found above: Alice's counts are not a single power law, so \(\beta = 1/s\) is a guide, not an identity, on real text.

Why a word vocabulary can never be closed. Differentiate: \(V'(n) = K\beta\, n^{\beta - 1}\). Because \(\beta < 1\) this shrinks as \(n\) grows, but it is positive for every \(n\): each new word you read has a probability of about \(K\beta n^{\beta-1}\) of being a type you have never seen. At the end of Alice the fit predicts 54 new types per 1,000 words, and the last 10% of the book (2,696 words) brought 145 new types, 54 per 1,000. Extrapolating the fit, ten times as much text of the same kind would hold about 9,500 types. No finite word list is ever complete: every new document brings names, misspellings, numbers and new words. A model whose vocabulary is a list of words must map all of these to one "unknown" token and loses them.

Where BPE uses it. The head of Zipf's law is why BPE's first merges pay so well: a few pairs cover much of the text. The tail is why rare words can never get their own tokens (there are not enough occurrences to learn them), so they must be built from shared pieces. Heaps' law is why the base alphabet must be closed: BPE starts from the 256 byte values, which can spell any string, so nothing is ever "unknown".

Solution
zipf.py
import math, re
from collections import Counter

def read_words(path="data/alice.txt"):
    text = open(path, encoding="utf-8").read().replace("<|endoftext|>", " ")
    return [w.lower() for w in re.findall(r"[A-Za-z']+", text)]

def ls_slope(xs, ys):                     # least-squares slope of y on x
    mx, my = sum(xs) / len(xs), sum(ys) / len(ys)
    return sum((x - mx) * (y - my) for x, y in zip(xs, ys)) / sum((x - mx) ** 2 for x in xs)

def zipf_slope(words, top=None):
    freqs = sorted(Counter(words).values(), reverse=True)[:top]
    xs = [math.log(r) for r in range(1, len(freqs) + 1)]
    ys = [math.log(f) for f in freqs]
    return ls_slope(xs, ys)

words = read_words()
print(len(words), len(set(words)))        # 26956 2559
print(zipf_slope(words))                  # -1.22
print(zipf_slope(words, top=1000))        # -1.11

A slope of −1.22 means frequency falls a little faster than \(1/r\) overall. Fitting only the top 1,000 ranks gives −1.11, closer to the classic −1, because it drops most of the flat steps of the tail.

power_law_mle.py
def alpha_continuous(freqs, xmin):
    """Clauset, Shalizi, Newman (2009), Section 3. Exact for continuous data."""
    xs = [x for x in freqs if x >= xmin]
    return 1 + len(xs) / sum(math.log(x / xmin) for x in xs)

def zeta(a, xmin, K=10_000):              # sum_{k >= xmin} k^-a, with an integral tail
    head = sum(k ** -a for k in range(xmin, xmin + K))
    return head + (xmin + K) ** (1 - a) / (a - 1) + 0.5 * (xmin + K) ** -a

def alpha_discrete(freqs, xmin):
    """Maximize the exact discrete log-likelihood  -n log zeta(a, xmin) - a sum log x."""
    xs = [x for x in freqs if x >= xmin]
    n, slog = len(xs), sum(math.log(x) for x in xs)
    loglik = lambda a: -n * math.log(zeta(a, xmin)) - a * slog
    lo, hi = 1.01, 4.0                    # loglik is concave in a: ternary search
    for _ in range(60):
        m1, m2 = lo + (hi - lo) / 3, hi - (hi - lo) / 3
        if loglik(m1) < loglik(m2): lo = m1
        else: hi = m2
    return (lo + hi) / 2

freqs = list(Counter(words).values())
print(alpha_continuous(freqs, 1))         # 2.00  (wrong tool: the data are integers)
print(alpha_discrete(freqs, 1))           # 1.68
print(alpha_discrete(freqs, 6))           # 1.87  (xmin = 6 chosen by the KS distance)

The ternary search keeps the interval containing the maximum of a concave function and shrinks it by a third each step; 60 steps shrink 3 units to under \(10^{-10}\). The zeta tail beyond \(K\) terms is replaced by its integral, \(\int_{x_{\min}+K}^\infty k^{-\alpha}\,dk\), plus half the first omitted term.

heaps.py
def heaps_fit(words, every=100, start=1000):
    seen, pts = set(), []
    for n, w in enumerate(words, 1):
        seen.add(w)
        if n % every == 0 and n >= start:
            pts.append((math.log(n), math.log(len(seen))))
    xs, ys = zip(*pts)
    beta = ls_slope(xs, ys)
    K = math.exp(sum(ys) / len(ys) - beta * sum(xs) / len(xs))
    return beta, K

print(heaps_fit(words))                   # (0.56, 8.4)

Algorithms · counting and hashing, top-k, sliding windows, UTF-8

1. Counting with a hash map

The question. Count how often each of the 2,559 types occurs among the 26,956 words. More generally: keep a value for each key, where keys arrive in any order and the set of keys is not known in advance.

First attempt. Keep a list of (word, count) pairs and search it for each word. A search scans up to \(V\) entries, so the whole count costs \(O(N V)\): here up to 26,956 × 2,559, about 69 million comparisons. A sorted list with binary search makes lookups \(O(\log V)\), but inserting a new word in the middle shifts \(O(V)\) entries. Sorting all the words and counting runs of equal words costs \(O(N \log N)\) and needs the whole text first.

The key idea, step by step.

  • Direct addressing. If keys were the integers \(0, \dots, m-1\), an array of size \(m\) would do: count[key] += 1 is \(O(1)\). Words are not small integers, so turn them into one.
  • Hash function. A function \(h\) from keys to integers, cheap to compute and spreading keys evenly. The key goes to bucket \(h(k) \bmod m\) of an array of \(m\) buckets.
  • Collisions. There are infinitely many strings and only \(m\) buckets, so by the pigeonhole principle some different keys share a bucket. Separate chaining keeps a short list per bucket and compares keys inside it. (Open addressing, used by Python's dict, instead stores everything in the array and probes other slots on a collision.)
  • Load factor. \(\lambda = n/m\), the average number of keys per bucket. The cost of an operation depends on \(\lambda\), not on \(n\) itself, so keep \(\lambda\) bounded by growing the array.
m = 40:the1:andto2:3:a4 keys, λ = 1. and and to collide in bucket 1; a lookup of to compares 2 keys
insertit5 > m. Doubling: allocate 8 buckets and re-insert every key at \(h(k) \bmod 8\)
m = 80:the1:and3:a5:to6:itλ = 0.625. The collision is gone; chains are short again
Bucket numbers are illustrative; they depend on the hash function.

Why it is fast: expected cost. Assume simple uniform hashing: each key lands in each bucket with probability \(1/m\), independently of the others. Then:

  • A search for a missing key scans one whole chain. Its expected length is \(n/m = \lambda\), so the cost is \(O(1 + \lambda)\) (the 1 is computing the hash).
  • A search for a present key. Chains append new keys at the end. Searching for the \(i\)-th key inserted scans the keys inserted before it in its bucket, then itself. Each of the \(i-1\) earlier keys is in the same bucket with probability \(1/m\), so the expected number of comparisons is \(1 + (i-1)/m\). Averaged over the \(n\) keys:
\[ \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(1 + \frac{i-1}{m}\right) = 1 + \frac{n-1}{2m} \approx 1 + \frac{\lambda}{2}. \]

With \(\lambda \le 1\), both are \(O(1)\) expected.

Why resizing is cheap: amortized cost. When \(n\) passes \(m\), allocate \(2m\) buckets and move every key: one resize costs \(O(n)\). But resizes are rare. If the final table has \(n\) keys, the last resize moved at most \(n\) keys, the one before at most \(n/2\), and so on, so all resizes together moved at most \(n + n/2 + n/4 + \dots < 2n\) keys. Inserting \(n\) keys therefore costs \(O(n)\) in total, which is amortized \(O(1)\) per insert: any single insert may be slow, but the average over any sequence of inserts is constant. (Growing by a fixed amount instead, say 8 buckets at a time, would make the total \(O(n^2)\).)

Expected versus worst case. The \(O(1)\) is an average over the hash function's behaviour. In the worst case every key lands in one bucket: each lookup is \(O(n)\), and building the table is \(O(n^2)\). This is not only theoretical. An attacker who knows the hash function can send keys that all collide ("hash flooding"; Crosby and Wallach, 2003). That is why Python randomizes its string hash per process (SipHash, PEP 456): the same word hashes differently each run, so collisions cannot be planned.

Keys must not change. A key's bucket is computed from its value when it is inserted. If the value later changed, the key would sit in the wrong bucket and never be found. So Python only hashes immutable values: the tuple (97, 116) can be a pair key, the list [97, 116] cannot.

Confirm by measuring. Inserting the 2,559 types of Alice into a chained table (FNV-1a hash, start at 8 buckets, double when \(n > m\)) ends with 4,096 buckets, \(\lambda = 0.625\). The resizes moved 4,088 keys in total, 1.60 per key, under the bound of 2. A successful lookup compares 1.316 keys on average; the formula \(1 + (n-1)/2m\) gives 1.312. The longest chain has 5 keys.

Where BPE uses it. Every count in BPE is a hash map: chunk → count, and pair → count with tuple keys, built by Counter(zip(ids, ids[1:])). Python's Counter is a dict whose missing keys read as 0.

2. Top-k: the k largest counts

The question. Given \(m\) distinct items with counts (total count \(n\)), return the \(k\) with the largest counts. For Alice with \(k = 3\): the, and, to.

First attempt. Find the maximum, remove it, repeat \(k\) times: \(O(km)\). Fine for \(k = 1\) (one scan), but \(O(m^2)\) when \(k\) is a fixed fraction of \(m\). Four better ways follow, each with its cost derived.

Way 1: sort. Sort all \(m\) items by count, take the first \(k\). Comparison sorting costs \(O(m \log m)\), and no comparison sort can do better in the worst case: there are \(m!\) possible orders and each comparison has two outcomes, so at least \(\log_2 m! = \Theta(m \log m)\) comparisons are needed. Correct by definition; wasteful when \(k \ll m\), because it also orders the \(m - k\) items we throw away.

Way 2: a size-\(k\) min-heap. A binary min-heap keeps a set with its smallest element readable in \(O(1)\) at the root, and insert or remove-the-smallest in \(O(\log \text{size})\). (Day 4 builds it from scratch.) Scan the items once, keeping a heap of the best \(k\) seen so far:

  • if the heap has fewer than \(k\) items, push the new one;
  • otherwise, if the new count beats the root (the weakest kept item), replace the root with the new item.

Invariant: after processing \(i\) items, the heap holds the \(k\) largest among the first \(i\). Proof by induction. It holds trivially while \(i \le k\). Suppose it holds after \(i\) items, and item \(i+1\) has count \(x\). The \(k\) largest of the first \(i+1\) are either the old top \(k\) (if \(x\) is not larger than the smallest of them), or the old top \(k\) with its smallest member replaced by \(x\). The smallest member is the root, so the update does exactly this. After all \(m\) items the heap is the answer.

Why a min-heap, not a max-heap? At every step the question is "is the new item better than the worst one I am keeping, and if so, evict the worst". The worst is the minimum, so the minimum must be at the root. A max-heap of size \(k\) puts the best item at the root, which we never need; finding its minimum would take \(O(k)\). (A max-heap does work differently: heapify all \(m\) items in \(O(m)\) and pop \(k\) times, \(O(m + k \log m)\). But it needs all \(m\) items in memory, while the min-heap needs only \(k\) and works on a stream.)

Cost: \(m\) steps, each at most one heap operation on \(k\) items: \(O(m \log k)\) time, \(O(k)\) space.

Way 3: bucket sort by count. Counts are not arbitrary numbers: each is an integer between 1 and \(n\), since no item can occur more often than there are items. So use the count itself as an array index. Make buckets \(0, \dots, n\); put each item in bucket[count]; walk from bucket \(n\) down, collecting items until you have \(k\). Cost: \(O(n)\) to create the buckets, \(O(m)\) to fill them, \(O(n + m)\) to walk, so \(O(n + m) = O(n)\) time and space. This beats the \(\Omega(m \log m)\) bound because it is not a comparison sort: it never compares two counts, it indexes by them. The bound only applies to algorithms that learn about the order through comparisons. (The code sizes the bucket array by the largest count, which is never more than \(n\).)

Way 4: quickselect. We do not need the top \(k\) in order, only the set. Pick a random item's count \(p\) as the pivot and split the items into three groups: counts greater than \(p\), equal to \(p\), less than \(p\). If the "greater" group has at least \(k\) items, the answer lies inside it: recurse there only. If "greater" plus "equal" reaches \(k\), stop: the answer is "greater" plus enough of "equal". Otherwise keep "greater" and "equal" and look for the rest inside "less". Unlike quicksort, only one side is ever processed.

Why expected \(O(m)\). Let \(T(m)\) be the expected work on \(m\) items. The split costs \(m\). The pivot is equally likely to have any of the \(m\) positions \(i = 0, \dots, m-1\) in sorted order, and the recursion continues on at most \(\max(i, m-1-i)\) items. So

\[ T(m) \le m + \frac1m \sum_{i=0}^{m-1} T\big(\max(i,\, m-1-i)\big). \]

Claim: \(T(m) \le 4m\). By induction, assume \(T(j) \le 4j\) for \(j < m\). For even \(m\), each value \(j\) from \(m/2\) to \(m-1\) appears exactly twice as \(\max(i, m-1-i)\) (once for \(i = j\), once for \(i = m-1-j\)), so

\[ \sum_{i=0}^{m-1} \max(i, m-1-i) = 2 \sum_{j=m/2}^{m-1} j = \frac{3m^2}{4} - \frac{m}{2} \le \frac{3m^2}{4}, \]

For odd \(m = 2t+1\), the values \(t+1, \dots, 2t\) appear twice and \(t\) once, so the sum is \(2 \cdot \frac{t(3t+1)}{2} + t = 3t^2 + 2t \le 3t^2 + 3t + \frac34 = \frac{3m^2}{4}\). Then \(T(m) \le m + \frac{1}{m} \cdot 4 \cdot \frac{3m^2}{4} = m + 3m = 4m\). In words: on average a random pivot leaves at most three quarters of the items, and the passes form a geometric series \(m + \tfrac34 m + (\tfrac34)^2 m + \dots = 4m\). The worst case, a pivot at the extreme every time, is \(O(m^2)\); random pivots make it very unlikely. The three-way split matters: with a two-way split, many equal counts (1,105 types of Alice have count 1) would all land on one side and the recursion would barely shrink. If the \(k\) results must be in order, sort them afterwards for \(O(k \log k)\) more.

Confirm by measuring. Over 200 runs of randomized quickselect for the median of 10,000 shuffled numbers, the average number of comparisons was 3.32 × 10,000, under the bound of 4 × 10,000. (The exact expected constant for the median is known to be \(2 + 2\ln 2 \approx 3.39\).) On Alice, all four methods return the, and, to for \(k = 3\) and the same count multiset for every \(k\) tested.

3. Fixed-size sliding windows

The question. For every block of \(k\) consecutive items in a sequence of length \(n\), compute something: a sum, a count, or the block itself as a key. Example, 643 Maximum Average Subarray I: for [1, 12, −5, −6, 50, 3] and \(k = 4\), the best block is [12, −5, −6, 50], average 12.75.

First attempt. Recompute each of the \(n - k + 1\) windows from scratch: \(O(k)\) each, \(O(nk)\) total.

The key idea. Neighbouring windows share \(k - 1\) items. Moving the window one step right adds one item and removes one. If the quantity can be updated by "add the incoming item, undo the outgoing one" (sums, counts, a counter of items), each step costs \(O(1)\).

i = 3112−5−6503sum 2. First window, computed directly
i = 4112−5−6503sum 2 + 50 − 1 = 51. 50 enters, 1 leaves
i = 5112−5−6503sum 51 + 3 − 12 = 42. Best stays 51, average 12.75

Why it is correct. Invariant: after processing index \(i\), s equals the sum of nums[i−k+1 .. i]. It holds for \(i = k-1\) by direct computation. If it holds at \(i\), then adding nums[i+1] and subtracting nums[i−k+1] gives the sum of nums[i−k+2 .. i+1], so it holds at \(i+1\). The best of all these sums is the answer. Cost: \(O(n)\) time, \(O(1)\) space. The trick needs an undo step: a window maximum cannot be updated by "subtracting" the outgoing item, and needs a monotonic deque instead (Day 4, problem 239).

The window as a key. In 187 Repeated DNA Sequences the window (10 letters) is itself the key of a counter. Slicing out each 10-letter string costs \(O(k)\) per window. Instead, code each letter in 2 bits (A=0, C=1, G=2, T=3): a window is a 20-bit integer, and sliding is "shift left by 2, add the new letter, mask off the oldest 2 bits", \(O(1)\). Because the code is one-to-one (\(4^{10} = 2^{20}\) windows, \(2^{20}\) codes), equal codes mean equal strings, with no collision check needed. This is a rolling hash with no collisions.

The size-2 window. With \(k = 2\) the windows are the \(n - 1\) adjacent pairs, which Python produces with zip(w, w[1:]). Windows overlap: [a, a, a] contains two (a, a) windows, but only one merge fits, because the two windows share the middle a. So a pair's count is an upper bound on how many times it can be merged, not the exact number.

aaawindow 1aaawindow 2→ merge left to right:aaa
Two overlapping windows, one merge.

Where BPE uses it. Every training step counts the size-2 windows of every chunk into a hash map and takes the top-1. The pair with the most windows wins; ties go to the smaller pair, so the result does not depend on hash order.

4. UTF-8: a variable-length prefix code

The question. BPE starts from bytes, not characters. How does a string become bytes so that every string has exactly one byte sequence, and the bytes can be split back into characters without ambiguity?

Some definitions. Unicode assigns each character a number, its code point, written U+hex: a is U+0061, न is U+0928, 🙂 is U+1F642. Code points run from U+0000 to U+10FFFF, about 1.1 million values. An encoding turns a sequence of code points into bytes.

First attempt: fixed width. One byte per character (Latin-1) has only 256 values: it cannot write न. Four bytes per code point (UTF-32) can write everything, but English text becomes four times larger: Alice's 150,433 characters take 601,732 bytes, and three of every four bytes are zero.

The key idea: let common characters be short. UTF-8 uses 1 to 4 bytes. The first byte says how many bytes the character has, and every following byte is marked as a continuation:

BytesCode pointsPayload bitsFirst byteContinuation bytesExample
1U+0000 – U+007F70xxxxxxxnonea = 97
2U+0080 – U+07FF11110xxxxx10xxxxxxé = 195 169
3U+0800 – U+FFFF161110xxxx2 × 10xxxxxxन = 224 164 168
4U+10000 – U+10FFFF2111110xxx3 × 10xxxxxx🙂 = 240 159 153 130

By hand: न is U+0928. In binary, with 16 bits: 0000 1001 0010 1000. It needs the 3-byte form, which has slots for 4 + 6 + 6 bits. Split the bits that way: 0000 | 100100 | 101000. Fill the slots: 1110 0000 = 224, 10 100100 = 164, 10 101000 = 168. ASCII stays one byte, identical to ASCII, so Alice (150,433 characters, only 3,099 of them outside ASCII: curly quotes, dashes and one ù) is 156,630 bytes.

Why decoding is unambiguous. UTF-8 is a prefix code: no character's byte sequence is the beginning of another's. Two sequences with different first bytes differ immediately. Two with the same first byte have the same length, because the first byte fixes the length. So a decoder reading left to right always knows where the current character ends, without looking ahead.

Why it is self-synchronizing. The three kinds of byte are told apart by their top bits alone: 0 (a 1-byte character), 11 (the start of a longer one), 10 (a continuation). Dropped into the middle of a byte stream, or after a corrupted byte, a decoder skips continuation bytes (at most 3) until it reaches a start byte, and from there decodes correctly. One bad byte damages one character, not the rest of the text. A useful side effect: every byte of a multi-byte character is ≥ 128, so an ASCII byte such as a space or / never appears inside another character, and byte-level search for ASCII is always safe.

Validating. 393 UTF-8 Validation: is a list of bytes valid UTF-8? Scan once, keeping need, the number of continuation bytes the current character still owes. Invariant: before each byte, need is exactly that number. A start byte sets it (0 to 3, from its top bits), a continuation byte decrements it and is only legal when need > 0, and any other byte is invalid. At the end need must be 0, or the last character was cut off. Cost: \(O(n)\) time, \(O(1)\) space. Problem 393 checks only the bit patterns. A real decoder also rejects overlong forms (a character written with more bytes than needed: 192 128 is a 2-byte spelling of U+0000), surrogates U+D800–U+DFFF (reserved for UTF-16), and values above U+10FFFF. Overlong forms are a security issue: a filter that looks for the byte / would miss a 2-byte overlong /. The strict validator below handles all three and agrees with Python's own decoder on 50,000 random byte strings.

Confirm by measuring. In Alice, the fifth most common pair of adjacent bytes is 226 128, 3,098 times. It is not a pair of characters at all: it is the first two bytes shared by ’, ‘, “, ” and — (U+2018 to U+201D and U+2014 all start with these two bytes). A byte-level BPE will merge it early, creating a token that is part of a character.

Where BPE uses it. Byte-level BPE's base alphabet is the 256 byte values of UTF-8, which closes the vocabulary (Heaps' law above). The price: tokens can be fragments of characters, so decoding a slice of tokens can produce invalid UTF-8, and the decoder must handle it (errors="replace").

hashmap.py
class HashMap:
    """Separate chaining. Doubles when the load factor n / m would pass 1."""
    def __init__(self, m=8):
        self.buckets = [[] for _ in range(m)]
        self.n = 0

    def _bucket(self, key):
        return self.buckets[hash(key) % len(self.buckets)]

    def get(self, key, default=None):
        for k, v in self._bucket(key):
            if k == key:
                return v
        return default

    def set(self, key, value):
        b = self._bucket(key)
        for i, (k, _) in enumerate(b):
            if k == key:
                b[i] = (key, value)
                return
        b.append((key, value))
        self.n += 1
        if self.n > len(self.buckets):
            self._resize(2 * len(self.buckets))

    def _resize(self, m):
        old, self.buckets = self.buckets, [[] for _ in range(m)]
        for b in old:
            for k, v in b:
                self.buckets[hash(k) % m].append((k, v))

counts = HashMap()
for w in words:
    counts.set(w, counts.get(w, 0) + 1)
print(counts.get("alice"), counts.n, len(counts.buckets))   # 372 2559 4096

Python's string hash is randomized per process, so the bucket layout changes from run to run, but the counts do not.

topk.py · four ways
import heapq

def topk_sort(c, k):                      # O(m log m)
    return sorted(c, key=lambda x: -c[x])[:k]

def topk_heap(c, k):                      # O(m log k): keep the k best seen so far
    h = []                                # min-heap of (count, item); h[0] is the weakest kept
    for x, f in c.items():
        if len(h) < k:
            heapq.heappush(h, (f, x))
        elif f > h[0][0]:
            heapq.heapreplace(h, (f, x))  # evict the weakest, insert x
    return [x for f, x in sorted(h, reverse=True)]

def topk_bucket(c, k):                    # O(n + m): counts are integers in 1..n
    n = max(c.values())
    buckets = [[] for _ in range(n + 1)]
    for x, f in c.items():
        buckets[f].append(x)
    out = []
    for f in range(n, 0, -1):
        for x in buckets[f]:
            out.append(x)
            if len(out) == k:
                return out
    return out

def topk_quickselect(c, k):               # expected O(m)
    a = list(c.items())
    lo, hi = 0, len(a)                    # find the k largest counts in a[0:k]
    while hi - lo > 1:
        p = a[random.randrange(lo, hi)][1]
        big = [t for t in a[lo:hi] if t[1] > p]
        eq = [t for t in a[lo:hi] if t[1] == p]
        small = [t for t in a[lo:hi] if t[1] < p]
        a[lo:hi] = big + eq + small
        if k <= lo + len(big):
            hi = lo + len(big)
        elif k <= lo + len(big) + len(eq):
            break                         # the boundary falls inside the ties
        else:
            lo = lo + len(big) + len(eq)
    return [x for x, f in a[:k]]          # the top k, in no particular order

topk_heap pushes (count, item), so on equal counts it breaks ties by comparing items; the four methods may return different members of a tie, but always the same counts. topk_quickselect builds new lists for clarity; an in-place partition gives \(O(1)\) extra space.

class _Worse:                             # heap key for 692: "smaller" means "worse"
    def __init__(self, f, w): self.f, self.w = f, w
    def __lt__(self, o):                  # lower count is worse; on a tie, the later word is worse
        return self.f < o.f or (self.f == o.f and self.w > o.w)

def findRepeatedDnaSequences(s):          # 187: slide a 20-bit code, O(n)
    code = {"A": 0, "C": 1, "G": 2, "T": 3}
    mask, h = (1 << 20) - 1, 0
    seen, out = Counter(), []
    for i, ch in enumerate(s):
        h = ((h << 2) | code[ch]) & mask  # shift in 2 bits, drop the oldest 2
        if i >= 9:
            seen[h] += 1
            if seen[h] == 2:
                out.append(s[i - 9:i + 1])
    return out

def kgram_counts(seq, kmax):              # all k-grams, k = 1..kmax, one pass
    c = Counter()
    for i in range(len(seq)):
        for k in range(1, kmax + 1):
            if i + k > len(seq):
                break
            c[tuple(seq[i:i + k])] += 1
    return c

def valid_utf8_strict(data):              # also rejects overlong forms, surrogates, > U+10FFFF
    i, n = 0, len(data)
    while i < n:
        b = data[i]
        if b < 0x80:
            i += 1
            continue
        if b >> 5 == 0b110:   need, cp, low = 1, b & 0x1F, 0x80
        elif b >> 4 == 0b1110: need, cp, low = 2, b & 0x0F, 0x800
        elif b >> 3 == 0b11110: need, cp, low = 3, b & 0x07, 0x10000
        else: return False
        if i + need >= n:
            return False                  # truncated: not enough bytes left
        for j in range(1, need + 1):
            if data[i + j] >> 6 != 0b10: return False
            cp = cp << 6 | data[i + j] & 0x3F
        if cp < low or 0xD800 <= cp <= 0xDFFF or cp > 0x10FFFF:
            return False                  # overlong, surrogate, or out of range
        i += need + 1
    return True

Design · the pre-tokenization pattern

The question. BPE counts adjacent pairs. Adjacent inside what? Should a merge be allowed to join the last letter of one word to the space before the next?

First attempt: no boundaries. Count byte pairs over the raw text. On Alice, 4 of the 10 most frequent byte pairs are e␣, d␣, t␣ and ,␣: the end of one word glued to the space that starts the next. BPE would spend its first merges on them and go on to learn tokens like e␣t that straddle two words. It would also learn separate tokens for dog., dog, and dog!, splitting the counts of one word three ways.

The key idea. Before counting, cut the text into chunks with a regular expression, and count pairs only inside chunks. Merges then never cross a chunk boundary. The pattern is a decision made by hand about where token boundaries must fall. Training cannot undo it: a cut the pattern makes is permanent, and a cut it does not make may be learned or not.

To write such a pattern for every script, you need Unicode general categories. Every code point has one: L letter (subdivided into Lu upper case, Ll lower case, Lt title case, Lm modifier, Lo other, the last for scripts without case such as Devanagari), M mark, N number, P punctuation, S symbol, Z separator (spaces), C other (controls). In the regex module, \p{L} matches any letter, \p{N} any number, and so on. GPT-2's pattern:

So the chunks concatenate back to the original text exactly; the split loses nothing. The tests check this on all of Alice. Cost: one left-to-right scan. tiktoken's production patterns write the runs with possessive quantifiers (\p{L}++), which forbid backtracking into a run, keeping the scan linear.

A mark (category M) is a character that attaches to the character before it rather than standing alone: combining accents, and the vowel signs of Indic scripts. Take the Hindi word मैं ("I"). It is three code points:

मU+092E MA · LoैU+0948 VOWEL SIGN AI · MnंU+0902 ANUSVARA · Mn
A reader sees one word. GPT-2's \p{L}+ sees one letter followed by two non-letters.

GPT-2's ?\p{L}+ stops at the first mark, because Mn is not L. The marks then match the punctuation rule ?[^\s\p{L}\p{N}]+ as a separate chunk. Measured on the sentence मैं घर जा रहा हूँ ("I am going home"), 5 words:

GPT-2 cuts 4 of the 5 words, and since merges never cross chunks, BPE can never put them back together: मैं can never be one token, however much Hindi it trains on. This is part of the reason the Hindi sentence in the week reading costs 90 GPT-2 tokens and 22 with o200k_base. o200k_base adds \p{M} inside its letter classes, so a word is a run of letters and marks.

Digits

GPT-2's ?\p{N}+ lets a number of any length become one chunk, so BPE learns tokens for whichever numbers were frequent in training (years, round amounts), and two similar numbers can split in unrelated ways. cl100k_base and o200k_base cap number chunks at three digits with \p{N}{1,3}, so only 1,110 chunks of one to three ASCII digits exist and every one can be a token. They also drop the optional leading space before digits.

PatternSplit of I'll pay $1500 tomorrow!!
GPT-2I 'll ␣pay ␣$ 1500 ␣tomorrow !!
cl100k_baseI 'll ␣pay ␣$ 150 0 ␣tomorrow !!
o200k_baseI'll ␣pay ␣$ 150 0 ␣tomorrow !!

Grouping runs from the left: 1500 becomes 150 + 0, not 1 + 500 as thousands separators would suggest.

Where BPE uses it. The pattern runs first, on every string, in training and in encoding. Alice (the whole file, as the trainer reads it) becomes 37,404 chunks, only 3,218 of them distinct, and the trainer counts pairs once per distinct chunk, weighted by how often the chunk occurs (Day 4).

Solution
pattern with marks and digit groups
import regex

GPT2 = regex.compile(r"""'(?:[sdmt]|ll|ve|re)| ?\p{L}+| ?\p{N}+| ?[^\s\p{L}\p{N}]+|\s+(?!\S)|\s+""")
OURS = regex.compile(r"""'(?:[sdmt]|ll|ve|re)| ?[\p{L}\p{M}]+| ?\p{N}{1,3}| ?[^\s\p{L}\p{M}\p{N}]+|\s+(?!\S)|\s+""")

s = "मैं घर जा रहा हूँ, I paid 12345 rupees!"
print(GPT2.findall(s))
# ['म', 'ैं', ' घर', ' ज', 'ा', ' रह', 'ा', ' ह', 'ूँ,', ' I', ' paid', ' 12345', ' rupees', '!']
print(OURS.findall(s))
# ['मैं', ' घर', ' जा', ' रहा', ' हूँ', ',', ' I', ' paid', ' 123', '45', ' rupees', '!']

Two edits: add \p{M} to the letter class (and to the excluded set of the punctuation rule, or marks would also match there), and cap digits with {1,3}. Note that 12345 becomes 123 + 45, grouped from the left. Unlike o200k_base, this pattern keeps GPT-2's optional space before digits (␣123).
