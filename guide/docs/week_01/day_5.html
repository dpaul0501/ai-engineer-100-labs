DAY 5 · A BILLION USERSSystem DesignDSAMLDelivery

A billion users

Where we are. Everything so far ran on one laptop: ten thousand people, a hundred thousand friendships, all in memory. Day 1 counted mutual friends exactly and then as a MapReduce job, Day 2 kept the top k on one machine and on many, Day 3 saw the graph and split it across workers, and Day 4 replaced the hand-made score with a ranker trained on what people actually did. Today the box serves a billion people. The question is no longer "what is the right list?" but "how do we keep the right list ready, fresh enough and safe, for everyone, all the time?"

how big?→precompute or not→shard→cache→update→the tail→architecture→product
Part 1 builds the system, each step answering a problem the step before it uncovers. Part 2 decides what the system may show, and how the same design moves to other products.

Part 1 · Distributed algorithms and systems

System Design · 1 · estimate before you draw

The situation

An interviewer says: "A billion users." Before any boxes, find out what that number does to the code you already have. Which resource runs out first: storage, network, or the time a request may take?

The obvious attempt

"Run Day 1's suggest on request, on a bigger machine." To judge it you need the size of the graph and the work per request at a billion users. Nobody hands you those. You estimate them, with every input written down so anyone can change one and redo the arithmetic.

Now the arithmetic, one line at a time.

  • Friendships. Each friendship gives two people one friend each: 10⁹ × 200 / 2 = 100 billion friendships.
  • Storage for friend lists. Each friendship sits in two lists: 200 billion ids × 8 bytes = 1.6 TB. A server with 256 GB of memory holds 1.6 TB / 256 GB = 6.25 of these, so 7 servers hold one copy. Storage is not the problem.
  • Requests. 500 million active users × 2 loads = 10⁹ requests a day; 10⁹ / 86,400 = 11,574 per second on average, × 3 = 34,722 per second in the busiest hour.
  • Two-hop work per request. Reading every friend's list: 200 × 200 = 40,000 ids if everyone had 200 friends. Friends are more popular than average (Day 3 measured the heavy tail), so the real mean is 40,000 × 3.69 = 147,600 ids per request, from about 181 of the 1,000 shards.
  • Bandwidth at peak. 34,722 × 147,600 × 8 bytes = 41.0 GB of friend lists read every second, each request waiting on its slowest shard.

The conclusion the numbers force

Computing two hops online for everyone means 41 GB/s of random reads with every request fanned out to about 181 machines under a latency deadline. That is the design to avoid. Notice what the estimate also says: one nightly self-join of the whole graph emits about 10⁹ × 147,600 = 1.5 × 10¹⁴ rows, the same order as a full day of online scans (10⁹ requests × 147,600). Precomputing does not make the work smaller. It moves it to where it is cheap: sequential scans spread over hours, no deadline, hubs capped (Days 2 and 3), off the peak. The request then reads one key. So:

  • Precompute and refresh every user's list in batch, and serve it from a key-value store: 10⁹ × 100 × (8-byte id + 4-byte score) = 1.2 TB, about the size of the graph.
  • Compute online only for some: users with almost no friends (a new user's scan is tiny, because the work is the sum of their friends' degrees) and users whose own friendships just changed, each with a cap on how many entries it reads (the hub cap from Day 3).

In one line. Label every input, compute in round numbers, and let the estimate pick the design: reads per request break first, so precompute for everyone and compute online only where it is cheap or urgent.

The math, step by step: why the work per request is the mean squared degree

We want the average number of ids a request reads, and why it is 3.69 times "200 × 200".

Small numbers first. In the six-person example You have friends Ben (4 friends) and Chloe (4 friends). Your request reads 4 + 4 = 8 ids. Your own degree is 2, so "degree × degree" guesses 4: too low, because your friends are better connected than you.

The work of one request. For user u, read the list of each friend f, which has \(\deg(f)\) entries: \(W(u) = \sum_{f \in N(u)} \deg(f)\).

Average over users. Add \(W(u)\) over every u. Each person f appears once for each of their friends, that is \(\deg(f)\) times, contributing \(\deg(f)\) each time, so \(\sum_u W(u) = \sum_f \deg(f)^2\). Divide by the number of users n:

\[ \text{mean } W = \frac{1}{n}\sum_f \deg(f)^2 = E[d^2]. \]

Compare with the guess. The guess is \(E[d]^2\). On the week's graph \(E[d] = 20.0\) and \(E[d^2] = 1{,}476.8\), so the ratio is \(1{,}476.8 / 400 = 3.69\). It equals \(1 + \mathrm{Var}(d)/E[d]^2\): 1 only when everyone has the same degree, larger the wider the spread.

The batch join. The self-join on the middle person emits \(\deg(w)(\deg(w)-1)\) rows for each middle person w, about \(n\,E[d^2]\) in all: the same sum, which is why a nightly join costs about as many reads as one online scan per user.

System Design · 2 · batch precompute, online re-rank, and how stale is too stale

The situation

The estimate says precompute. Concretely, a nightly job runs Day 1's distributed mutual-friend count (emit friend pairs per middle user, reduce by pair), keeps each user's top few hundred candidates with Day 2's distributed top-k, scores them with Day 4's ranker, and writes each user's top N to a key-value store keyed by user id. At request time the server reads one key, drops anyone who became ineligible since the job ran, re-ranks the short list with a few fresh features, and returns ten.

Three readings. Counts change almost everywhere, lists much less: after 100 new friendships, 56.8% of users have some changed count but only 3.2% have a different top 10, because new friendships cluster on sociable people and most increments land below the tenth place. Rot grows steadily with growth: at 1.25% growth a quarter of the lists differ, but only 3.4% are materially worse; at 12.5% growth, 30.1% are. That one is not a ranking error, and the request-time filter removes it on every response whatever the store says (Part 2).

The trade-off

A refresh costs one full batch job; staleness grows with the time between refreshes. Pick the refresh interval from the table and the graph's growth rate: if the graph grows by at most 1.25% between nightly runs, a nightly job keeps materially stale lists at or below 3.4%. Between runs, the incremental updates of block 5 patch the users whose lists actually move, and the batch job becomes the safety net that resets any drift.

In one line. Precompute nightly into a key-value store, filter and re-rank online; measure staleness against graph growth and patch the few lists that move in between.

Interview takeaway. "Batch for everyone, one key read per request, a request-time eligibility filter, incremental patches for users whose friendships just changed. I would size the refresh interval from how fast lists go stale: on a replay, 1.25% graph growth leaves 3.4% of lists materially stale."

DSA · 3 · sharding by user id: mod N, consistent hashing, hot keys

The situation

Both stores, the graph (user → friends) and the suggestions (user → top N), are spread over many machines, called shards. Any server must find a user's shard from the id alone, without asking anyone. Adding a machine should move as few users as possible, because every moved row is copied while the service runs. And shards should be equally full.

The obvious attempt: hash mod N

Put user k on shard h(k) mod N, with h a well-mixed 64-bit hash. It needs no state and balances well: with 16 shards the fullest holds 1.08 times the mean. Now add one machine. Pretend keys 0 to 11 hash to themselves:

The math, step by step: why mod N moves N/(N+1) of the keys

Small numbers first. With N = 3, take hash values 0 to 11, a block of 3 × 4 = 12. A key stays when "value mod 3" equals "value mod 4": only 0, 1, 2. So 3 of 12 stay and 3/4 = N/(N+1) move.

In general. In a block of N(N+1) consecutive values, every pair (value mod N, value mod (N+1)) appears exactly once, because N and N+1 share no factor (the Chinese remainder theorem). A key stays when the two residues are equal: (0, 0), …, (N−1, N−1), which is N of the N(N+1) pairs. So 1/(N+1) stay and N/(N+1) move.

Doubling. "value mod 2N" equals "value mod N" exactly when value mod 2N is below N: half the time.

The idea: consistent hashing

Bend the hash range into a circle, the ring. Hash the servers onto the ring too. A key walks clockwise from its position and belongs to the first server point it meets. A new server takes over only the stretch of ring just before its points; every other key still meets the same server first.

Servers A, B, C at positions 10, 45, 75 (filled). Keys (squares) go clockwise to the next server: k1 (5) → A, k2 (20) → B, k3 (50) and k4 (60) → C, k5 (90) wraps to A. Add server D at 30 (hollow): only the shaded arc (10, 30] changes owner, so k2 moves from B to D and nothing else moves.

One point per server balances badly, because random gaps vary a lot. Give each server many points, its virtual nodes: a server's share becomes the sum of many small gaps, which evens out. Lookup is a binary search over the sorted points.

code/day_5/consistent_hash.py · a ring with virtual nodes and replica lookup
def h64(x):
    """splitmix64: a fast, well-mixed 64-bit hash of a non-negative integer."""
    z = (x + 0x9E3779B97F4A7C15) & MASK
    z = ((z ^ (z >> 30)) * 0xBF58476D1CE4E5B9) & MASK
    z = ((z ^ (z >> 27)) * 0x94D049BB133111EB) & MASK
    return z ^ (z >> 31)


class Ring:
    """Consistent hashing: each server owns v points; a key belongs to the next point clockwise."""
    def __init__(self, servers, v=100, salt=0):
        self.v, self.salt = v, salt
        self.points = []                                  # sorted (position, server)
        for s in servers:
            self.add(s)

    def _positions(self, s):
        base = h64(s ^ (self.salt << 32) ^ 0x632BE59BD9B4E019)   # never hash a server like a key
        return [h64((base + i) & MASK) for i in range(self.v)]

    def add(self, s):
        for p in self._positions(s):
            bisect.insort(self.points, (p, s))

    def remove(self, s):
        self.points = [(p, t) for p, t in self.points if t != s]

    def lookup(self, key):
        i = bisect.bisect_left(self.points, (h64(key), -1))
        return self.points[i % len(self.points)][1]       # wrap past the end

    def replicas(self, key, r):
        """The first r distinct servers clockwise: where copies of a hot key live."""
        i = bisect.bisect_left(self.points, (h64(key), -1))
        out = []
        while len(out) < r:
            s = self.points[i % len(self.points)][1]
            if s not in out:
                out.append(s)
            i += 1
        return out

    def arc_shares(self):
        share = {}
        pts = self.points
        for j, (p, s) in enumerate(pts):
            prev = pts[j - 1][0] if j else pts[-1][0] - (1 << 64)
            share[s] = share.get(s, 0) + (p - prev) / (1 << 64)
        return share

A pitfall the tests check: hash servers and keys from different inputs. If virtual node i were hashed exactly like user i, those users would sit on server points. The code salts the server id.

The math, step by step: a new server takes about K/(N+1) keys, all to itself, and V virtual nodes cut the spread to about 1/√V

Every mover goes to the new server. Old points stay where they were. A key's owner is the first point clockwise. After the addition that first point is either the same old point as before (no old point appeared in between) or a point of the new server. So a key keeps its owner or moves to the new server.

About K/(N+1) move. Treat all N+1 servers' points as random. For one key, each server is equally likely to own the first point clockwise, so the new one does with probability 1/(N+1). Over K keys: K/(N+1) on average; 10,000/17 = 588 users, against 602 measured.

One point per server is uneven. Put M random points on a ring of length 1. A gap is longer than x when the other M−1 points miss a stretch of length x: probability \((1-x)^{M-1}\). Then

\[ E[\text{gap}] = \int_0^1 (1-x)^{M-1}dx = \frac{1}{M}, \qquad E[\text{gap}^2] = \int_0^1 2x(1-x)^{M-1}dx = \frac{2}{M(M+1)}, \]

so std/mean is \(\sqrt{(M-1)/(M+1)}\), close to 1: the spread is as large as the share itself.

With V virtual nodes. Now \(M = NV\) and a server's load is the sum of V gaps. The gaps add to exactly 1, which forces a small negative covariance between any two of them, and working the variance of a sum of V gaps through gives

\[ \frac{\text{std}}{\text{mean}} = \sqrt{\frac{N-1}{NV+1}} \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt V}. \]

With N = 16 and V = 100: \(\sqrt{15/1601} = 0.097\), the "predicted" column.

Hot keys: hub users

Even shares of users are not even shares of traffic. In the graph store, a person's friend list is read once for each of their friends (every friend's two-hop scan reads it), and each read returns the whole list. So reads follow the degree and bytes follow the degree squared. Measured on the week's graph, if every user scanned two hops once: the top 1% of lists get 11.1% of the reads but 69.1% of the ids read; the hottest list is read 1,480 times, the median 15. A key that gets far more traffic than the rest is a hot key: its shard saturates while others idle, and adding shards does not help, because the key still lives on one shard.

The fix is replication of the hot set: copy the top 1% of lists to the next r distinct servers clockwise (Ring.replicas) and spread reads over the copies.

The suggestion store has no such problem: with requests proportional to activity and one key per request, its fullest shard is 1.22 times the mean, about the ring's own imbalance. Precomputing also removes the hot keys from the request path.

In one line. Consistent hashing with virtual nodes moves only the new server's share on growth and keeps shards even; hub lists are hot keys, so replicate them, and precompute so requests never read them.

Interview takeaway. "Shard both stores by user id on a consistent-hash ring with about 100 virtual nodes per server; adding a server then moves about 1/(N+1) of users, all to it. Hub friend lists are hot keys: replicate the top 1% and cap them in the batch job."

DSA · 4 · an LRU cache for hot users' suggestions

The situation

Each request reads one key from the suggestion store. Active people open the app many times a day, so the same keys come back again and again. A small cache in front of the store, on the serving machines, saves a network round trip for them.

The idea: least recently used

LRU keeps the most recently read entries and evicts the one unused for longest. It needs a hash map from key to entry, to find an entry at once, and a list in order of use, to move an entry to the front and drop the last one, also at once. This is LeetCode ★146.

code/day_5/lru.py · LeetCode 146 with OrderedDict (a hash map plus a recency order)
class LRUCache:
    """LeetCode 146: get and put in O(1). A hash map plus a recency order."""
    def __init__(self, capacity):
        self.cap = capacity
        self.d = OrderedDict()                   # least recently used first

    def get(self, key):
        if key not in self.d:
            return -1
        self.d.move_to_end(key)                  # now the most recent
        return self.d[key]

    def put(self, key, value):
        if key in self.d:
            self.d.move_to_end(key)
        self.d[key] = value
        if len(self.d) > self.cap:
            self.d.popitem(last=False)
Solution: the same cache with the doubly linked list written out
code/day_5/lru.py · LeetCode 146 from scratch, with sentinels
class Node:
    __slots__ = ("key", "val", "prev", "next")
    def __init__(self, key=0, val=0):
        self.key, self.val, self.prev, self.next = key, val, None, None


class LRUFromScratch:
    """The same cache with the doubly linked list written out; two sentinels avoid edge cases."""
    def __init__(self, capacity):
        self.cap, self.map = capacity, {}
        self.head, self.tail = Node(), Node()    # head.next = most recent, tail.prev = least
        self.head.next, self.tail.prev = self.tail, self.head

    def _unlink(self, x):
        x.prev.next, x.next.prev = x.next, x.prev

    def _push_front(self, x):
        x.prev, x.next = self.head, self.head.next
        self.head.next.prev = x
        self.head.next = x

    def get(self, key):
        x = self.map.get(key)
        if x is None:
            return -1
        self._unlink(x); self._push_front(x)
        return x.val

    def put(self, key, value):
        x = self.map.get(key)
        if x:
            x.val = value
            self._unlink(x); self._push_front(x)
            return
        if len(self.map) == self.cap:
            lru = self.tail.prev
            self._unlink(lru); del self.map[lru.key]
        x = Node(key, value)
        self.map[key] = x; self._push_front(x)

Why the map stores the node. Moving a key to the front needs its place in the list. Storing the node gives that place at once; storing the value would force a search.

In one line. An LRU cache is a hash map plus a recency list, O(1) per operation; it wins on recency, a pinned set wins on fixed popularity, and the eligibility filter runs after either.

System Design · 5 · incremental updates from the stream of new friendships

The situation

Between nightly runs, friendships keep forming. Rerunning the 9.5-million-row join after each one is absurd. Which stored counts does one new friendship change?

Work it by hand

In the running example, Dev and Farid become friends. Dev's only friend was Ben; Farid's only friend was Chloe. A count changes only where a new two-hop path through the new friendship appears:

new pathBen–Dev–FaridBen and Farid gain a mutual friend: 2 (Chloe and Dev) instead of 1.
new pathChloe–Farid–DevChloe and Dev gain one: 2 instead of 1.
unchangedYou EshaNo new path passes through them. Your list is still Esha (2), Dev (1), Farid (1).

In general, when a and b connect, every friend of a gains b as a candidate (one more mutual friend), and every friend of b gains a. The users touched are a, b and their friends; nobody else.

code/day_5/incremental.py · update the stored counts for one new friendship
def add_friendship(adj, m, u, v, owned=None):
    """Update counts in place when u and v become friends. Returns the (row, column) pairs changed.
    If `owned` is given, only those rows are stored here (one shard's users)."""
    changed = []
    for x in adj[u]:                            # new two-hop paths x - u - v
        changed += [(x, v), (v, x)]
    for y in adj[v]:                            # new two-hop paths u - v - y
        changed += [(u, y), (y, u)]
    for a, b in changed:
        if owned is None or a in owned:
            m[a][b] += 1
    adj[u].add(v); adj[v].add(u)
    return changed

The tests check the Dev–Farid case and, on 30 random graphs, that replaying friendships incrementally gives exactly the counts of a full recompute.

A check on 500 sampled users' rows confirmed that the maintained counts and top 10s match a full recompute at the end.

The math, step by step: exactly which counts one new friendship changes

Write the count as a sum over middle people. With \([w \in N(x)]\) equal to 1 when w is a friend of x:

\[ m(x,y) = \sum_{w} [\,w \in N(x)\,]\,[\,w \in N(y)\,]. \]

What the friendship changes. Exactly two memberships: b joins \(N(a)\) and a joins \(N(b)\). A term can change only if one of its brackets is one of those.

  • The term with w = a now has \([a \in N(b)] = 1\), so \(m(b, y)\) rises by 1 for every friend y of a: the paths b – a – y.
  • Symmetrically \(m(a, y)\) rises by 1 for every friend y of b.
  • \(m(a, b)\) itself would need someone to be their own friend, so it does not change (but a and b leave each other's lists as friends).

Count them. \(\deg(a) + \deg(b)\) pairs, degrees before the friendship. Dev and Farid: 1 + 1 = 2 pairs.

Expected cost. An endpoint of a random new friendship is picked in proportion to degree, so its expected degree is \(E[d^2]/E[d]\), not \(E[d]\); the update costs about \(2E[d^2]/E[d]\) pairs. That is why the mean (126.2) is far above twice the mean degree of the past graph (2 × 16.0).

The top 10 under +1 updates. Counts in an unaffected row only rise. A candidate outside the top 10 whose count did not change cannot pass the tenth, whose count did not fall; so after a +1, only the incremented candidate can enter. Compare it with the tenth and you are done.

Versioned keys and snapshots

Two writers now touch the suggestion store: the nightly batch, which rewrites every row, and the incremental path, which patches a few. Readers must never see half a batch, and a bad batch must be undone in one step. The idea is the one behind LeetCode 981 and 1146: keep values with a version, read "as of" a version. The batch writes version v+1 next to v, then one pointer flip makes it live; rollback flips back; old versions are garbage-collected.

code/day_5/versions.py · publish a whole batch with one flip, roll it back with another
class SuggestionStore:
    """Keys are (version, user). A batch writes a whole new version, then one pointer flip makes
    it live; readers never see half a batch, and a bad batch is undone by flipping back."""
    def __init__(self, keep=3):
        self.data, self.live, self.keep = {}, None, keep
        self.versions = []

    def write_batch(self, version, lists):
        for user, top in lists.items():
            self.data[(version, user)] = top
        self.versions.append(version)

    def publish(self, version):
        assert version in self.versions
        self.live = version                                   # the atomic step
        old = self.versions[:-self.keep]                      # garbage-collect old versions
        self.versions = self.versions[-self.keep:]
        self.data = {k: v for k, v in self.data.items() if k[0] not in old}

    def rollback(self):
        i = self.versions.index(self.live)
        assert i > 0, "no older version kept"
        self.live = self.versions[i - 1]

    def get(self, user):
        return self.data.get((self.live, user), [])
Solution: LeetCode 981 and 1146, the same idea in miniature
code/day_5/versions.py · values with versions, read as of a time or a snapshot
class TimeMap:
    """LeetCode 981. Timestamps arrive increasing per key, so each key's list stays sorted."""
    def __init__(self):
        self.times, self.vals = defaultdict(list), defaultdict(list)

    def set(self, key, value, timestamp):
        self.times[key].append(timestamp)
        self.vals[key].append(value)

    def get(self, key, timestamp):
        i = bisect.bisect_right(self.times[key], timestamp)   # first time > timestamp
        return self.vals[key][i - 1] if i else ""


class SnapshotArray:
    """LeetCode 1146. Each index keeps (snap_id, value) pairs; a snapshot costs O(1)."""
    def __init__(self, length):
        self.hist = [[(-1, 0)] for _ in range(length)]
        self.snap_id = 0

    def set(self, index, val):
        h = self.hist[index]
        if h[-1][0] == self.snap_id:
            h[-1] = (self.snap_id, val)                       # overwrite within the same snapshot
        else:
            h.append((self.snap_id, val))

    def snap(self):
        self.snap_id += 1
        return self.snap_id - 1

    def get(self, index, snap_id):
        h = self.hist[index]
        i = bisect.bisect_right(h, (snap_id, float("inf")))
        return h[i - 1][1]

Both store a sorted history per key and answer "the value as of t" with a binary search. 1146 keeps a snapshot O(1) by recording only the indices that changed.

In one line. A friendship a–b changes only the rows of a, b and their friends, deg(a) + deg(b) increments; write to the store only when a top 10 moves, and publish batches as versions so a reader never sees half of one.

Interview takeaway. "New friendships go on a stream; a consumer increments the rows of the endpoints' friends, about 126 pairs per friendship on a replay, and rewrites a user's list only when the top 10 changes, 3% of the time. Hubs are capped or deferred to the nightly batch, which writes a new version and flips a pointer."

System Design · 6 · fan-out, tail latency, hedged requests and Little's law

The situation

Some requests still fan out: the online path for new users, a re-rank that fetches fresh features from a few shards, and, in the design we rejected, a full two-hop scan over about 181 shards. A request that waits for all its calls is as slow as the slowest one.

The obvious answer, and why it is wrong

"Each shard is slow 1% of the time, so requests are slow 1% of the time." That holds for one call. With s independent calls each slow with probability p, the request is fast only if every call is fast:

\[ P(\text{request slow}) = 1 - (1-p)^s . \]

To measure it, a stated hypothetical latency model: each call takes a lognormal time with median 2 ms, and 1% of calls hit a 50 ms stall (a pause, a busy disk). One call then has p50 2.01 ms, p95 3.38 ms, p99 6.29 ms. Simulate 100,000 requests at each fan-out:

code/day_5/tail.py · requests that wait for s calls, with optional hedging
def call_times(size):
    t = 2.0 * np.exp(0.3 * rng.standard_normal(size))
    return t + STALL * (rng.random(size) < P_SLOW)


def request_times(s, n=100_000, hedge_at=None):
    """A request waits for all s calls. With hedging, a call not back by `hedge_at` ms gets a
    second copy sent to another replica; the first answer wins."""
    t = call_times((n, s))
    extra = 0.0
    if hedge_at is not None:
        late = t > hedge_at
        backup = hedge_at + call_times((n, s))
        t = np.where(late, np.minimum(t, backup), t)
        extra = late.mean()                      # share of calls that sent a second copy
    return t.max(axis=1), extra


def p_any_slow(p, s):
    return 1 - (1 - p) ** s

At 100 shards, 63.3% of requests are slow, so even the median request takes 51.72 ms, although each machine is fine 99% of the time. A request's p99 is set by its backends' p99.99.

Hedged requests

If a call has not answered by its p95 time (3.38 ms here), send a copy to another replica and take whichever answers first. A call is now slow only if both copies stall, about \(p^2\). Measured: at 100 shards the slow share falls from 0.633 to 0.0098 and the p99 from 54.00 ms to 8.93 ms, for 5.0% extra calls. At 181 shards the hedged slow share is 1.83%, still above 1%, so the p99 stays at 51.92 ms: hedging shrinks p, but past a point you must cut s. That is the deeper reason for precomputing: a precomputed request reads one key from one shard.

The levers, from the formula: cut s (precompute, batch calls by shard, cap hubs), cut p (hedge, keep queues short), or stop waiting (a deadline, then rank with what arrived; the eligibility filter is never skipped).

Little's law: how many requests are in flight

To size thread pools, connections and memory you need the number of requests inside the system at once. In any stable system, the average number inside equals the arrival rate times the average time each one stays: L = λW. A coffee shop where 2 customers arrive a minute and each stays 5 minutes holds 10 on average.

Worked example. At the peak of 34,722 requests per second and a hypothetical 50 ms per request: 34,722 × 0.050 = 1,736 requests in flight. If each fanned out to 181 shards, storage would hold 1,736 × 181 = 314,216 outstanding calls; precomputed, it holds 1,736. If one server can hold 100 requests in flight, the fleet needs at least 1,736 / 100 → 18 servers before headroom, and a slower tail raises W, which raises the concurrency the fleet must absorb. Checked by simulation: a single-server queue with random arrivals at rate 0.8 and service rate 1 measured arrival rate 0.8010 and mean time inside 5.040, so λW = 4.037, against 4.030 for the number inside measured directly at random moments.

The math, step by step: 1 − (1 − p)s, hedging, and why L = λW

Fan-out. "Request fast" means "call 1 fast and … call s fast". For independent calls the probability of all is the product, \((1-p)^s\). With p = 0.01 and s = 100: \(0.99^{100} = 0.366\), so 0.634 slow. Turned around: for 99% of 100-call requests to be fast, each call needs \((1-p) \ge 0.99^{1/100} = 0.9999\).

Hedging. A hedged call is slow only if both copies are slow, \(p^2 = 0.0001\) when independent: \(1 - 0.9999^{100} = 0.0100\), as measured (0.0098). At s = 181: \(1 - 0.9999^{181} = 0.0179\), as measured (0.0183).

Little's law. Watch the system for a long time T and draw N(t), the number inside. Its area is \(L \cdot T\) by definition of the time-average L. Counted item by item, each item raises the curve by 1 for as long as it stays, so the area is also the sum of stay times, about \(\lambda T \cdot W\). Equate and divide by T: \(L = \lambda W\). Nothing about the distribution of arrivals or service times was used, only that the system is stable.

In one line. A request waiting on s calls is slow with probability 1 − (1 − p)s: cut s, hedge to shrink p, or stop waiting; Little's law turns latency into the concurrency you must provision.

ML · 7 · training–serving skew

Day 4's ranker is valid only for features computed exactly as in training. The design above computes features in two places: the nightly job (from last night's snapshot) and the request path (fresh re-rank). If they disagree, the model sees inputs at serving time it never saw in training, and its offline numbers stop predicting what users get. That is training–serving skew.

Measured on the replay. For each stored suggestion, compare its mutual-friend count as the batch computed it with the live count: after 1,000 new friendships (1.25% growth) 2.4% of the feature values differ, after 10,000 (12.5%) 18.6%. A ranker trained on batch features but served live ones, or the reverse, is scoring different inputs.

  • One code path. The same feature function runs in the batch job and the server, so a change cannot reach one and not the other.
  • Log the served features. Write the exact feature values used for each impression into the impression log, and train the next model on those logs, not on features recomputed later. That also removes the leak Day 4 warned about: a feature recomputed today includes friendships formed after the label.
  • Monitor the gap. Compare distributions of logged online features with the batch ones every day; a jump means a pipeline changed.

In one line. Compute features with one code path, log what was served, and train on the log.

System Design · 9 · rules on every response

The situation

A ranking can be a little wrong and still useful: a so-so suggestion costs a glance. Some mistakes are different. They break trust or hurt someone. Those become hard rules checked on every response, in a small step after the ranker, on fresh data, so they hold even when the store is stale, the cache is old, or the model is replaced.

  • Never yourself or a current friend. Stale lists produce this (1.1% of users at 1.25% growth, measured above).
  • Never a blocked person, in either direction, and never someone the viewer hid from their list.
  • Never someone with a request already pending, either way.
  • Minors and privacy settings. Respect "don't suggest me". An illustrative age rule: never suggest a minor to an adult (or the reverse) without a strong shared context such as a school.
  • Contact-import consent. A candidate that came from an uploaded address book is used only if the uploader agreed to that use.
code/day_5/rules.py · hard filters, applied after ranking
def eligible(w, u, v, source):
    """Every rule is a hard filter; returns the reason v is dropped, or None if v may be shown."""
    g = lambda d, x: d.get(x, set())
    if v == u or v in g(w.friends, u):
        return "self or friend"
    if v in g(w.blocked, u) or u in g(w.blocked, v):
        return "blocked (either direction)"
    if v in g(w.dismissed, u):
        return "hidden by the viewer"
    if (u, v) in w.requested or (v, u) in w.requested:
        return "request already pending"
    if v in w.opted_out:
        return "opted out of suggestions"
    if (u in w.minors) != (v in w.minors) and not (g(w.schools, u) & g(w.schools, v)):
        return "minor and adult with no shared school"
    if source == "contacts" and u not in w.contacts_consent:
        return "contact import without consent"
    return None


def final_check(w, u, ranked, k=10):
    """ranked: (candidate, source) pairs from the store, best first. Keeps the first k eligible."""
    return [v for v, src in ranked if eligible(w, u, v, src) is None][:k]

On the six people. Your stored list is Esha, Dev, Farid. Farid blocks you: he is dropped. You send Dev a request: he is dropped. Esha is marked a minor with no shared school: she is dropped, until a shared school group is added. Each step is one assertion in rules.py.

Explaining a suggestion, and its privacy risk

"12 mutual friends" helps people decide. Naming them helps more, and leaks more: "Ben and Chloe are friends with Esha" reveals two friendships. If Chloe hides her friend list, the reason may name only Ben ("Ben and 1 more"); if Esha hides hers, show no reason at all. The ranker may still use the count; the display may not.

code/day_5/rules.py · name only what nobody chose to hide
def reason(w, u, v):
    """'Ben and Chloe are mutual friends': naming f reveals the friendship f-v, so only name
    people who do not hide their list, and say nothing if v hides theirs."""
    if v in w.hides_list:
        return ""
    names = sorted(f for f in w.friends[u] & w.friends[v] if f not in w.hides_list)
    hidden = len(w.friends[u] & w.friends[v]) - len(names)
    if not names:
        return f"{hidden} mutual friends" if hidden else ""
    return ", ".join(map(str, names)) + (f" and {hidden} more" if hidden else "")

Fatigue and notifications

The same face every day stops working, and notifications are expensive attention. Rotate shown candidates (drop someone after a few impressions without action), cap suggestion notifications per user per week, and never notify about the same person twice. The cap is a guardrail metric in every A/B test.

code/day_5/rules.py · at most cap notifications per window, never the same person twice
class NotificationCap:
    """At most `cap` suggestion notifications per user per window, never the same person twice."""
    def __init__(self, cap=2):
        self.cap, self.sent = cap, {}

    def allow(self, u, v, window):
        seen = self.sent.setdefault(u, {})
        if v in seen or sum(1 for t in seen.values() if t == window) >= self.cap:
            return False
        seen[v] = window
        return True

Fair exposure

Ranking by mutual friends favours the already-popular, since hubs are everyone's friend of a friend. Measured on last night's graph with every user's top 10 by mutual count: the top 1% of users by degree take 50.1% of all shown slots, while they are only 10.8% of the endpoints of future friendships. code/day_5/exposure.py · fill lists in order, skipping anyone already shown cap times
def capped_lists(cands, order, cap, k=10):
    """Fill lists in `order`; a person already shown `cap` times this round is skipped."""
    shown, out = Counter(), {}
    for u in order:
        lst = []
        for v in cands[u]:
            if shown[v] < cap:
                lst.append(v); shown[v] += 1
                if len(lst) == k:
                    break
        out[u] = lst
    return out, shown

Cold start

A new user has no friends, so two hops find nothing. Among the week's 71 users with at most 2 friends last night, the median has 28 two-hop candidates; of their 129 future friendships, two hops find 18.6%, a shared group finds 58.9%, and either finds 70.5% (a small sample). So onboarding signals carry the cold start: groups and schools chosen at sign-up, consented contacts, city. Compute these users online, cheaply, until the next batch includes them.

In one line. Hard rules run last on fresh data; reasons reveal only what nobody hid; exposure, fatigue and cold start are product choices with measured costs.

System Design · 10 · the same design in other products

The skeleton (candidates → features → ranker → rules, precomputed and patched) carries over. What changes is the target, the candidate sources, the signals and the rules.

  • A directed follow graph (follow suggestions). Following is one-sided: no acceptance step, so the label is "follows and keeps following", not "request accepted". Candidates come from who the people you follow follow, which is dominated by a few very popular accounts, so the hot-key and fair-exposure problems are sharper. Rules: no consent from the followed side, but blocking and muting still apply. LeetCode 355 is a toy version of the same graph.
  • A professional network. Signals change: shared workplaces, schools, industries, overlapping dates. Recruiters and salespeople send many requests to strangers, which skews the logs: an accepted request means less, and the ranker must not learn to push everyone towards recruiters. Rules: respect "open to connect" settings; never reveal a job search to a current employer.
  • People to work with, inside a company. Candidates from the org chart (same team, manager's peers) and a collaboration graph (shared documents, meetings, code reviews). The target is a useful working contact, not a friendship. Privacy is the hard part: collaboration data is sensitive, so suggestions must not reveal who meets whom, and the company's own policies govern which signals may be used at all.
