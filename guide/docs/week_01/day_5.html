DAY 5 · RANKING WITH MLMLDSASystem Design

From counting mutual friends to a model that ranks them

Where we are in the design. Days 1–4 built the left half of the pipeline: a candidate set for each user (friends of friends, made cheap and approximate where it is safe). Today builds the features → ranking boxes and then closes the week. The ML block turns "who will become friends?" into a supervised learning problem, derives the features and the model, and measures them honestly on the week's graph. The Algorithms block merges ranked lists from several shards and sources with a heap. The Design block assembles the full serving architecture, where every box answers a problem found earlier in the week, and shows how to test a new ranker on real users.

candidate generation→features→ranking→filtering and top-k→serving
Today's boxes are highlighted. The ranker's job: among about 800 candidates per user, put the few who will actually connect into the 10 slots shown.
ML · link prediction, from first principles

1 · The question: predicting friendships that do not exist yet

The question. At some moment t we know the friendship graph so far. For a user u, rank u's candidates by how likely each is to become u's friend after t. This is link prediction: the graph is the input, a future edge is the thing to predict. On the week's graph, the first 80,000 friendships are the past and the last 20,000 are the future (temporal_split in ref.py).

First attempt: hide random edges. A common recipe takes the whole graph, hides a random 20% of its edges, and asks the model to recover them. On the week's graph that recipe looks wonderful: ranking by mutual-friend count recovers 14.2% of the hidden friendships in each user's top 10 (95% CI 13.7–14.7%), and 62.5% of them have at least one mutual friend. The honest temporal split gives 5.2% and 48.4%. The random split overstates recall by 2.7×.

The reason is leakage from the future. Suppose u–v formed early and was hidden. Later, a friend x of u met v through u, so the edge x–v formed. That later edge is visible in the random split, and it makes x a mutual friend of u and v. The evidence for the hidden edge was caused by the hidden edge. At serving time this cannot happen: nothing after "now" is known.

2 · The key idea: two time cuts, and a precise population

Definitions. Order the friendships by formation time. A cut at position c splits them into a feature graph (edges before c) and a label window (edges after it). A pair (u, v) is a positive if it is not an edge of the feature graph and becomes one in the label window. A model needs two cuts: one to learn from, one to be judged on.

trainedges 0–59,999 · features60,000–79,999 · labels80,000–99,999 · unseenfit the model's weights
testedges 0–79,999 · features80,000–99,999 · labelsmeasure it, exactly as it would be served
The model never sees a label from the test window, and the test features never use an edge from it. Both cuts keep the order of time.

The evaluation population, precisely. These choices decide every number below, so they are stated once:

  • Users: every user with at least one future friendship: 9,357 users (5 of them had no past friend at all). Each future friendship is counted from both ends, 40,000 (user, future friend) pairs in total.
  • Candidates: C(u) = the two-hop candidates of u in the past graph (friends of friends, minus u and u's friends), the set of Days 1–4. On average 829 per user (median 533, maximum 7,790); 7,757,153 scored pairs in all.
  • Positives: C(u) ∩ F(u), where F(u) is u's future friends: 21,290 pairs, a base rate of 0.274%.
  • Misses: a future friend outside C(u) can never be ranked. It stays in the denominator of recall and counts as a miss. Only 53.2% of the 40,000 future (user, friend) pairs are inside the candidate set; averaged per user, the share is 48.4% (95% CI 47.8–49.1%). That is the ceiling: no ranker over two-hop candidates can reach a higher mean recall.

Why this is the right test. At serving time the system knows the graph up to "now", scores the candidates it generated, and shows 10. The test cut reproduces exactly that: features from edges 0–79,999 only, candidates from the same graph, and success measured on what happened next. Any quantity computed this way is an estimate of what the deployed ranker would have achieved over that window.

3 · Negatives, and their bias

A classifier needs negatives: pairs that did not connect. Which ones you take changes what is learned and what is measured.

Random users are too easy. Take the same 21,290 positives and, as negatives, 20 random non-friends per user. 91.7% of those random negatives have no mutual friend at all, while every positive has at least one. Counting mutual friends then scores an AUC of 0.969 (AUC is defined in §8; 1 is perfect, 0.5 is chance). Against negatives drawn from the candidate set, the pairs the ranker actually has to separate, the same score reaches only 0.620 (Adamic–Adar, introduced next: 0.984 against 0.805). Random negatives test whether a model can tell a friend of a friend from a stranger, which candidate generation already did.

Negatives must come from the candidate set, and in training there are too many: 4,541,304 candidate pairs in the training window for 18,322 positives. We keep every positive and each negative independently with probability r = 0.05, giving 244,152 training rows with 7.5% positives. This makes training fast but shifts the model's probabilities upward; §7 derives the exact correction and §11 measures it.

Negatives are "not yet", not "never". A pair that did not connect in the window may connect later, or may never have been offered the chance. That second problem is exposure bias, and it only appears once a ranker is live (§12).

4 · Features: four ways to score a mutual friend

The question. Two candidates each share two mutual friends with you. Which is more likely to become your friend? Counting says they tie. The example below, worked by hand, shows why they should not.

YOU's friends are A (2 friends), B (3) and H, a hub with 6. X and Y both have two mutual friends with YOU, so counting ties them (each candidate is labelled with its mutual friends and scores). Y's evidence comes partly from H, who knows many people; X's comes from two people with small circles.

Definitions. Write \(N(x)\) for the friends of x and \(d_x = |N(x)|\) for its degree. For a candidate pair (u, v):

\[ \text{CN}(u,v) = |N(u)\cap N(v)|, \qquad \text{J}(u,v) = \frac{|N(u)\cap N(v)|}{|N(u)\cup N(v)|}, \qquad \text{AA}(u,v) = \sum_{z \in N(u)\cap N(v)} \frac{1}{\log d_z}, \qquad \text{RA}(u,v) = \sum_{z \in N(u)\cap N(v)} \frac{1}{d_z}. \]

CN is common neighbours (Day 1's mutual-friend count). Jaccard (Day 4) divides by the size of the union, \( d_u + d_v - \text{CN} \). Adamic–Adar (AA) and resource allocation (RA) weight each mutual friend z by a decreasing function of z's degree. Every z in a candidate pair knows both u and v, so \(d_z \ge 2\) and \(\log d_z > 0\). By hand, for YOU: X has Jaccard 2/|{A, B, H}| = 0.67, AA = 1/ln 2 + 1/ln 3 = 1.443 + 0.910 = 2.35, RA = 1/2 + 1/3 = 0.83. Y has the same CN and Jaccard (N(Y) = {B, H}), but AA = 1/ln 3 + 1/ln 6 = 0.910 + 0.558 = 1.47 and RA = 1/3 + 1/6 = 0.50. AA and RA break the tie in X's favour.

5 · Why a mutual friend with many friends is weak evidence

First attempt: every mutual friend counts 1. That is CN. It treats an introduction by a friend with 2 friends and by a celebrity with 1,480 friends as equally telling. Day 2 showed why that matters here: your friends are biased towards hubs (the mean degree of a friend is 73.8, against 20 for a user), so many candidates are reached only through a hub.

The key idea, derived from a model of how friendships close. Model one way friendships form: u picks one of its friends z uniformly at random (probability \(1/d_u\)), and z introduces u to one of z's other friends uniformly at random (probability \(1/(d_z-1)\), since z will not introduce u to u). The introduction reaches v through z only if z is a mutual friend. The paths through different mutual friends are disjoint events, so their probabilities add:

\[ P(u \text{ is introduced to } v) \;=\; \sum_{z \in N(u)\cap N(v)} \frac{1}{d_u}\cdot\frac{1}{d_z - 1} \;=\; \frac{1}{d_u}\sum_{z \in N(u)\cap N(v)} \frac{1}{d_z-1}. \]

For one user u, the factor \(1/d_u\) is the same for every candidate, so the ranking is by \( \sum_z 1/(d_z - 1) \): resource allocation, up to the −1. Check it on the example: X gets (1/3)(1/1 + 1/2) = 0.50, Y gets (1/3)(1/2 + 1/5) = 0.23, and Z, P, Q, R get (1/3)(1/5) = 0.067 each. They add up to 0.50 + 0.23 + 4 × 0.067 = 1, as they must here, because every friend-of-friend of YOU is a candidate. A mutual friend's weight falls like 1/d because a popular friend spreads one unit of attention over many people. The week's generator makes 45% of its friendships with exactly this kind of step, so the model is not arbitrary for this graph.

Where 1/log d comes from. Adamic and Adar (2003) built their score for a different setting: two web pages that share a rare feature are more likely related than two that share a common one, so they weighted each shared feature by 1/log(its frequency). Applied to graphs, the shared "feature" is a mutual friend and its frequency is its degree. 1/log d falls far more slowly than 1/d: going from d = 5 to d = 921 divides the RA weight by 184 but the AA weight only by 4.2. Both discount hubs; they disagree on how much. That is a question for data.

6 · The other features, and one that hurts

  • Shared groups \( |G(u)\cap G(v)| \): 35% of the graph's friendships form inside a group, so this should carry signal that the friendship graph cannot see.
  • Same city (0 or 1). In this synthetic graph city plays no role in how friendships form, so a good model should learn a weight near zero. It is a useful honesty check.
  • Degrees \(\log(1+d_u)\), \(\log(1+d_v)\). Sociable users make more friendships in every window, both as the one who reaches out and as the one reached (Day 2's preferential attachment).

Why Jaccard does worse than chance here. Jaccard's denominator \(d_u + d_v - \text{CN}\) grows with the candidate's degree, so it pushes sociable candidates down. But sociable candidates are exactly the ones who make new friends: ranking the candidates by their degree alone already gives an AUC of 0.579. Jaccard measures how similar two neighbourhoods are, not how likely a new edge is, and here those point in opposite directions: its AUC on the candidates is 0.417, below the 0.5 of a coin flip (§10).

Cost. CN, AA and RA are accumulated in the same scan that generates the candidates: for each friend z of u and each friend v of z, add 1, 1/log dz and 1/dz. That is O(W(u)) time, Day 2's work per request (mean 1,477 adjacency reads on the full graph), and O(|C(u)|) space. Jaccard then costs O(1) per candidate from CN and the two degrees. Shared groups cost O(|G(u)| + |G(v)|) per candidate with a hash set of u's groups.

ranker/features.py · eight features per candidate, in one two-hop scan
FEATURES = ["log_cn", "jaccard", "log_aa", "log_ra", "shared_groups", "same_city", "log_du", "log_dv"]

def pair_features(adj, groups, city, u):
    """Two-hop candidates of u in the graph adj, and one feature row per candidate."""
    cn, aa, ra = Counter(), Counter(), Counter()
    for z in adj[u]:                           # z = a mutual friend of u and v
        dz = len(adj[z])                       # dz >= 2: z knows u and v
        for v in adj[z]:
            if v != u and v not in adj[u]:
                cn[v] += 1
                aa[v] += 1.0 / math.log(dz)
                ra[v] += 1.0 / dz
    cands = sorted(cn)
    du, gu = len(adj[u]), set(groups[u])
    X = np.empty((len(cands), len(FEATURES)))
    for i, v in enumerate(cands):
        dv = len(adj[v])
        X[i] = (math.log1p(cn[v]), cn[v] / (du + dv - cn[v]),  # |N(u) ∪ N(v)| = du + dv - cn
                math.log1p(aa[v]), math.log1p(ra[v]),
                len(gu.intersection(groups[v])), city[u] == city[v],
                math.log1p(du), math.log1p(dv))
    return cands, X

The tests compare every row against a brute-force version (set intersections, one pair at a time) on the worked example and on 30 random graphs.

7 · Logistic regression, derived

The question. We have eight numbers per candidate. How should they be combined into one score? Choosing weights by hand does not scale past two or three features, and the features overlap (CN, AA and RA measure nearly the same thing).

First attempt: linear regression on 0/1 labels. Fit \( \hat y = w\cdot x + b \) by least squares. The predictions are not probabilities (they go below 0 and above 1), and squared error punishes a confident correct prediction (predict 3 for a positive: error 4) as if it were wrong. We want a model whose output is a probability and a loss that matches a yes/no outcome.

The model. The odds of an event with probability p are \(p/(1-p)\), any number in (0, ∞); the log-odds or logit is \(\log\frac{p}{1-p}\), any real number. Logistic regression assumes the log-odds are linear in the features, and solves for p:

\[ \log\frac{p}{1-p} = z = w\cdot x + b \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p = \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}, \qquad \sigma'(z) = \frac{e^{-z}}{(1+e^{-z})^2} = \sigma(z)\big(1-\sigma(z)\big). \]

A weight now has a meaning: increasing a feature by 1 multiplies the odds by \(e^{w_j}\).

The loss, from the likelihood. Treat each label \(y_i \in \{0,1\}\) as a coin flip with probability \(p_i = \sigma(z_i)\), independent across pairs. The probability of all the observed labels (the likelihood) is \(\prod_i p_i^{y_i}(1-p_i)^{1-y_i}\): each factor is \(p_i\) when \(y_i = 1\) and \(1-p_i\) when \(y_i = 0\). Maximizing it is the same as minimizing the mean negative log:

\[ \ell(w,b) = -\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[y_i \log p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\Big]. \]

This is the log loss. It is also the cross-entropy between the label distribution (\(y_i\), \(1-y_i\)) and the prediction (\(p_i\), \(1-p_i\)): the average number of nats a code built from the predictions spends on the true labels. A confident wrong prediction costs a lot (\(p = 0.01\) for a positive costs \(-\log 0.01 = 4.6\)); a confident right one costs almost nothing.

The gradient. For one example, use \(\log p = \log\sigma(z)\) and \(\log(1-p) = \log\sigma(-z)\), with \(\frac{d}{dz}\log\sigma(z) = 1-\sigma(z)\):

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\Big[-y\log\sigma(z) - (1-y)\log\sigma(-z)\Big] = -y\,(1-p) + (1-y)\,p = p - y. \]

By the chain rule, \(\partial z_i/\partial w = x_i\). Put a column of 1s in front of X so the bias is just another weight; then

\[ \nabla \ell = \frac1n X^\top (p - y), \qquad \nabla^2 \ell = \frac1n X^\top S X, \quad S = \operatorname{diag}\big(p_i(1-p_i)\big). \]

The gradient reads naturally: each example pushes the weights along its features by its error \(p_i - y_i\).

Why it is correct: the loss is convex, so gradient descent finds the global minimum. For any direction v, \( v^\top \nabla^2\ell\, v = \frac1n \sum_i p_i(1-p_i)\,(x_i\cdot v)^2 \ge 0 \), because every term is a non-negative weight times a square. A function whose Hessian is positive semidefinite everywhere is convex, and every local minimum of a convex function is a global one. Adding \(\frac{\lambda}{2}\|w\|^2\) (L2 regularization, bias excluded) adds \(\lambda\) to the Hessian on the non-bias weights, which makes the minimum unique and keeps the weights finite even when the data can be separated perfectly or the features are collinear, as CN, AA and RA nearly are.

The step size, derived. Since \(p(1-p) \le \tfrac14\), the Hessian is bounded: \( v^\top\nabla^2\ell\,v \le \frac{1}{4n}\|Xv\|^2 + \lambda\|v\|^2 \le L\|v\|^2 \) with \( L = \|X\|_2^2/(4n) + \lambda \), where \(\|X\|_2\) is the largest singular value. Taylor's theorem with this bound gives, for the step \(w' = w - g/L\):

\[ \ell(w') \le \ell(w) - \frac1L\|g\|^2 + \frac{L}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{L^2}\|g\|^2 = \ell(w) - \frac{1}{2L}\|g\|^2 . \]

So every step lowers the loss unless the gradient is already zero; no step size needs tuning. With \(\lambda > 0\) the loss is also strongly convex, and the error shrinks by at least a factor \(1 - \lambda/L\) per step. Standardizing each feature to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 (with the training set's mean and deviation, reused unchanged at serving) keeps \(\|X\|_2\) small and the problem well conditioned.

Training on sampled negatives. We kept each negative with probability r. By Bayes' rule, for a pair with features x:

\[ \frac{P(y=1\mid x,\text{kept})}{P(y=0\mid x,\text{kept})} = \frac{P(y=1\mid x)\cdot 1}{P(y=0\mid x)\cdot r} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{logit}_{\text{true}}(x) = \text{logit}_{\text{sampled}}(x) + \log r . \]

Sampling multiplies every pair's odds by the same 1/r, so the weights are unchanged and only the bias moves, by exactly \(-\log r\). Rankings are unaffected; probabilities need the correction (§11 measures both).

ranker/logreg.py · the model, its loss and gradient, trained by gradient descent
def sigmoid(z):
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + np.tanh(0.5 * z))               # = 1/(1+e^-z), without overflow

def loss_grad(w, X, y, lam):
    """Mean log loss + (lam/2)|w|^2 (bias excluded), and its gradient. X has a column of 1s first."""
    p = sigmoid(X @ w)
    eps = 1e-12
    loss = -np.mean(y * np.log(p + eps) + (1 - y) * np.log(1 - p + eps))
    reg = np.r_[0.0, w[1:]]
    return loss + 0.5 * lam * reg @ reg, X.T @ (p - y) / len(y) + lam * reg

def train_logreg(X, y, lam=1e-3, iters=3000):
    """Full-batch gradient descent with step 1/L, L = ||X||_2^2 / (4n) + lam (the Hessian bound)."""
    X = np.c_[np.ones(len(X)), X]
    L = np.linalg.norm(X, 2) ** 2 / (4 * len(y)) + lam
    w = np.zeros(X.shape[1])
    for _ in range(iters):
        _, g = loss_grad(w, X, y, lam)
        w -= g / L
    return w

def predict_logit(w, X):
    return w[0] + X @ w[1:]

def fit_scaler(X):
    mu, sd = X.mean(0), X.std(0)
    return mu, np.where(sd > 0, sd, 1.0)

def scale(X, scaler):
    mu, sd = scaler
    return (X - mu) / sd

def prior_correct(logit, r):
    """Negatives were kept with probability r: true log-odds = sampled log-odds + log r."""
    return logit + math.log(r)

Cost. One gradient step multiplies X (n × d) by a vector twice: O(nd) time, O(nd) space for X. 3,000 steps on 244,152 rows × 9 columns is about 13 billion multiply-adds. Newton's method converges in a few dozen steps but each costs O(nd² + d³); with d = 9 both are cheap. Serving one candidate costs O(d).

Confirm by measuring. On the training cut, gradient descent reaches a log loss of 0.21534 with a gradient norm of 7.1 × 10⁻⁸; Newton's method on the same objective reaches the same loss, and the two weight vectors differ by at most 2.3 × 10⁻⁵. Where PYMK uses it. This is the ranking box. A linear model over a handful of graph features is a strong baseline for this problem, cheap to serve (nine multiply-adds per candidate), and easy to debug: every weight can be read and checked against the data.

8 · Evaluation 1: AUC is the Mann–Whitney statistic

The question. One number that says how well a score puts positives above negatives, whatever the threshold.

First attempt: accuracy. With a base rate of 0.274%, the model "nobody ever becomes friends" is right on 99.73% of candidate pairs. Accuracy rewards ignoring the rare class.

The key idea. Pick one positive and one negative at random. The AUC is the probability that the score ranks the positive higher, with ties counting half:

\[ \text{AUC} = P(s^+ > s^-) + \tfrac12 P(s^+ = s^-) = \frac{1}{n_+ n_-}\sum_{i \in \text{pos}}\sum_{j \in \text{neg}} \Big( [s_i > s_j] + \tfrac12 [s_i = s_j] \Big). \]

It is called the area under the ROC curve because sweeping a threshold from high to low and plotting the true-positive rate against the false-positive rate traces a curve whose area equals exactly this probability. 1 means every positive outranks every negative; 0.5 is a coin flip. By hand: scores (3, 2, 2, 1) with labels (1, 1, 0, 0). The four positive–negative pairs are (3, 2): 1, (3, 1): 1, (2, 2): ½, (2, 1): 1. AUC = 3.5/4 = 0.875.

Why the rank formula is correct. The double sum costs \(n_+ n_-\) comparisons: 21,290 × 7,735,863 ≈ 1.6 × 10¹¹ on our test set. Sort instead. Give the scores ranks 1 to n from lowest to highest, and assume no ties first. The rank of a positive i is 1 plus the number of items below it, positives and negatives:

\[ R_+ = \sum_{i\in\text{pos}} \text{rank}_i = \sum_{i\in\text{pos}}\big(1 + \#\text{pos below } i\big) + \sum_{i\in\text{pos}} \#\text{neg below } i = \frac{n_+(n_+ + 1)}{2} + U . \]

The positives' own ranks among themselves are 1, 2, …, \(n_+\), which sum to \(n_+(n_++1)/2\). What is left, U, counts the (positive, negative) pairs with the positive on top: the Mann–Whitney U statistic. So \(\text{AUC} = \big(R_+ - n_+(n_++1)/2\big)/(n_+n_-)\). With ties, give every member of a tied run the average of the ranks it spans; a positive tied with t negatives then gains t/2, exactly the ½ per tied pair in the definition. On the example, ranks are 4, 2.5, 2.5, 1; \(R_+ = 6.5\); \((6.5 - 3)/4 = 0.875\).

Cost. O(n log n) for the sort, O(n) for the ranks, O(n) space.

ranker/auc.py · Mann–Whitney AUC, and a vectorized, weighted version
def auc(scores, labels):
    """P(score of a random positive > score of a random negative), ties count 1/2 (Mann-Whitney)."""
    s, y = np.asarray(scores, float), np.asarray(labels, bool)
    order = np.argsort(s, kind="mergesort")
    s_sorted = s[order]
    ranks = np.empty(len(s))
    i = 0
    while i < len(s):                              # average rank over each run of tied scores
        j = i
        while j + 1 < len(s) and s_sorted[j + 1] == s_sorted[i]:
            j += 1
        ranks[order[i:j + 1]] = (i + j) / 2 + 1    # ranks are 1-based
        i = j + 1
    n_pos, n_neg = y.sum(), (~y).sum()
    return (ranks[y].sum() - n_pos * (n_pos + 1) / 2) / (n_pos * n_neg)

def auc_weighted(scores, labels, weights=None):
    """Same AUC, vectorized, with an optional weight per pair (used to bootstrap over users)."""
    s, y = np.asarray(scores, float), np.asarray(labels, bool)
    w = np.ones(len(s)) if weights is None else np.asarray(weights, float)
    order = np.argsort(s, kind="mergesort")
    s, y, w = s[order], y[order], w[order]
    starts = np.r_[0, np.flatnonzero(np.diff(s)) + 1]         # first index of each tie group
    pos = np.add.reduceat(w * y, starts)                      # positive weight per tie group
    neg = np.add.reduceat(w * ~y, starts)
    neg_below = np.cumsum(neg) - neg                          # negatives strictly lower
    return (pos * (neg_below + 0.5 * neg)).sum() / (pos.sum() * neg.sum())

The weighted version sums, over groups of tied scores, (positive weight) × (negative weight strictly below + half the tied negative weight). With integer weights it equals the plain AUC on duplicated rows (tested), which is what the bootstrap in §9 needs. On a 200,000-pair subsample both functions give 0.810048.

A caution about pooling. AUC here is pooled over all users' pairs. Features such as du are constant within one user, so they change pooled AUC (they separate busy users from quiet ones) without changing any user's ranking. The metric that matches the product is per user.

9 · Evaluation 2: what the user sees, recall@10, and error bars over users

Definitions. Let \(T_{10}(u)\) be the 10 candidates shown (ties by smaller id, as in Day 1). Then

\[ \text{recall@10}(u) = \frac{|T_{10}(u)\cap F(u)|}{|F(u)|}, \quad \text{precision@10}(u) = \frac{|T_{10}(u)\cap F(u)|}{10}, \quad \text{NDCG@10}(u) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{10} [\,v_i \in F(u)\,]/\log_2(i+1)}{\sum_{i=1}^{\min(10,|F(u)|)} 1/\log_2(i+1)} . \]

The denominator of recall is all of F(u), including future friends outside the candidate set, so the ceiling of §2 applies. NDCG (normalized discounted cumulative gain) also rewards putting hits near the top: slot i counts \(1/\log_2(i+1)\), and dividing by the best possible ordering puts it in [0, 1]. Each metric is averaged over the 9,357 users.

ranker/metrics.py · top-10, recall, precision and NDCG per user
def top_k(cands, scores, k=10):
    """The k best candidates; ties by smaller id, as in the exact baseline."""
    best = heapq.nsmallest(k, range(len(cands)), key=lambda i: (-scores[i], cands[i]))
    return [cands[i] for i in best]

def recall_at_k(top, future):
    """Share of u's future friends that appear in the shown list. Friends outside it are misses."""
    return len(set(top) & future) / len(future)

def precision_at_k(top, future, k=10):
    return len(set(top) & future) / k

def ndcg_at_k(top, future, k=10):
    dcg = sum(1 / math.log2(i + 2) for i, v in enumerate(top[:k]) if v in future)
    ideal = sum(1 / math.log2(i + 2) for i in range(min(k, len(future))))
    return dcg / ideal

Error bars: resample users, not pairs. Pairs of the same user share that user's degree, friends and future, so they are not independent; the roughly independent unit is the user (the same argument as Day 3's bootstrap for p99, where the unit was the request). Draw 9,357 users with replacement, recompute the mean, repeat 1,000 times, and take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. To compare two rankers, use the same resampled users for both and bootstrap the per-user difference: users who are easy for one ranker are easy for the other, and the paired interval removes that shared variation. For pooled AUC, a resample is just a weight per pair (how many times its user was drawn), so the scores are sorted once and each of the 200 replicates is a weighted pass.

ranker/bootstrap.py · percentile interval for a per-user mean
def bootstrap_ci(per_user, B=1000, alpha=0.05, seed=0):
    """Percentile CI for the mean of a per-user metric, resampling users with replacement."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    x = np.asarray(per_user, float)
    idx = rng.integers(0, len(x), size=(B, len(x)))
    means = x[idx].mean(1)
    return x.mean(), np.quantile(means, alpha / 2), np.quantile(means, 1 - alpha / 2)

Cost. O(B·U) for B resamples of U users, after the per-user metrics are computed once. Never rescore inside the loop.

11 · Calibration: is a 2% prediction really 2%?

The question. The ranking only needs scores in the right order. But the system also needs the numbers themselves: to merge lists from different sources (Algorithms block), to decide whether a suggestion is worth a notification, and to report expected value. A model is calibrated if, among all pairs it gives probability p, a fraction p actually connect.

How to check it. Sort the predictions, cut them into 10 equal-count bins, and compare the mean prediction with the observed rate in each bin (a reliability table). Two summaries: the expected calibration error \( \text{ECE} = \sum_b \frac{n_b}{n} |\bar p_b - \bar y_b| \), and the Brier score \( \frac1n\sum_i (p_i - y_i)^2 \), the squared error of the probabilities.

ranker/calibration.py · reliability table and ECE
def reliability(p, y, bins=10):
    """Bin by predicted probability (equal-count bins); mean prediction vs observed rate per bin."""
    p, y = np.asarray(p, float), np.asarray(y, float)
    order = np.argsort(p)
    rows = []
    for chunk in np.array_split(order, bins):
        rows.append((p[chunk].mean(), y[chunk].mean(), len(chunk)))
    ece = sum(n * abs(pm - om) for pm, om, n in rows) / len(p)  # expected calibration error
    return rows, ece

In production this is why a ranker is recalibrated on the most recent window (for example by fitting a one-feature logistic regression on its logit, Platt scaling, or a monotone step function, isotonic regression) more often than it is retrained.

12 · Feedback loops and exposure bias

The question. Our labels came from a graph that grew without any recommender. Once PYMK is live, most new friendships start from a suggestion, so the next training set records mostly the pairs the current model chose to show. What does a model trained on that learn?

The bias, derived. Let S = 1 if a pair was shown and Y = 1 if it connected. A pair that is never shown rarely connects whatever its merit, so the logged labels estimate \(P(Y=1 \mid S=1, x)\) at best, only for the kinds of pairs the old model liked. A new model trained on them learns to imitate the old one, and the pairs the old model never showed stay negatives forever. This is a feedback loop; in a social graph it also compounds Day 2's rich-get-richer effect, because positive degree weights show hubs more, hubs gain more friends, and their degree weights grow.

Confirm by measuring the starting point. The 100 users with the highest past degree (the top 1%) take part in 10.8% of the future friendships. They fill 50.9% of the shown slots when ranking by CN, 41.4% by Adamic–Adar, 43.9% by the logistic regression, 34.2% by the union ranker of the Algorithms block and 22.6% by RA (Jaccard, which penalizes degree, gives them 0.4%). Every useful ranker over-exposes hubs by 2–5× relative to their share of new friendships; a live system would turn that exposure into more hub friendships, and a model retrained on them would learn even larger degree weights.

The standard fix, and why it works. Show each candidate with a known probability \(\pi(x) > 0\) (most slots by the ranker, a few by controlled randomization: exploration). Weight each logged outcome by \(1/\pi\). Then for any function f of the outcome,

\[ \mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{S}{\pi(x)}\, f(Y)\Big] = \mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{\mathbb{E}[S\mid x]}{\pi(x)}\, f(Y)\Big] = \mathbb{E}\big[f(Y)\big], \]

using that S is drawn independently of Y given x, with \(\mathbb{E}[S \mid x] = \pi(x)\). This is inverse propensity weighting: unbiased as long as every candidate has a nonzero chance of being shown, and noisy when \(\pi\) is small, which is why exploration slots cannot be too rare. The week's graph has no impression log, so there is nothing to measure here beyond the exposure shares above; the derivation is the point.

13 · Privacy and fairness are constraints, not features

  • Privacy. A suggestion leaks information: showing v to u "because you have 3 mutual friends" tells u something about v's friend list, and a suggestion driven by a private signal (an uploaded address book, a hidden group, a location) can reveal that signal. So the eligibility rules (Day 1's contract: not self, not already friends, not blocked, privacy settings respected) are applied as a hard filter on every response, never as a feature weight that a large enough score could outvote. Features may only use data the user has allowed to be used for this purpose.
  • Fairness of exposure. Ten slots are a scarce resource. A ranker that learns "sociable people make more friends" gives hubs more of the slots, and quiet or new users fewer, even when they would welcome the connection. Measure who receives exposure (by degree bucket, by account age), as §12 did for the top 1%, and set explicit limits, for example a cap on how many users a hub can be suggested to per day, rather than hoping the model balances it.

Solution: Newton's method, the cross-check used above
ranker/newton.py · Newton's method on the same objective
def newton(X, y, lam, iters=30):
    """Newton's method on the same objective: w <- w - H^-1 g, with H = X^T S X / n + lam (bias excluded)."""
    X = np.c_[np.ones(len(X)), X]
    w = np.zeros(X.shape[1])
    R = lam * np.eye(X.shape[1]); R[0, 0] = 0
    for _ in range(iters):
        p = sigmoid(X @ w)
        g = X.T @ (p - y) / len(y) + R @ w
        H = (X * (p * (1 - p))[:, None]).T @ X / len(y) + R
        w -= np.linalg.solve(H, g)
    return w

Each step solves the second-order Taylor model of the loss exactly. Because the Hessian is \(X^\top S X/n\), each step is a weighted least-squares problem with weights \(p_i(1-p_i)\), hence the name iteratively reweighted least squares. On the training set it agrees with gradient descent to 2.3 × 10⁻⁵ in every weight. The gradient for n = 2, d = 1: \(\partial\ell/\partial w = \tfrac12[(p_1-y_1)x_1 + (p_2-y_2)x_2]\). For B in the example (friends YOU, X, Y): the candidates are A (via YOU and X) and H (via YOU and Y). Both have CN 2, and AA and RA tie as well: AA = 1/ln 3 + 1/ln 2 = 2.35 and RA = 1/3 + 1/2 = 0.83 for each, since YOU has 3 friends and X and Y have 2 each. Jaccard separates them, the wrong way round for this graph: 2/3 for A but 2/7 for H, whose six friends inflate the union. The tests check these numbers with pair_features. Per-user AUC, recency and r = 0.2 are left for you to measure; the bias should fall by about log(0.2/0.05) = 1.39.

Algorithms · merging ranked lists with a heap

1 · The question

Day 3 sharded the graph. Suppose the ranker runs on 16 shards, each scoring the candidates it stores and returning its own best 10, sorted. The user needs the best 10 overall. More generally: given L lists, each sorted from best to worst, with N items in total, output the best k items overall, each user once. A tiny case: lists (0.9 Ana, 0.5 Ben, 0.1 Cy), (0.8 Ben, 0.7 Dee), and an empty one; k = 3.

First attempt: concatenate and sort. Correct, but O(N log N) time and O(N) space, and it reads every item although only k are needed. The second attempt, scanning all L heads to find the largest for each output, is O(kL): fine for 3 lists, slow for 1,000 shards.

2 · The key idea: a heap of heads

Each list's best remaining item is its head. Keep the heads in a max-heap (Day 1's binary heap: a complete binary tree in an array where each parent is at least its children, so the maximum is at the root). Pop the root, output it, and push the next item of the list it came from. If a user was already output (the same person found by two sources), skip it.

ranker/merge.py · k-way merge of sorted candidate lists, with dedupe
def merge_top_k(lists, k=10):
    """lists: several candidate lists, each sorted by probability, highest first: [(prob, user), ...].
    Returns the k best distinct users across all lists."""
    heap = [(-lst[0][0], lst[0][1], s, 0) for s, lst in enumerate(lists) if lst]
    heapq.heapify(heap)                         # one entry per list: its current head
    out, seen = [], set()
    while heap and len(out) < k:
        neg_p, user, s, i = heapq.heappop(heap) # the best head is the best remaining item overall
        if user not in seen:                    # dedupe: the first time a user pops is its best score
            seen.add(user); out.append((-neg_p, user))
        if i + 1 < len(lists[s]):
            p, v = lists[s][i + 1]
            heapq.heappush(heap, (-p, v, s, i + 1))
    return out

3 · Why it is correct

Invariant. Before each pop, the heap contains exactly one entry for each list that still has unread items: that list's head. It holds after heapify, and each iteration removes one list's head and inserts that same list's next item, so it is preserved.

Each pop is the best unread item. Every unread item is in some list behind that list's head, and the lists are sorted, so it is no better than its head. The root is the best head. So the root is at least as good as every unread item. By induction, items come out in non-increasing order of score.

Dedupe keeps each user's best score. Copies of one user pop in non-increasing order, so the first copy to pop has the highest score of all copies; later copies are skipped. The output is therefore the k users with the highest best-score, which is what the tests check against a brute force on 200 random cases.

Why per-shard top 10 is enough. Let v be in the global top k and stored on shard s. Fewer than k items in the whole system beat v, so fewer than k on shard s do, and v is in shard s's top k. So asking every shard for k items and merging is exact, not an approximation.

4 · Cost

heapify on L heads is O(L). Each pop and push is O(log L), since the heap never holds more than L entries. To output k users while skipping D duplicates takes k + D pops: O(L + (k + D) log L) time, O(L + k) space. The cost does not depend on N at all, which matters when lists are long or lazily generated. Merging everything (Python's heapq.merge, or LeetCode 23) is O(N log L).

6 · Top-k relatives

  • Top k of a stream (973): keep a heap of size k whose root is the worst item kept; a new item replaces the root only if it is better. O(n log k) time, O(k) space; Day 1 derived it and compared quickselect.
  • An implicit k-way merge (373): the pairs (i, j) for a fixed i form a sorted list in j, so the problem is a merge of len(nums1) lists that are never built.
  • Heads plus a running maximum (632): the range covering one item from each list is [min of heads, max of heads]; advance the list with the minimum head.

Where PYMK uses it. In the scatter-gather step of serving (Day 3): every shard ranks its own candidates with the same model and returns its top 10; the front end merges them with a heap and applies the final filter. When the lists come from different sources, merge them into one candidate set and let one ranker score it.

class ListNode:
    def __init__(self, val=0, next=None):
        self.val, self.next = val, next

def mergeKLists(lists):                         # 23
    heap = [(node.val, i, node) for i, node in enumerate(lists) if node]
    heapq.heapify(heap)
    dummy = tail = ListNode()
    while heap:
        _, i, node = heapq.heappop(heap)        # i breaks ties: nodes are not comparable
        tail.next = tail = node
        if node.next:
            heapq.heappush(heap, (node.next.val, i, node.next))
    return dummy.next

def kClosest(points, k):                        # 973: keep the k closest in a max-heap of size k
    heap = []
    for x, y in points:
        d = -(x * x + y * y)                    # negate: heapq is a min-heap
        if len(heap) < k:
            heapq.heappush(heap, (d, x, y))
        elif d > heap[0][0]:
            heapq.heapreplace(heap, (d, x, y))
    return [[x, y] for _, x, y in heap]

def kSmallestPairs(nums1, nums2, k):            # 373: k-way merge of len(nums1) implicit sorted lists
    if not nums1 or not nums2:
        return []
    heap = [(nums1[i] + nums2[0], i, 0) for i in range(min(k, len(nums1)))]
    heapq.heapify(heap)
    out = []
    while heap and len(out) < k:
        _, i, j = heapq.heappop(heap)
        out.append([nums1[i], nums2[j]])
        if j + 1 < len(nums2):
            heapq.heappush(heap, (nums1[i] + nums2[j + 1], i, j + 1))
    return out

def smallestRange(nums):                        # 632: heap of heads + the running max of the heads
    heap = [(lst[0], r, 0) for r, lst in enumerate(nums)]
    heapq.heapify(heap)
    hi = max(lst[0] for lst in nums)
    best = (heap[0][0], hi)
    while True:
        lo, r, i = heapq.heappop(heap)
        if hi - lo < best[1] - best[0]:
            best = (lo, hi)
        if i + 1 == len(nums[r]):
            return list(best)
        nxt = nums[r][i + 1]
        hi = max(hi, nxt)
        heapq.heappush(heap, (nxt, r, i + 1))

In 23 the list index i breaks ties because nodes cannot be compared. In 373 the heap starts with at most k rows, since no pair from a row beyond the k-th can be among the k smallest. With 256 shards, most shards hold fewer than 10 of a user's candidates and return all of them, so the merge reads nearly every candidate: per-shard top-k saves network only when shards hold many more than k candidates each.

2 · Training–serving skew, and point-in-time features

The question. The model's weights are only valid for features computed exactly as in training. What goes wrong otherwise?

First attempt: compute training features from today's tables. To build a training row for a pair labelled in last month's window, look up the pair's features in the current feature store. But today's mutual-friend count already includes friendships formed during the label window, some of them caused by the very friendship being predicted. This is the random-split leak of the ML block (§1) in production form, and it inflated recall 2.7× there.

The key idea. A point-in-time feature store keeps each feature value with the time it became true, and a training row for a label at time t reads every feature as of t. The temporal_split with two cuts is the offline version of the same rule. The second rule is one code path: the function that computes features for training is the one that computes them for serving (on this page, pair_features for both cuts), so a change cannot reach one and not the other. Offline features (batch: AA over the whole graph, group overlaps) and online features (streaming: a friendship accepted just now) are both stored with timestamps.

Where PYMK uses it. In the feature-store box, and in the retraining job: every retrain draws a fresh pair of cuts from the most recent data, and recalibrates on the latest window (ML §11 measured why).

3 · Online experiments: A/B testing a new ranker

The question. Offline, logistic regression beats Adamic–Adar by 0.008 recall@10. Will users actually make more connections they value? Offline metrics are computed on a graph that grew without the new ranker; only showing it to users answers the question.

First attempt: ship it and compare this week with last week. Anything else that changed between the weeks (a holiday, a marketing campaign, an app release) is mixed into the difference, and there is no way to separate it.

The key idea: randomize. Assign each user by a hash of the user id to control (old ranker) or treatment (new ranker), so each user always sees the same arm. Randomization makes the two groups alike in everything except the ranker, on average, so a difference in outcome is caused by the ranker. Define, before starting:

  • a primary metric, per user: for example the share of users who accept at least one suggestion during the test, or accepted connections per user;
  • guardrail metrics that must not get worse: p95 latency of the PYMK request, block and report rates on suggested connections, requests left unanswered, notification opt-outs. A ranker that raises requests by pushing users towards strangers who then block them has failed.

How many users? Derived. Let the primary metric be a proportion: p in control, \(q = p(1+\text{lift})\) in treatment, with n users per arm. The difference of sample proportions is approximately normal with standard deviation \(\sqrt{(p(1-p) + q(1-q))/n}\). A two-sided test at level α rejects when the observed difference exceeds \(z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{2\bar p(1-\bar p)/n}\) (its spread when there is no effect, with \(\bar p = (p+q)/2\)). For the test to reject with probability 1 − β when the true difference is \(q-p\), the threshold must sit \(z_{1-\beta}\) true standard deviations below \(q-p\):

\[ q - p = \frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{2\bar p(1-\bar p)} + z_{1-\beta}\sqrt{p(1-p)+q(1-q)}}{\sqrt n} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n = \frac{\Big(z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{2\bar p(1-\bar p)} + z_{1-\beta}\sqrt{p(1-p)+q(1-q)}\Big)^2}{(q-p)^2}. \]
experiment/sample_size.py · users per arm for a two-proportion test
def users_per_arm(p, lift, alpha=0.05, power=0.8):
    """Users per arm to detect p -> p*(1+lift) with a two-sided two-proportion z-test."""
    q = p * (1 + lift)
    z_a = NormalDist().inv_cdf(1 - alpha / 2)
    z_b = NormalDist().inv_cdf(power)
    pbar = (p + q) / 2
    n = (z_a * math.sqrt(2 * pbar * (1 - pbar)) + z_b * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) + q * (1 - q))) ** 2
    return math.ceil(n / (q - p) ** 2)

The n ∝ 1/(q − p)² law dominates: halving the lift you want to detect needs four times the users. Small lifts are why these tests need very large user bases, and why a 10,000-user graph like ours can only ever be evaluated offline.

Two ways the result can mislead.

  • Novelty effects. A new set of faces in the PYMK panel attracts clicks at first simply because it is new; the effect fades as users get used to it. (The opposite, a primacy effect, hurts a change at first.) Plot the treatment effect by day since a user's first exposure; if it shrinks, the early numbers overstate the long-run gain. Run long enough to see it flatten, or compare users who joined after the test started.
  • Interference. A friendship involves two users, who may be in different arms. If a treatment user sends more requests and control users accept them, the control arm's numbers rise too and the measured difference shrinks; the assumption that one user's outcome does not depend on another's assignment is broken. Randomizing by graph clusters (whole communities in one arm) reduces it, at the cost of fewer independent units and wider intervals.

Where PYMK uses it. As the last step of every change to the ranker: offline evaluation on the temporal split decides what is worth testing; the A/B test decides what ships.

4 · Week 2 teaser: from hand-made features to learned representations

This week every feature was designed by hand: mutual friends weighted by 1/log d, shared groups, degrees. Each captures one idea, and the union ranker showed that the gains came from adding the right idea. Next week asks whether the ideas can be learned instead. In language, word2vec gives every word a vector, trained so that words appearing in similar contexts get similar vectors, and nobody writes down a feature for "is a colour" or "is a city". The same trick applies to people: treat a random walk on the friendship graph as a sentence and each user as a word (DeepWalk, node2vec), and every user gets a vector whose dot products predict who will connect, including across groups and over more than two hops. Week 2 builds representation learning from first principles, from word embeddings to user embeddings, and those embeddings become both a candidate source (nearest neighbours in vector space) and the features that replace much of today's hand-made list.

Problem variations
  • The primary metric is a mean (connections per user), not a proportion. What replaces p(1 − p) in the sample-size formula?
  • You test 20 metrics at α = 0.05. How many "significant" results do you expect when nothing changed?
  • Design an A/A test (both arms get the old ranker). What does it check, and what result would worry you?
Solution

Means. Replace \(p(1-p)\) and \(q(1-q)\) by the variance σ² of the per-user metric in each arm: \(n = (z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})^2 \cdot 2\sigma^2/\delta^2\) for a difference δ in means. Heavy-tailed metrics (connections per user, like degrees on Day 2) have a large σ², so they are often capped or replaced by a proportion. Twenty metrics. 20 × 0.05 = 1 false positive expected; pre-register one primary metric, or correct the level (for example α/20 for each). A/A. It checks the randomization and the metric pipeline: about 5% of A/A tests should show a significant difference at α = 0.05, the two arms should have the same size up to chance (a large imbalance means assignment or logging is broken), and the p-values of repeated A/A tests should be roughly uniform.
