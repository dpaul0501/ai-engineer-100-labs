DAY 4 · APPROXIMATION AND CANDIDATESMLDSASystem Design

Read less, find nearly the same people, and find some the count misses

Where we are in the design. Today rebuilds the candidate generation box of the map in R.7 under a budget. Day 1's exact baseline reads every friend's friend list: W(u) = Σf∈N(u) deg(f) entries, 1,477 on average on the week's graph but 7,284 at p99 and 51,806 at the maximum (Day 2), and about 40,000 per request at a billion users (R.6). A serving path cannot promise to read all of that. So the box must take a read budget, return almost the same top 10, and say how close it came. It should also not be the only source of candidates: Day 1 found that 46.8% of future friendships had no mutual friend at all.

The ML block builds the sampling estimator of mutual counts, proves it unbiased, derives its variance, and measures recall@10 against the exact baseline as the budget shrinks, with and without Day 2's hub capping. The Algorithms block teaches five structures from first principles: MinHash for Jaccard similarity, LSH banding to find similar pairs without comparing all of them, Bloom filters for the "already friends, already shown, blocked" checks, personalized PageRank as a third candidate source, and three small sampling and hashing structures from LeetCode. The Design block writes the candidate-budget contract: what each source promises, how sources merge, and what must stay exact.

ML · sampling estimators and recall against the exact answer

1 · The question

A user has d = 100 friends, and the friends have 70 friends each on average. The exact count reads 7,000 entries. The serving budget is 700. Read 10 friends' lists instead of 100: how close is the resulting top 10 to the exact one, and can the counts from 10 lists stand in for the counts from 100?

2 · First attempt: read the first lists that fit

Read friends in storage order (say, by id or by when the friendship formed) until the budget runs out. Two things go wrong.

  • It is biased, and repeating it does not help. Storage order is the same on every request. If friends are stored oldest first, the sample is always the user's oldest friends, and a candidate known only through recent friends is never seen. The error is systematic: no amount of averaging over requests removes it, because every request makes the same choice.
  • One hub eats the budget. If the first friend in the order has 1,480 friends, a budget of 700 reads nothing at all, and the user gets an empty list.

The fix for the first problem is randomness, and it has to be the right kind of randomness for the counts to mean anything. The fix for the second is Day 2's hub cap, and it has a price this block measures.

3 · The key idea, derived: a uniform sample of friends, scaled up

Definitions. Fix the user u with d = deg(u) friends and one candidate v with m = m(u, v) mutual friends. Draw a set S of s friends of u uniformly without replacement: every one of the \(\binom{d}{s}\) subsets of size s is equally likely. For each friend f define two indicators: \(X_f = 1\) if \(f \in S\) (random), and \(Y_f = 1\) if f is also a friend of v (fixed; \(\sum_f Y_f = m\)). Read the lists of the sampled friends only, and count

\[ c \;=\; \sum_{f \in N(u)} X_f\, Y_f \quad(\text{sampled friends who know } v), \qquad \hat m \;=\; \frac{d}{s}\, c . \]

Scaling by \(d/s\) is the natural guess: if a fraction \(s/d\) of the friends was read, about that fraction of the mutual friends was seen.

By hand. YOU have d = 6 friends f1…f6; candidate v knows f1 and f2, so m = 2. Sample s = 3 of the 6, so \(\hat m = 2c\). There are \(\binom63 = 20\) equally likely samples:

c = 0f3f4f5…neither f1 nor f2: \(\binom43 = 4\) samples · \(\hat m = 0\)
c = 1f1f3f4…one of f1, f2 and two of the other four: \(2 \cdot \binom42 = 12\) samples · \(\hat m = 2\)
c = 2f1f2f3…both, plus one of four: \(\binom41 = 4\) samples · \(\hat m = 4\)
Mean of \(\hat m\): \((0 \cdot 4 + 2 \cdot 12 + 4 \cdot 4)/20 = 2 = m\). Mean of \(\hat m^2\): \((0 + 4 \cdot 12 + 16 \cdot 4)/20 = 5.6\), so the variance is \(5.6 - 2^2 = 1.6\).

The average over all samples is exactly the true count. That is not luck; it is the first theorem.

4 · Why it is correct: unbiasedness and the variance, proved

Inclusion probabilities. A fixed friend f is in the sample in \(\binom{d-1}{s-1}\) of the \(\binom{d}{s}\) subsets (choose the other \(s-1\) members from the remaining \(d-1\)), and a fixed pair \(f \ne g\) is in \(\binom{d-2}{s-2}\) of them. So

\[ P(X_f = 1) = \frac{\binom{d-1}{s-1}}{\binom{d}{s}} = \frac{s}{d}, \qquad P(X_f = 1, X_g = 1) = \frac{\binom{d-2}{s-2}}{\binom{d}{s}} = \frac{s(s-1)}{d(d-1)} . \]

Unbiased. By linearity of expectation, which needs no independence,

\[ \mathbb E[c] = \sum_f Y_f\, \mathbb E[X_f] = m \cdot \frac{s}{d} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \mathbb E[\hat m] = \frac{d}{s} \cdot \frac{m s}{d} = m . \]

Variance. The \(X_f\) are not independent: if f is in the sample, there is one less place for g. Their covariance is negative:

\[ \operatorname{Var}(X_f) = \frac{s}{d}\Big(1 - \frac{s}{d}\Big) = \frac{s(d-s)}{d^2}, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(X_f, X_g) = \frac{s(s-1)}{d(d-1)} - \frac{s^2}{d^2} = -\frac{s(d-s)}{d^2(d-1)} . \]

The variance of a sum is the sum of the variances plus the covariances of all ordered pairs. Only the m friends with \(Y_f = 1\) contribute, so there are m variance terms and \(m(m-1)\) covariance terms:

\[ \operatorname{Var}(c) = m\,\frac{s(d-s)}{d^2} - m(m-1)\,\frac{s(d-s)}{d^2(d-1)} = \frac{m\,s(d-s)}{d^2}\Big(1 - \frac{m-1}{d-1}\Big) = \frac{m\,s\,(d-s)(d-m)}{d^2(d-1)} . \]

This is the variance of the hypergeometric distribution: c counts the "marked" items (mutual friends, m of them) in a sample of s drawn without replacement from d. Scaling by \(d/s\) multiplies the variance by \((d/s)^2\):

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\hat m) = \frac{d^2}{s^2}\cdot\frac{m\,s\,(d-s)(d-m)}{d^2(d-1)} = \frac{m\,(d-m)\,(d-s)}{s\,(d-1)} . \]

Check on the example: \(2 \cdot 4 \cdot 3 / (3 \cdot 5) = 1.6\), as counted by hand. Three things to read off the formula:

  • The factor \((d-s)/(d-1)\) is the finite population correction. It is 0 when \(s = d\) (reading every friend is exact) and close to 1 when \(s \ll d\). Sampling with replacement would drop it and be strictly worse.
  • Relative error. For \(m, s \ll d\) the standard deviation divided by m is about \(\sqrt{d/(m s)} = 1/\sqrt{\mathbb E[c]}\): one over the square root of the number of mutual friends you expect to see. A candidate with 15 mutuals among 65 friends, sampled at s = 10, is expected to show up about 2.3 times, so its estimate is off by about half.
  • Strong candidates are estimated best. The relative error falls as m grows. The candidates that are hard to rank are the ones near the 10th place, where counts are small (on the week's graph the exact 10th-best count is 2 or 3 for 72.7% of a sample of 1,000 users).

Ranking needs less than unbiasedness. For one request the factor \(d/s\) is the same for every candidate, so ranking by \(\hat m\) is ranking by c. Unbiasedness matters when \(\hat m\) leaves the request: as a feature for a model (Day 5), against a threshold ("show only if at least 3 mutual friends"), or in the "12 mutual friends" text on a profile. Then the response should carry s with it, so that a consumer can compute the variance.

5 · The budgeted version, and its cost

A budget is in reads, not in friends: reading friend f costs deg(f). The code reads friends in a uniformly random order and stops at the first list that would overflow the budget, then optionally skips hubs before sampling (Day 2's cap: drop any friend with more than C friends).

sampled.py · two-hop counts under a read budget, with an optional hub cap
def sampled_suggest(adj, u, k=10, budget=500, cap=None, rng=random):
    """Approximate top-k by mutual friends, reading at most `budget` adjacency entries.

    Friends are read in a uniformly random order; a friend whose degree exceeds `cap`
    (a hub) is skipped before sampling. Returns (top, estimate, reads)."""
    friends = sorted(f for f in adj[u] if cap is None or len(adj[f]) <= cap)
    rng.shuffle(friends)                          # uniform random order
    count, reads, s = Counter(), 0, 0
    for f in friends:
        if reads + len(adj[f]) > budget:          # the next list does not fit: stop
            break
        reads += len(adj[f]); s += 1
        for v in adj[f]:
            if v != u and v not in adj[u]:
                count[v] += 1
    scale = len(friends) / s if s else 0.0        # d / s: scale the sample count up
    estimate = {v: scale * c for v, c in count.items()}
    top = heapq.nsmallest(k, count, key=lambda v: (-count[v], v))
    return top, estimate, reads
estimator.py · the fixed-s estimator and its variance
def estimate_mutual(adj, u, v, s, rng=random):
    """Unbiased estimate of m(u, v) from s friends of u drawn without replacement."""
    sample = rng.sample(sorted(adj[u]), s)
    c = sum(1 for f in sample if v in adj[f])     # c ~ Hypergeometric(d, m, s)
    return len(adj[u]) / s * c

def estimator_variance(d, m, s):
    """Var(m_hat) = m (d - m) (d - s) / (s (d - 1)), derived on the page."""
    return m * (d - m) * (d - s) / (s * (d - 1)) if d > 1 else 0.0

A caveat about the stopping rule. The proof above is for a fixed s. In the budgeted code, s depends on the degrees of the friends that happened to come first, so \(d/s \cdot c\) is no longer exactly unbiased. For ranking that does not matter (the scale is common to all candidates). When the estimate is exported, use estimate_mutual with an s fixed before sampling, for example \(s = \lfloor B / \bar{d}_{\text{friend}} \rfloor\), where the mean friend degree is known from stored degree counters.

Cost. Shuffling u's list: O(d). Reading: at most B entries by construction, each a set lookup to drop u's own friends, so O(B) expected. Top-k: O(C log k) for C ≤ B distinct candidates touched. Space O(C). The exact baseline is the same with B = W(u).

6 · The fidelity metric: recall@10 against the exact answer

To measure an approximation we compare it with the thing it approximates, not with the future. Recall@K is the share of the exact top K that the approximate top K recovers. Both lists have length K, so precision@K is the same number. Measuring against the future (Day 5) mixes two errors, "the approximation lost candidates" and "mutual count is an imperfect predictor"; measuring against the exact baseline isolates the first, which is what a read budget changes.

Ties need a rule. If the exact 10th-best count is 2 and 40 candidates have 2 mutuals, Day 1's tie-break by id picks 10 of them, and an approximation that picks 10 other candidates with 2 mutuals has done nothing wrong. The tie-aware version counts an approximate pick as a hit when its exact count is at least the exact 10th-best count. On the week's graph at a budget of 400 reads, strict overlap is 0.470 and tie-aware recall is 0.585, so the rule matters; the rest of the page reports tie-aware recall.

recall.py · tie-aware recall@k
def recall_at_k(approx_top, exact_count, k=10):
    """Tie-aware recall@k: an approximate pick counts if its exact mutual count is at
    least the exact k-th best count. Denominator: min(k, number of candidates)."""
    if not exact_count:
        return 1.0
    best = sorted(exact_count.values(), reverse=True)
    need = min(k, len(best))
    threshold = best[need - 1]
    hits = sum(1 for v in approx_top[:k] if exact_count.get(v, 0) >= threshold)
    return hits / need

7 · Hub capping as a bias–variance trade-off

Skipping friends with more than C friends changes what is being estimated: the capped count \(m_C(u, v)\) counts only non-hub mutual friends. That is bias, an error that more reads cannot remove. In exchange it lowers variance at a fixed budget. A hub of degree 1,480 costs as many reads as 74 friends of degree 20, and every one of those 74 friends is a full vote in m, while the hub is one vote. Because of the friendship paradox (Day 2: the mean friend degree is 73.8 against a mean degree of 20), most of a random sample's reads go to a few hubs. Removing them buys more votes per read. Which effect wins depends on the budget, so measure it.

What the numbers say:

  • The variance formula is right to within simulation noise at every s, and the mean sits on 15.
  • At 800 reads and below, capping wins. At 400 reads, cap 200 recovers 0.735 of the exact top 10 against 0.585 uncapped. Skipping the lists of just 27 users (0.27% of users) cuts the full scan from 1,518 to 620 reads on average, 2.4 times less, so a fixed budget covers far more of the friends.

A practical rule falls out: cap hubs when the budget is below the typical full scan, and lift the cap when the budget allows. For the week's graph, C = 200 and a budget of 800 reads gives 0.83 of the exact top 10 at about a third of the exact work.

9 · Where PYMK uses it

The two-hop source in the serving path is this function: a seeded random order (so that a refresh gives the same answer, and two requests give independent errors), a read budget set by the latency budget, and a hub cap. Its recall@10 against the exact baseline is a release metric: a change that drops it below the agreed level does not ship. The counts it exports go with their sample size, because Day 5's ranker uses them as features.

Solution

s = 2. 15 samples: 6 have neither f1 nor f2, 8 have one, 1 has both. \(\hat m = 3c\): mean \((3 \cdot 8 + 6 \cdot 1)/15 = 2\); mean of squares \((9 \cdot 8 + 36)/15 = 7.2\); variance 3.2. Formula: \(2 \cdot 4 \cdot 4/(2 \cdot 5) = 3.2\).

Capped bias. The sample is drawn from the non-hub friends only, so the same proof with d replaced by the number of non-hub friends shows \(\mathbb E[\hat m] = m_C\). The bias for m is \(m_C - m = -\)(number of hubs that are mutual friends of u and v).

With replacement. Now c is binomial with s trials and success probability \(m/d\), so \(\operatorname{Var}(c) = s\,(m/d)(1 - m/d)\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat m) = m(d-m)/s\). The ratio to the without-replacement variance is \((d-1)/(d-s) \ge 1\), equal to 1 only at \(s = 1\).

Horvitz–Thompson. \(\hat m = \sum_f X_f Y_f / \pi_f\) with \(\mathbb E[X_f] = \pi_f\), so \(\mathbb E[\hat m] = \sum_f Y_f = m\), by linearity again. The uniform case is \(\pi_f = s/d\).

Algorithms · sketches and walks for candidate generation

1 · The question: similar friend sets, without the two-hop scan

The two-hop scan finds candidates by walking through friends, so its cost is set by the friends' degrees. Can we find, for every user, the non-friends whose friend sets overlap theirs most, with work that does not grow with the hubs? And then: can we find good candidates that are not two hops away, and can we check "already friends, already shown, blocked" without storing every pair?

Jaccard similarity. For two sets, \(J(A, B) = |A \cap B| / |A \cup B|\), a number in [0, 1]. Take A = N(u) and B = N(v). Compared with the mutual count \(|A \cap B|\), Jaccard divides by the size of the union, so a hub, who shares a few friends with everyone, gets a small score. Example: A = {1, 2, 3}, B = {2, 3, 4}: two in common, four in all, J = 0.5.

First attempt. Compare all pairs: \(\binom{n}{2}\) intersections, 50 million at 10,000 users and \(5 \times 10^{17}\) at a billion. Or compare only two-hop pairs, which costs Σv deg(v)² = 14,768,324 counter updates on the week's graph (Day 2), dominated by hubs. Both are the costs we want to escape.

2 · MinHash: a signature whose agreements estimate Jaccard

The idea. Put all user ids in a uniformly random order π. The min-hash of a set is its element that comes first in that order: \(h_\pi(A) = \min_{x \in A} \pi(x)\). Do this with k independent orders and keep the k minima: the set's signature, k numbers however large the set.

By hand. A = {1, 2, 3}, B = {2, 3, 4}. Order the union {1, 2, 3, 4} at random. If 2 or 3 comes first, it is the first element of both sets and the min-hashes agree. If 1 comes first, A's min-hash is 1 and B's is something else; if 4 comes first, the reverse. Each element of the union is equally likely to be first, so the min-hashes agree with probability 2/4 = J.

Theorem. For a uniformly random order, \(P\big(h_\pi(A) = h_\pi(B)\big) = J(A, B)\).

Proof. Let \(x^{*}\) be the element of \(A \cup B\) that comes first in π. By symmetry every element of \(A \cup B\) is equally likely to be \(x^{*}\), with probability \(1/|A \cup B|\). If \(x^{*} \in A \cap B\), it precedes every other element of both sets, so both min-hashes are \(\pi(x^{*})\) and they agree. If \(x^{*} \in A \setminus B\), then \(h_\pi(A) = \pi(x^{*})\) is smaller than \(\pi(y)\) for every \(y \in B\), so \(h_\pi(A) < h_\pi(B)\) and they differ; the case \(x^{*} \in B \setminus A\) is the same. So they agree exactly when \(x^{*} \in A \cap B\), which has probability \(|A \cap B| / |A \cup B|\). ∎

The estimator and its variance. With k independent orders, let \(Z_i = 1\) if the i-th min-hashes agree. Each \(Z_i\) is a Bernoulli(J) variable, independent of the others, so

\[ \hat J = \frac1k \sum_{i=1}^k Z_i, \qquad \mathbb E[\hat J] = J, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\hat J) = \frac{1}{k^2}\sum_{i=1}^k J(1-J) = \frac{J(1-J)}{k} . \]

At J = 0.1 and k = 100 the standard deviation is 0.03. Halving it costs four times the hashes, the same \(1/\sqrt{k}\) law as the sampling estimator.

Hash functions instead of orders. Storing random orders of a billion ids is impossible, so a hash function plays the order: \(h_i(x) = \text{mix}(x \oplus \text{seed}_i)\), where mix is a 64-bit function that sends nearby inputs to unrelated outputs. The proof needs every element to be equally likely to come first (a family with this property is called min-wise independent), and cheap hash families can fail it badly. The textbook universal family \((a x + b) \bmod p\) is fine for hash tables but not here: on A = {0…59}, B = {30…89} (J = 1/3) with k = 400, it estimated 0.282, 0.237, 0.270, 0.285 and 0.268 for five seeds, all low, because consecutive inputs map to an arithmetic progression mod p, whose minimum falls near its ends rather than at a random element. The mixing hash gave 0.347, 0.320, 0.335, 0.310 and 0.340.

minhash.py · signatures and the Jaccard estimate
MASK = (1 << 64) - 1

def mix64(x):
    """A 64-bit mixing function (the splitmix64 finalizer): nearby inputs give unrelated outputs."""
    x = (x + 0x9E3779B97F4A7C15) & MASK
    x = ((x ^ (x >> 30)) * 0xBF58476D1CE4E5B9) & MASK
    x = ((x ^ (x >> 27)) * 0x94D049BB133111EB) & MASK
    return x ^ (x >> 31)

def make_hashes(k, seed=0):
    """k random seeds; h_i(x) = mix64(x XOR seed_i) stands in for k random permutations."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    return [rng.getrandbits(64) for _ in range(k)]

def minhash(items, hashes):
    """Signature: for each hash function, the smallest hash value over the set."""
    return [min(mix64(x ^ h) for x in items) for h in hashes]

def jaccard(a, b):
    return len(a & b) / len(a | b) if a or b else 1.0

def estimate_jaccard(sig_a, sig_b):
    """Fraction of positions where the two signatures agree."""
    return sum(x == y for x, y in zip(sig_a, sig_b)) / len(sig_a)

Cost. One signature costs k hash evaluations per friend: O(k · deg u), and O(k · 2|E|) for all users, linear in the edges, with no Σ deg² term. Storage: k numbers per user. Comparing two signatures: O(k). A signature also updates cheaply when a friendship forms: \(h_i(A \cup \{x\}) = \min(h_i(A), h_i(x))\), O(k). A friendship that ends cannot be removed that way; the signature must be recomputed from the list.

Confirm by measuring. 1,000 two-hop pairs of the week's graph (mean J 0.029), 8 independent hash sets: the mean squared error is 0.001585 at k = 16 against a predicted \(J(1-J)/k\) of 0.001741, and 0.000409 at k = 64 against 0.000435. With k = 20,000 on 40 pairs, the standardized errors \((\hat J - J)/\text{sd}\) have mean +0.17 and standard deviation 0.80, close to the 0 and 1 an unbiased estimator gives.

3 · LSH banding: find the similar pairs without comparing all pairs

The question. Signatures make one comparison cheap, but there are still \(\binom n2\) pairs. We want only the pairs with high J, and we want to find them by hashing, not by comparing.

The key idea. Split each signature of k = b · r values into b bands of r consecutive values. For each band, put every user into a bucket keyed by that band's r values. Two users become a candidate pair if they share a bucket in at least one band. A pair that agrees on a whole band is likely similar; one band is a narrow door, and b doors give many chances.

By hand. r = 2, b = 3, signatures of three users:

Userband 1band 2band 3
u(17, 4)(9, 30)(2, 11)
v(17, 8)(9, 30)(5, 11)
w(3, 4)(6, 30)(2, 7)

u and v agree on all of band 2, so (u, v) is a candidate. u and w agree on single values in every band but on no full band, so (u, w) is not, although they agree on 3 of 6 values against 4 of 6 for u and v: banding rewards agreement that is concentrated, which is likely only when J is high.

The S-curve, derived. Take a pair with Jaccard s. Each signature position agrees with probability s, independently across positions (MinHash theorem, independent hash functions). So

\[ P(\text{one band agrees}) = s^r, \qquad P(\text{no band agrees}) = (1 - s^r)^b, \qquad P(\text{candidate}) = 1 - (1 - s^r)^b . \]

As a function of s, this rises from 0 to 1 in an S shape. Where is the step? At \(s^r = 1/b\), i.e. \(s = (1/b)^{1/r}\), the probability is \(1 - (1 - 1/b)^b \approx 1 - 1/e \approx 0.63\). The steepest point (where the second derivative is 0) is close by. Differentiate \(f(s) = 1 - (1 - s^r)^b\):

\[ f'(s) = b\,r\,s^{r-1}(1 - s^r)^{b-1}, \qquad \frac{f''(s)}{f'(s)} = \frac{r-1}{s} - \frac{(b-1)\,r\,s^{r-1}}{1 - s^r} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; s^r = \frac{r-1}{rb-1} . \]

For large b that is \(s^r \approx (1 - 1/r)/b\), the same order as \(1/b\). So the rule of thumb is: threshold ≈ (1/b)1/r; more rows r make the step sharper and move it right; more bands b move it left. A pair below the threshold still becomes a candidate with probability \(\approx b\,s^r\) (a false positive), and one above it is missed with probability \((1 - s^r)^b\) (a false negative).

lsh.py · banding and the S-curve
def lsh_pairs(sigs, r, b):
    """Banding: split each signature into b bands of r values; two users become a
    candidate pair if they agree on every value of at least one band.
    Returns (set of pairs u < v, work = pairs emitted before deduplication)."""
    pairs, work = set(), 0
    for j in range(b):
        buckets = defaultdict(list)
        for u, sig in sigs.items():
            buckets[tuple(sig[j * r:(j + 1) * r])].append(u)
        for members in buckets.values():
            work += len(members) * (len(members) - 1) // 2
            for i, x in enumerate(members):
                for y in members[i + 1:]:
                    pairs.add((min(x, y), max(x, y)))
    return pairs, work

def s_curve(s, r, b):
    """P(a pair with Jaccard s becomes a candidate) = 1 - (1 - s^r)^b."""
    return 1 - (1 - s ** r) ** b

Cost. Bucketing: O(n b) band keys. Candidate pairs: \(\sum_{\text{buckets}} \binom{\text{size}}{2}\), which is the work that matters and depends on the data. Then verify each candidate pair exactly (one intersection, Day 1) or from the signatures (O(k)). The trade-off: a lower threshold finds more of the similar pairs and emits many more dissimilar ones, because dissimilar pairs vastly outnumber similar ones.

4 · Bloom filters: membership in a few bits per key

The question. Before a list is returned, every candidate is checked: not already a friend, not blocked in either direction, not shown many times already. At a billion users with 100 billion friendships (R.6), an exact set of all pairs is 1.6 TB of ids before overhead. Can a membership test fit in much less, if it may sometimes say "yes" wrongly?

First attempt. A hash set (Day 1, and LeetCode 705 below) stores the keys themselves: 8 bytes per id, more with pointers and empty slots. Storing only a short fingerprint of each key saves space but still pays per key for the table.

The key idea. A Bloom filter is an array of m bits, all 0, and k hash functions into positions 0 … m−1. To add a key, set its k bits to 1. To query, check its k bits: if any is 0, the key was never added; if all are 1, answer "probably yes". Bits only ever go from 0 to 1, so an added key always finds its bits set: no false negatives, ever. A false positive happens when other keys have, between them, set all k bits of a key that was never added.

By hand. m = 10, k = 2. Add "u3–u7" at positions {1, 4} and "u3–u9" at {4, 8}. Query "u3–u5" at {1, 8}: both bits are 1 (set by two different keys), so the answer is a false "yes". Query "u3–u6" at {2, 4}: bit 2 is 0, so a certain "no".

The false-positive rate, derived. Assume the hash positions are uniform and independent. One position of one key misses a given bit with probability \(1 - 1/m\). After n keys, kn positions have been set, so

\[ P(\text{a given bit is still } 0) = \Big(1 - \frac1m\Big)^{kn} \approx e^{-kn/m} \quad\text{(using } (1 - 1/m)^m \approx e^{-1}\text{)}. \]

A key that was never added is a false positive when all its k bits are 1. Treating the bits as independent (very nearly true for large m),

\[ f \;=\; \big(1 - e^{-kn/m}\big)^{k} . \]

The best k. More hash functions check more bits (good) but set more bits (bad). Write \(p = e^{-kn/m}\), the fraction of bits still 0, so \(k = -(m/n)\ln p\) and

\[ \ln f = k \ln(1 - p) = -\frac{m}{n}\,\ln p\,\ln(1-p) . \]

Minimizing f means maximizing \(g(p) = \ln p \cdot \ln(1-p)\) on (0, 1); both logarithms are negative, so g > 0, and \(g \to 0\) at both ends. Its derivative is

\[ g'(p) = \frac{\ln(1-p)}{p} - \frac{\ln p}{1-p} = \frac{(1-p)\ln(1-p) - p \ln p}{p(1-p)} = -\frac{D(p)}{p(1-p)}, \qquad D(p) = p\ln p - (1-p)\ln(1-p) . \]

D is 0 at 0, ½ and 1, and \(D'(p) = \ln\big(p(1-p)\big) + 2\), which is negative for \(p < 0.161\) and positive from there to ½. So on (0, ½) D first falls below 0 and then climbs back to 0 at ½: \(D < 0\), and g increases. By the symmetry \(D(1-p) = -D(p)\), g decreases on (½, 1). The maximum is at \(p = 1/2\): the best filter is half full. Then

\[ k^{*} = \frac{m}{n}\ln 2, \qquad f^{*} = \Big(\frac12\Big)^{k^{*}} = \big(2^{-\ln 2}\big)^{m/n} \approx 0.6185^{\,m/n}, \qquad \frac{m}{n} = \frac{\log_2(1/f)}{\ln 2} \approx 1.44 \log_2\frac1f . \]

So 1% false positives costs 9.6 bits per key with k = 7, whatever the keys are. That is the whole point: the size depends on the error rate, not on the key length.

bloom.py · a Bloom filter and its false-positive rate
class Bloom:
    """m bits; k positions per integer key from two base hashes (Kirsch-Mitzenmacher).
    A pair (u, v) is stored as the integer u * N + v. Uses mix64 from the MinHash block."""
    def __init__(self, m, k):
        self.m, self.k, self.bits = m, k, bytearray((m + 7) // 8)

    def _positions(self, key):
        h = mix64(key)
        h1, h2 = h & 0xFFFFFFFF, (h >> 32) | 1    # h2 odd, so the k positions differ
        return [(h1 + i * h2) % self.m for i in range(self.k)]

    def add(self, key):
        for p in self._positions(key):
            self.bits[p >> 3] |= 1 << (p & 7)

    def __contains__(self, key):                  # False is certain; True may be wrong
        return all(self.bits[p >> 3] >> (p & 7) & 1 for p in self._positions(key))

def bloom_fpr(n, m, k):
    """False-positive rate after n insertions: (1 - e^{-kn/m})^k."""
    return (1 - math.exp(-k * n / m)) ** k

def bloom_best_k(n, m):
    return max(1, round(m / n * math.log(2)))

Cost. Add and query: k hash positions and k bit operations, O(k), with no comparisons of keys. Space: m bits. The code derives the k positions from two base hashes as \(h_1 + i\,h_2\), which Kirsch and Mitzenmacher (2006) showed keeps the same asymptotic false-positive rate.

Why the one-sided error fits these checks. The filters guard exclusions. A false positive drops a good candidate, which costs a little relevance at a rate we choose. A false negative would show a blocked person, which is a privacy failure, and the filter never produces one for keys it contains. Two cautions follow. A filter is a snapshot: a block made after it was built is not in it, so the final exact check against the source of truth (Day 3) still runs on every response. And a Bloom filter cannot delete: an unblock cannot clear bits other keys share. Rebuild periodically, or use a counting Bloom filter (small counters instead of bits).

Why a Bloom filter cannot count mutual friends. Counting needs the candidates, and a filter cannot list what it holds: it stores bits, not keys, so it cannot replace the scan that enumerates friends of friends. Given a candidate v from somewhere else, you could ask "is f–v a friendship?" for each of u's d friends, but each of the \(d - m\) non-mutual friends adds a false "yes" with probability f, so the count is inflated by \((d - m) f\) on average. Measured with the 8-bits-per-key filter on 200 users and up to 20 candidates each: the mean over-count was 0.381 against a predicted 0.396, and no count was ever too low. The error is one-sided, grows with the user's degree, and is largest for exactly the weak candidates that a threshold must separate.

Where PYMK uses it. Per-user filters next to the candidate lists: "already shown to u" (a user who has seen 2,000 suggestions needs 2.4 KB at 1%), "blocked by or blocking u", and a global "recently dismissed" filter. They run in the merge step, before ranking, to cut the list cheaply; the exact checks run after ranking on the 10 that are shown.

5 · Personalized PageRank: a third candidate source

The question. Two-hop counting gives every mutual friend the same vote and sees nobody three hops away. Can one score add up evidence over all paths from u, favour short paths, and discount votes that pass through hubs?

First attempt. Count paths of length 2, 3, 4 … from u to v. The counts are the entries of \(A^2, A^3, \dots\), and they explode through hubs: a hub with 1,480 friends creates 1,480 paths to each of its friends' friends. Raw path counts reward being near a hub, the opposite of what we want.

The key idea: a random walk with restart. Start a walker at u. At each step, with probability α it jumps back to u; otherwise it moves to a uniformly random friend of where it is. The long-run share of time it spends at v is the personalized PageRank \(\pi_u(v)\). Write \(P = D^{-1}A\) for the step matrix: \(P_{fv} = 1/\deg(f)\) if f–v is an edge. Each row sums to 1. If \(x_t\) is the walker's distribution after t steps (a row vector),

\[ x_{t+1} = \alpha\, e_u + (1 - \alpha)\, x_t P, \qquad \text{and the limit satisfies} \qquad \pi_u = \alpha\, e_u + (1 - \alpha)\, \pi_u P . \]

What the score adds up. Substitute the equation into itself T times:

\[ \pi_u = \alpha \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} (1-\alpha)^t\, e_u P^t \;+\; (1-\alpha)^T \pi_u P^T \;\xrightarrow{T \to \infty}\; \alpha \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} (1-\alpha)^t\, e_u P^t . \]

The last term vanishes because \(\pi_u P^T\) has total mass at most 1 and \((1-\alpha)^T \to 0\). \((e_u P^t)_v\) is the probability that a t-step walk from u ends at v, and paths of length t are weighted by \((1-\alpha)^t\). The two-step term is

\[ (e_u P^2)_v = \sum_{f \in N(u) \cap N(v)} \frac{1}{\deg(u)}\cdot\frac{1}{\deg(f)} , \]

a mutual-friend count in which each mutual friend f votes \(1/\deg(f)\). A hub's vote is small, as Day 2 argued it should be; Day 5 meets the same sum as the resource allocation index.

Two-step terms: D gets \(\tfrac13(\tfrac13 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac13) = \tfrac{7}{18} \approx 0.389\); E and F get \(\tfrac13 \cdot \tfrac13 = \tfrac19 \approx 0.111\). Full PPR with α = 0.3 (200 iterations): YOU 0.410, A 0.145, B 0.121, C 0.145, D 0.110, E 0.034, F 0.034. Among non-friends the order is D, then E and F, as the mutual counts say.

Power iteration, and why it converges. Compute \(x_{t+1} = \alpha e_u + (1-\alpha) x_t P\) from \(x_0 = e_u\). Two facts give the rate.

  • P does not increase the L1 norm. For any row vector y, \(\|yP\|_1 = \sum_v \big|\sum_f y_f P_{fv}\big| \le \sum_f |y_f| \sum_v P_{fv} = \|y\|_1\), using the triangle inequality and that each row of P sums to 1 (or 0 for a user with no friends).
  • The error contracts. Subtract the fixed-point equation from the iteration: \(x_{t+1} - \pi_u = (1-\alpha)(x_t - \pi_u)P\). So \(\|x_{t+1} - \pi_u\|_1 \le (1-\alpha)\|x_t - \pi_u\|_1\), and after t steps
\[ \|x_t - \pi_u\|_1 \le (1-\alpha)^t\, \|x_0 - \pi_u\|_1 \le 2\,(1-\alpha)^t \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad t \ge \frac{\ln(2/\varepsilon)}{\ln\!\big(1/(1-\alpha)\big)} \text{ steps reach error } \varepsilon . \]

(Both vectors have total mass 1, so their L1 distance is at most 2.) The same contraction shows the fixed point exists and is unique (two fixed points would be at distance \(\le (1-\alpha)\) times their own distance). For α = 0.3 and ε = 10−4: \(\ln(20{,}000)/\ln(1/0.7) = 9.90/0.357 \approx 28\) iterations. The bound does not depend on the graph's size, only on α.

ppr.py · personalized PageRank by power iteration and by walks
def to_arrays(adj):
    """Edge arrays for vectorized walks: an edge f -> v for every friendship, both ways."""
    src = np.array([f for f in range(len(adj)) for _ in adj[f]], dtype=np.int64)
    dst = np.array([v for f in range(len(adj)) for v in adj[f]], dtype=np.int64)
    deg = np.array([len(a) for a in adj], dtype=np.float64)
    return src, dst, deg

def ppr(src, dst, deg, u, alpha=0.3, iters=30):
    """Personalized PageRank of every user for walks that restart at u with prob. alpha.
    Power iteration on x = alpha e_u + (1 - alpha) x P, P[f, v] = 1/deg(f) per edge."""
    n = len(deg)
    e = np.zeros(n); e[u] = 1.0
    inv = np.divide(1.0, deg, out=np.zeros(n), where=deg > 0)
    x = e.copy()
    for _ in range(iters):
        spread = np.bincount(dst, weights=x[src] * inv[src], minlength=n)   # x P
        x = alpha * e + (1 - alpha) * spread
    return x

def ppr_suggest(adj, src, dst, deg, u, k=10, alpha=0.3, iters=30):
    x = ppr(src, dst, deg, u, alpha, iters)
    x[u] = -1.0
    for f in adj[u]:
        x[f] = -1.0                               # self and friends are not candidates
    top = np.argsort(-x, kind="stable")[:k]
    return [int(v) for v in top if x[v] > 0]

def ppr_walks(adj_lists, u, walks=1000, alpha=0.3, rng=random):
    """Monte Carlo PPR: a walk stops with probability alpha before each step; the share of
    walks that stop at v estimates ppr(u)[v] without bias. Returns (stop counts, steps)."""
    stops, steps = Counter(), 0
    for _ in range(walks):
        x = u
        while rng.random() >= alpha and adj_lists[x]:
            x = rng.choice(adj_lists[x]); steps += 1   # one random read: O(1), as in LC 380
        stops[x] += 1
    return stops, steps

Cost. One power iteration touches every edge: O(|E|) per iteration, O(t |E|) per user. On the past graph that is 30 × 160,000 = 4,800,000 edge updates per user, against a mean of 988 reads for the exact two-hop scan of the same users: power iteration is a batch tool, not a serving one. The Monte Carlo version in the code needs no matrix: run R walks that stop with probability α before each step and count where they stop. A walk stops after exactly t moves with probability \(\alpha(1-\alpha)^t\), and is then at v with probability \((e_u P^t)_v\); summing over t gives exactly the series above, so the share of walks stopping at v is unbiased for \(\pi_u(v)\), with variance \(\pi_u(v)(1 - \pi_u(v))/R\) (a binomial share, like MinHash). Expected cost: \(R(1-\alpha)/\alpha\) random reads. Each step is "pick a uniform random friend", O(1) on an array, which is LeetCode 380 below.

6 · Three small structures behind sampling and filtering

380 Insert Delete GetRandom O(1). The question: keep a set under inserts and deletes and return a uniformly random element, all in O(1). A random walk step and the friend sampler both need "a uniform random friend" from a list that changes. First attempt: a hash set has O(1) insert and delete but no index, so a random element costs O(n); a list has O(1) random access but O(n) delete. The key idea: a dense array of the elements plus a map from element to its index. To delete, move the last element into the hole and update its index, then pop. Invariant: items holds exactly the set with no gaps, and pos[items[i]] == i for every i; insert appends and records the index; delete overwrites slot i with the last element, records i as its new index and pops the duplicate, so both parts still hold. A uniform index into a gap-free array is a uniform element. Cost: O(1) expected per operation (hash map) and amortized (array growth); O(n) space.

randomized_set.py · 380
class RandomizedSet:                              # 380: insert, remove, getRandom in O(1)
    def __init__(self):
        self.pos, self.items = {}, []             # value -> index, and a dense array

    def insert(self, val):
        if val in self.pos:
            return False
        self.pos[val] = len(self.items); self.items.append(val)
        return True

    def remove(self, val):
        if val not in self.pos:
            return False
        i, last = self.pos[val], self.items[-1]
        self.items[i], self.pos[last] = last, i   # move the last element into the hole
        self.items.pop(); del self.pos[val]
        return True

    def getRandom(self):
        return random.choice(self.items)

528 Random Pick with Weight. The question: return index i with probability \(w_i / \sum_j w_j\). Weighted walks and unequal-probability friend sampling (the Horvitz–Thompson variation above) need it. First attempt: repeat each index \(w_i\) times in a list and pick uniformly: memory \(\sum w\), useless for real-valued weights. The key idea: prefix sums \(S_i = w_0 + \dots + w_i\) cut [0, Sn−1) into consecutive intervals, interval i being \([S_{i-1}, S_i)\) of length \(w_i\). Draw x uniformly on [0, total) and return the interval containing it. Correct: x lands in interval i with probability (its length)/(total) = \(w_i/\sum w\); bisect_right returns the first i with \(S_i > x\), which is exactly that interval, and an index with weight 0 has an empty interval and is never returned. Cost: O(n) to build, O(log n) per pick by binary search.

import bisect
import itertools

class Solution528:                                # 528: pick index i with prob. w[i] / sum(w)
    def __init__(self, w):
        self.prefix = list(itertools.accumulate(w))

    def pickIndex(self):
        x = random.random() * self.prefix[-1]     # uniform point on [0, total)
        return bisect.bisect_right(self.prefix, x)

705 Design HashSet. The question: build the exact set the Bloom filter approximates. The key idea: an array of M buckets; key x lives in bucket \(h(x) \bmod M\), a short list (separate chaining). Why it is fast: if h spreads n keys uniformly, a bucket holds \(n/M\) keys on average (the load factor), so a lookup scans O(1 + n/M) keys. Doubling M whenever \(n > M\) keeps the load at most 1. A doubling copies all keys, but the copies form a geometric series \(8 + 16 + \dots + n \le 2n\), so the total over n inserts is O(n): O(1) amortized. Compare with the Bloom filter: the hash set keeps the keys and so is exact and can delete; the filter throws the keys away and keeps k bits each.

hash_set.py · 705
class MyHashSet:                                  # 705: separate chaining, resize at load 1
    def __init__(self):
        self.size, self.buckets = 0, [[] for _ in range(8)]

    def _b(self, key):
        return self.buckets[hash(key) % len(self.buckets)]

    def add(self, key):
        b = self._b(key)
        if key not in b:
            b.append(key); self.size += 1
            if self.size > len(self.buckets):     # keep chains O(1) long on average
                old = [x for bucket in self.buckets for x in bucket]
                self.buckets = [[] for _ in range(2 * len(self.buckets))]
                for x in old:
                    self._b(x).append(x)

    def remove(self, key):
        b = self._b(key)
        if key in b:
            b.remove(key); self.size -= 1

    def contains(self, key):
        return key in self._b(key)

Solutions

MinHash by listing. The min-hashes agree exactly when the first of {1, 2, 3, 4} in the order is 2 or 3. Of the 24 orders, 6 start with each element, so 12 agree: 12/24 = ½ = J.

(r, b) for 0.15. Candidates with \(rb = 100\): (1, 100) gives 0.01, (2, 50) gives 0.141, (4, 25) gives 0.447, (5, 20) gives 0.549. The closest is (2, 50). At J = 0.3: \((1 - 0.09)^{50} = 0.91^{50} \approx 0.0090\), so it misses such a pair 0.9% of the time.

Bloom sizing. \(m/n = 1.44 \log_2(1000) = 1.44 \times 9.97 \approx 14.4\) bits per key, so m ≈ 14.4 million bits ≈ 1.8 MB, and \(k = 14.4 \ln 2 \approx 10\).

Bloom intersection. With \(t\) set bits, the number of keys in a filter is estimated by inverting the bit-is-zero probability: \(\hat n = -(m/k)\ln(1 - t/m)\). Apply it to each filter and to their OR (the filter of the union) and use \(|A \cap B| = |A| + |B| - |A \cup B|\). It estimates the size only; the filter still holds no keys, so it cannot name the mutual friends, and the estimate is noisy when the intersection is small, which is the usual case.

Bucket cap and disagreements: run the code; the pairs lost to a bucket cap are the small-degree friends of hubs, and the users PPR promotes over the mutual count are those whose mutual friends have small degrees.

System Design · the candidate-budget contract

The question

Candidate generation is now several approximate sources with budgets. What exactly does the box promise the ranker, and what may never be approximate?

First attempt: one source, best effort

Call the two-hop scan with a timeout and return whatever it has. Three problems. The output changes with machine load, so tests and experiments are not reproducible. Nobody downstream knows how complete the list is. And the 46.8% of future friendships with no mutual friend (Day 1) can never appear.

The key idea: a contract with before and after promises

PartPromise
Inputcandidates(u, K, budget, seed): user, list size (say 200 for the ranker), read budget in adjacency entries, and a seed derived from (u, day) so a refresh is stable.
Before (preconditions)u exists and is active; K ≥ 0; budget ≥ 0.
After: always exactAt most K ids, no duplicates, never u, never a current friend, never blocked in either direction, never a user whose privacy settings exclude u, never a deactivated user, all checked against the source of truth when the response is built.
After: boundedReads ≤ budget (per source budgets sum to it). Deterministic for a given (u, K, budget, seed, graph snapshot).
After: may be approximate, and says soWhich candidates appear, and their scores. Each candidate carries its source(s) and features, with sample sizes (the s behind \(\hat m\), the k behind \(\hat J\), the number of walks behind a PPR score), plus an exact: bool flag for the list.
Quality targetMeasured, not promised per request: recall@10 against the exact baseline (ML block) and recall of future friendships on the temporal split (this block), both checked before a release.

Multiple sources, merged and deduplicated. Each source returns a ranked list: the sampled two-hop scan (online), PPR and LSH partners (precomputed offline), and shared groups (members of u's groups, a cheap source for users with few friends). The merge must remove duplicates (a user found by two sources is one candidate with two source tags) and apply the hard filters once. The simplest merge is round-robin by rank, which gives each source an equal share; Day 5 replaces it with a score-based heap merge once the ranker can put scores from different sources on one scale.

merge.py · merge ranked sources, deduplicate, filter
def merge_sources(sources, k, exclude):
    """Round-robin merge of ranked candidate lists, deduplicated, hard filters applied.
    Each source is a list of user ids, best first. Returns at most k ids."""
    out, seen = [], set(exclude)
    for rank in range(max((len(s) for s in sources), default=0)):
        for s in sources:
            if rank < len(s) and s[rank] not in seen:
                seen.add(s[rank]); out.append(s[rank])
                if len(out) == k:
                    return out
    return out

Why it is correct. The seen set starts as the excluded ids, and an id is appended only if it is not in seen, after which it is added: so the output has no duplicates and no excluded ids. Ranks are visited in increasing order, so every source's r-th candidate is considered before any source's (r+1)-th. The loop returns as soon as K ids are collected. Cost: O(total input length) set operations. The exclude set here is the cheap, possibly stale filter (a Bloom filter is fine, since it errs only by dropping); the authoritative checks run again on the final 10.

DecisionApproximate?Why

"Already shown" and "already friends" prefiltersYes, one-sided: Bloom filterA false positive drops a candidate; never shows a wrong one.
Blocks (both directions), privacy settings, deactivation, age rulesNoShowing one wrong person is a safety and trust failure, not a relevance loss. Checked exactly, on the source of truth, on every response (Day 3).

Confirm by measuring

The same 1,000 users and 4,395 future friendships as section 5. The union of the sources' lists is what candidate generation hands the ranker:

Two-hop and PPR lists overlap by only 45% (share of the two-hop top 100 also in the PPR top 100), so their union adds recall (0.278 against 0.245 for PPR alone) at 1.5 times the size. LSH adds little on this graph. And a fixed equal-share merge into 100 slots does worse than PPR alone (0.216 against 0.245), because it gives the weak LSH source a third of the slots. That is the design lesson: generate generously from several sources, then let a ranker that has seen the outcomes decide (Day 5), rather than fixing quotas by hand.

Where PYMK uses it

This contract is the interface between the candidate box and the ranking box in R.7. The offline sources (PPR, LSH) are precomputed per user and stored next to the adjacency lists (Day 3); the online source (sampled two-hop) runs per request under its read budget; the merge deduplicates, tags and applies the cheap filters; the ranker scores about 150 candidates; the final exact eligibility check runs on the 10 that are shown.

Problem variations
  • The two-hop source times out. What does the response contain, and what does the exact flag say?
  • A user has 3 friends and no precomputed lists yet (a new user). Which sources still work?
  • Set source quotas from data: give each source a share proportional to its measured marginal recall. Does that beat the union?
Solution

Timeout. The scan returns its partial counts (it stopped at the budget, so it has a valid sample), the offline lists are unaffected, and the list is marked exact: false; the hard filters run as always. New user. Groups (from sign-up), and the two-hop scan over 3 friends, which is cheap and exact; PPR and LSH appear after the next batch run. Quotas. Proportional quotas recover most of the union's recall in a fixed size, but the ranker over the union is the better design, because the right share differs per user. The tests are the property tests of Day 1 applied to a new function.
