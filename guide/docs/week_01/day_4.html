DAY 4 · LEARN WHO CONNECTSMLAIDSA

From counting mutual friends to a model that learns who connects

Where we are. Day 3 made candidate generation cheap: for each user we now have a few hundred people worth considering, 829 on average on the week's graph. The app shows ten. So far the order has come from one rule, "most mutual friends first", which we picked because it sounded right. Today we stop guessing. The graph has a history: we know who became friends, and when. We can freeze the graph at some moment, look at who connected next, and train a model to predict exactly that. Then we judge it the way a user would, by how many real future friends land in the ten slots.

candidates (Day 3)→features→model→top 10
Today's boxes. Among about 800 candidates per user, the model's job is to put the few who will actually connect into the ten slots shown.
ML · link prediction as a learning problem

1 · Learn from the past: freeze the graph, see who connects next

To learn, a model needs examples with answers. Here the answer to "will these two become friends?" only exists in the future. The trick is to pretend we live in the past. Sort the 100,000 friendships of the week's graph by the order they formed. Take the first 80,000 as "what we knew", and the last 20,000 as "what happened next". A pair of people who were not friends in the first part and became friends in the second is a positive: a friendship the model should have predicted. Predicting the friendships a graph will grow is called link prediction.

The obvious shortcut, and why it lies. A common recipe skips the clock: take the whole graph, hide a random 20% of its friendships, and ask the model to find them. On the week's graph that recipe looks wonderful. Ranking by mutual friends recovers 14.2% of each user's hidden friendships in their top 10. With the honest time split, the same ranker recovers 5.2%. The random split flatters it by 2.7×.

The reason is that a random split lets the future leak in. Suppose Ana and Raj became friends early, and that is the friendship the split hides. Later, Raj's friend Mei met Ana through Raj, so Mei and Ana became friends. That later friendship is still visible, and it makes Mei a mutual friend of Ana and Raj. The evidence for the hidden friendship was caused by the hidden friendship. In the real app this never happens: nothing after "now" is known.

Two cuts: one to learn from, one to be judged on. Each cut splits the history into a feature graph (friendships before the cut, which the model may look at) and a label window (friendships after it, which it must predict).

trainedges 0–59,999 · look60,000–79,999 · predict80,000–99,999 · unseenfit the model
testedges 0–79,999 · look80,000–99,999 · predictmeasure it, exactly as it would be served
The model never sees a label from the test window, and the test features never use a friendship from it. Both cuts keep the order of time.

What the test measures. Every number below uses the test cut, so it is worth saying once what it contains.

  • Users: the 9,357 users who made at least one new friendship in the last 20,000. Each new friendship counts from both ends, so there are 40,000 (user, future friend) pairs.
  • Candidates: each user's friends of friends in the past graph, minus the user and their current friends. 829 per user on average (median 533, at most 7,790), 7,757,153 pairs in all.
  • Positives: candidates who became friends: 21,290 pairs. That is 0.274% of the candidates, about one in 365.
  • Misses: nearly half of all future friends were never candidates. Only 53.2% of the 40,000 future (user, friend) pairs had a mutual friend at the time of the cut. A future friend with no mutual friend can never be ranked; it still counts against us. Averaged per user, the best any ranker over these candidates could do is find 48.4% of a user's future friends (95% interval 47.8–49.1%). That is the ceiling.

In one line. Freeze the graph at a time cut, learn from what happened after an earlier cut, and test on what happened after a later one; hiding random edges instead leaks the future and inflated recall 2.7× here.

2 · Negatives, and why the choice biases you

A model also needs examples of pairs that did not connect. Which ones you choose changes both what it learns and how good it looks.

The obvious choice: random strangers. Pair each user with random people they are not friends with. 91.7% of those strangers share no mutual friend with the user, while every positive shares at least one. So counting mutual friends separates them almost perfectly: an AUC of 0.969 (AUC is a score from 0.5 for a coin flip to 1 for perfect; §5 defines it). But the app never has to choose between a friend of a friend and a stranger. Candidate generation already did that. Against negatives drawn from the candidates, the pairs the ranker really has to separate, the same score reaches only 0.620.

So negatives come from the candidates, and there are far too many. The training window has 4,541,304 candidate pairs and only 18,322 positives. We keep every positive and each negative with probability r = 0.05: 244,152 training rows, 7.5% of them positive. Training gets 19× smaller. The price is that the model now believes friendships are far more common than they are; §8 shows the one-line fix.

A negative means "not yet", not "never". A pair that did not connect in the window may connect later, or may simply never have had the chance to meet. Once a recommender is live, "had the chance" means "was shown", and that turns into a feedback loop (§9).

3 · Features: what makes a friend of a friend likely

A model sees each candidate as a handful of numbers, its features. Start with the one we have: mutual friends. In the six-person example, Esha shares two friends with you (Ben and Chloe), Dev and Farid one each. Counting puts Esha first.

Where counting goes wrong. Look at a different small graph. You have three friends: A, who has 2 friends; B, who has 3; and H, a sociable person with 6. X and Y both share two mutual friends with you, so counting ties them.

X and Y tie on mutual friends. X was reached through two people with small circles; Y partly through H, who knows everyone. AA is the Adamic–Adar score introduced below.

Think about how an introduction happens. A has only two friends, so if A introduces you to anyone, it is very likely X. H has six friends to choose from, so H vouching for Y is weaker news. A mutual friend who knows everyone says little about any one pair. So instead of adding 1 per mutual friend, add a weight that shrinks as that mutual friend's circle grows. The Adamic–Adar score (AA) uses one over the logarithm of the mutual friend's number of friends, their degree:

\[ \text{AA}(u,v) = \sum_{\text{mutual friends } z} \frac{1}{\log(\text{degree of } z)} . \]

By hand: X gets 1/ln 2 + 1/ln 3 = 1.44 + 0.91 = 2.35. Y gets 1/ln 3 + 1/ln 6 = 0.91 + 0.56 = 1.47. The tie is broken, in X's favour. A close cousin, resource allocation (RA), uses one over the degree itself, a much steeper discount: X gets 1/2 + 1/3 = 0.83, Y gets 1/3 + 1/6 = 0.50. In the six-person example, Ben and Chloe both have 4 friends, so AA gives Esha 2/ln 4 = 1.44 and Dev and Farid 0.72 each: the same order as counting. AA only changes the answer when mutual friends differ in popularity.

Is the discount real, and how steep is it? Take the test-cut candidates that share exactly one mutual friend with the user, group them by how many friends that mutual friend has, and see how often each group became friends.

The math, step by step: where the 1/degree weight comes from, and how the data compares with 1/log

What we will do. Write down one simple story of how a friendship closes, compute the probability it gives each candidate, and see which weight falls out.

The story. Write \(d_x\) for the degree of person x (their number of friends), and \(N(x)\) for the set of their friends. User u picks one of their friends z at random: each with probability \(1/d_u\). Then z introduces u to one of z's other friends at random. z will not introduce u to u, so there are \(d_z - 1\) choices, each with probability \(1/(d_z - 1)\).

Numbers first. In the picture, u is You with \(d_u = 3\). Going through A (2 friends, so 1 other friend: X) reaches X with probability (1/3)(1/1). Going through B (3 friends, so 2 others: X and Y) reaches X with probability (1/3)(1/2). Those two paths are different events (different first picks), so their probabilities add: X gets (1/3)(1 + 1/2) = 0.50. Y is reached through B or H (6 friends, 5 others): (1/3)(1/2 + 1/5) = 0.23. Z, P, Q and R are reached only through H: (1/3)(1/5) = 0.067 each. Check: 0.50 + 0.23 + 4 × 0.067 = 1, as it must, because every introduction lands on some candidate.

In symbols. The introduction reaches v through z only if z knows both, that is, z is in \(N(u)\cap N(v)\). Adding over those disjoint paths:

\[ P(u \text{ is introduced to } v) = \sum_{z \in N(u)\cap N(v)} \frac{1}{d_u}\cdot\frac{1}{d_z - 1} = \frac{1}{d_u}\sum_{z \in N(u)\cap N(v)} \frac{1}{d_z-1}. \]

For one user u, the factor \(1/d_u\) is the same for every candidate, so it does not change the order. What is left ranks candidates by \(\sum_z 1/(d_z - 1)\): resource allocation, up to the −1. The week's generator closes 45% of its friendships with exactly this kind of step (P_CLOSE = 0.45 in make_graph.py), so the story fits this graph.

Where 1/log comes from. Adamic and Adar (2003) built their score for web pages: two pages that share a rare feature are more likely related than two that share a common one, so they weighted each shared feature by one over the log of its frequency. On a graph, the shared feature is a mutual friend and its frequency is its degree. 1/log d falls far more slowly than 1/d: going from d = 5 to d = 921 divides the 1/d weight by 184 but the 1/log d weight only by 4.2.

What the data says. Using each row's median degree (5, 9, 15, 31, 71, 169, 921), the measured ratios in the table are 1, 0.499, 0.310, 0.161, 0.082, 0.045, 0.021. 1/d predicts 1, 0.556, 0.333, 0.161, 0.070, 0.030, 0.005; 1/log d predicts 1, 0.732, 0.594, 0.469, 0.378, 0.314, 0.236. Up to about 100 friends, the data follows 1/d closely; beyond that it falls more slowly. A straight-line fit of log(rate) against log(degree) has slope −0.74 (pure 1/d would be −1, 1/log d much flatter). The flattening is expected: 55% of the generator's friendships form through groups or popular users, routes that do not pass through z at all.

What we showed. Under the introduction story, a mutual friend's weight is one over their degree (minus one); the data agrees up to moderate degrees and then sits between 1/d and 1/log d.

The other features.

  • Shared groups. How many groups the two have in common. 35% of the graph's friendships form inside a group, a route the friendship graph cannot see.
  • Same city (0 or 1). In this synthetic graph city plays no role in who befriends whom, so a good model should give it a weight near zero. It is a useful sanity check.
  • Degrees of both people (on a log scale). Sociable people make more new friends, as the one reaching out and as the one reached (Day 2).
  • Jaccard (Day 3): mutual friends divided by the number of people either one knows.

The one feature that hurts. Jaccard sounds like the most principled of all: it measures how similar two circles of friends are. But dividing by the size of both circles pushes sociable candidates down, and sociable candidates are exactly the ones who make new friends. Ranking candidates by their degree alone gives an AUC of 0.579; Jaccard gives 0.417, worse than a coin flip. Similar circles and a likely new friendship point in opposite directions here.

All the graph features come out of the same scan that finds the candidates (Day 1): for each friend z of u and each friend v of z, add 1 to the mutual count, 1/log dz to AA and 1/dz to RA. So they cost no more than the candidates did, the Σ deg² work of Day 2 (about 1,477 reads per user on average). Counts and scores go through log(1 + ·) so a few huge values do not dominate.

ranker/features.py
FEATURES = ["log_cn", "jaccard", "log_aa", "log_ra", "shared_groups", "same_city", "log_du", "log_dv"]

def pair_features(adj, groups, city, u):
    """Two-hop candidates of u in the graph adj, and one feature row per candidate."""
    cn, aa, ra = Counter(), Counter(), Counter()
    for z in adj[u]:                           # z = a mutual friend of u and v
        dz = len(adj[z])                       # dz >= 2: z knows u and v
        for v in adj[z]:
            if v != u and v not in adj[u]:
                cn[v] += 1
                aa[v] += 1.0 / math.log(dz)
                ra[v] += 1.0 / dz
    cands = sorted(cn)
    du, gu = len(adj[u]), set(groups[u])
    X = np.empty((len(cands), len(FEATURES)))
    for i, v in enumerate(cands):
        dv = len(adj[v])
        X[i] = (math.log1p(cn[v]), cn[v] / (du + dv - cn[v]),  # |N(u) ∪ N(v)| = du + dv - cn
                math.log1p(aa[v]), math.log1p(ra[v]),
                len(gu.intersection(groups[v])), city[u] == city[v],
                math.log1p(du), math.log1p(dv))
    return cands, X

The tests compare every row with a slow version that intersects friend sets one pair at a time, on both small examples and on 30 random graphs.

In one line. Weight each mutual friend by how selective they are (Adamic–Adar: one over the log of their friend count), add groups and degrees, and drop the intuition that similar circles mean a likely friendship: Jaccard ranked worse than chance here.

4 · Logistic regression: a weighted vote turned into a probability

We now have eight numbers per candidate. How do we combine them into one score? Picking weights by hand works for two features, not eight, and several of them (mutual friends, AA, RA) say almost the same thing.

The idea in plain words. Let every feature cast a vote, with a weight. Add the votes up, starting from a baseline. A big total means "likely", a very negative one "unlikely". Then squash the total into a probability between 0 and 1 with the S-shaped sigmoid curve: a total of 0 maps to 50%, large positive totals approach 100%, large negative ones approach 0%.

\[ p = \sigma(\text{total}) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\text{total}}}, \qquad \text{total} = b + w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 + \dots \]

A worked vote, with made-up weights to see the mechanics: start at −3, add 2 per unit of AA and 0.5 per shared group. A candidate with no evidence scores −3, a probability of 4.7%. One with AA 1.5 and one shared group scores −3 + 3 + 0.5 = 0.5, a probability of 62%. This model is logistic regression.

Learning the weights. Start with every weight at zero. Predict a probability for each training pair. Compare it with what happened (1 if they connected, 0 if not). The difference, prediction minus truth, is the error. Nudge each weight against its error, in proportion to how large its feature was on that pair; average over all pairs; repeat. Features that were large on pairs the model underrated get their weights raised. That is all gradient descent does here, and it is the whole training loop below.

ranker/logreg.py
def sigmoid(z):
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + np.tanh(0.5 * z))               # = 1/(1+e^-z), without overflow

def loss_grad(w, X, y, lam):
    """Mean log loss + (lam/2)|w|^2 (bias excluded), and its gradient. X has a column of 1s first."""
    p = sigmoid(X @ w)
    eps = 1e-12
    loss = -np.mean(y * np.log(p + eps) + (1 - y) * np.log(1 - p + eps))
    reg = np.r_[0.0, w[1:]]
    return loss + 0.5 * lam * reg @ reg, X.T @ (p - y) / len(y) + lam * reg

def train_logreg(X, y, lam=1e-3, iters=3000):
    """Full-batch gradient descent with step 1/L, L = ||X||_2^2 / (4n) + lam (the Hessian bound)."""
    X = np.c_[np.ones(len(X)), X]
    L = np.linalg.norm(X, 2) ** 2 / (4 * len(y)) + lam
    w = np.zeros(X.shape[1])
    for _ in range(iters):
        _, g = loss_grad(w, X, y, lam)
        w -= g / L
    return w

def predict_logit(w, X):
    return w[0] + X @ w[1:]

def fit_scaler(X):
    mu, sd = X.mean(0), X.std(0)
    return mu, np.where(sd > 0, sd, 1.0)

def scale(X, scaler):
    mu, sd = scaler
    return (X - mu) / sd

Two practical details. Each feature is standardized first (shifted and scaled to mean 0, spread 1, using the training set's values, which are reused unchanged when serving), so no feature's units dominate. And the step size is computed from the data rather than tuned (the deep block derives it). The tests check the gradient against finite differences and compare the result with an independent solver, Newton's method.

On the training cut, gradient descent reaches a log loss of 0.21534; Newton's method reaches the same value, and the two weight vectors differ by at most 2.3 × 10⁻⁵. What the model learned, on standardized features:

A negative weight on mutual friends does not mean mutual friends are bad. Mutual friends and AA move together; the model uses their difference to discount hubs more steeply than AA's 1/log does, which is what the table in §3 asked for. This is the reason to learn weights rather than choose them.

The math, step by step: likelihood → log loss → gradient → convexity → a step size that always works

What we will do. Turn "make the observed outcomes likely" into a loss, differentiate it, show it has a single valley, and pick a step that is guaranteed to go downhill.

Notation. Training pair i has a feature vector \(x_i\) (with a leading 1 so the baseline b is just another weight) and a label \(y_i\), 1 if the pair connected and 0 if not. The weights are w. The total is \(z_i = w\cdot x_i\) and the prediction \(p_i = \sigma(z_i)\). Stack the rows into a matrix X with n rows.

Why "log-odds is linear". The odds of probability p are \(p/(1-p)\): 0.5 gives 1, 0.9 gives 9. Their log can be any real number. Logistic regression says the log-odds equal the total, \(\log\frac{p}{1-p} = z\); solving for p gives \(p = 1/(1+e^{-z})\), the sigmoid. So raising a feature by 1 multiplies the odds by \(e^{w_j}\).

Step 1, the likelihood. Treat each label as a coin flip that comes up 1 with probability \(p_i\). The probability of one observed label is \(p_i\) if \(y_i = 1\) and \(1 - p_i\) if \(y_i = 0\), which one formula covers: \(p_i^{y_i}(1-p_i)^{1-y_i}\). With independent pairs, the probability of all labels is the product. We want weights that make it large.

Step 2, the log loss. A product of many small numbers underflows, and the log turns it into a sum without moving the maximum. Maximizing the log is the same as minimizing its negative; dividing by n makes it an average:

\[ \ell(w) = -\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[y_i \log p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\Big]. \]

This is the log loss, also called cross-entropy. A confident wrong prediction is expensive: \(p = 0.01\) for a pair that connected costs \(-\log 0.01 = 4.6\). A confident right one costs almost nothing.

Step 3, the derivative of the sigmoid. \(\sigma(z) = (1+e^{-z})^{-1}\), so \(\sigma'(z) = e^{-z}/(1+e^{-z})^2\). Split it as \(\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\cdot\frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}}\); the second factor is \(1 - \sigma(z)\). So \(\sigma' = \sigma(1-\sigma)\).

Step 4, the gradient for one pair. Differentiate the bracket with respect to z, using \(\frac{d}{dz}\log\sigma = \frac{\sigma'}{\sigma} = 1-\sigma\) and \(\frac{d}{dz}\log(1-\sigma) = \frac{-\sigma'}{1-\sigma} = -\sigma\):

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\Big[-y\log p - (1-y)\log(1-p)\Big] = -y(1-p) + (1-y)p = p - y . \]

The error, exactly. By the chain rule, since \(z = w\cdot x\), \(\partial z/\partial w = x\), so one pair contributes \((p_i - y_i)x_i\). Averaging over pairs, in matrix form: \(\nabla\ell = \frac1n X^\top(p - y)\). This is the plain-words rule from above.

Step 5, convexity: one valley. Differentiate once more. \(\partial p_i/\partial w = p_i(1-p_i)x_i\), so the matrix of second derivatives (the Hessian) is \(H = \frac1n\sum_i p_i(1-p_i)\,x_i x_i^\top\). For any direction v, \(v^\top H v = \frac1n\sum_i p_i(1-p_i)(x_i\cdot v)^2\), a sum of non-negative weights times squares, so it is never negative. A function whose curvature is never negative in any direction is convex, and every low point of a convex function is the lowest point. Gradient descent cannot get stuck. Adding \(\frac{\lambda}{2}\|w\|^2\) (L2 regularization, baseline excluded; the code uses λ = 0.001) adds λ to the curvature, which makes the minimum unique and keeps the weights finite when features are nearly copies of each other, as mutual friends, AA and RA are.

Step 6, a step size that always goes down. Since \(p(1-p) \le \tfrac14\) (its largest value, at \(p = \tfrac12\)), the curvature is at most \(L = \|X\|_2^2/(4n) + \lambda\), where \(\|X\|_2\) is the largest singular value of X. A second-order Taylor bound with that worst curvature, for the step \(w' = w - g/L\) with \(g = \nabla\ell(w)\), gives

\[ \ell(w') \le \ell(w) - \frac1L\|g\|^2 + \frac{L}{2}\cdot\frac{\|g\|^2}{L^2} = \ell(w) - \frac{1}{2L}\|g\|^2 . \]

Every step lowers the loss unless the gradient is already zero, so nothing needs tuning. That is L in train_logreg. Standardizing keeps \(\|X\|_2\) small, so L is small and the steps are long.

Cost. One step multiplies X (n rows, d columns) by a vector twice: about 2nd operations. 3,000 steps on 244,152 rows × 9 columns is roughly 13 billion multiply-adds. Scoring one candidate when serving costs d = 9 multiply-adds.

What we showed. The log loss is the negative log-likelihood of the labels; its gradient is the average of error × features; it is convex, so gradient descent with step 1/L descends every step to the single best set of weights.

In one line. Logistic regression adds weighted feature votes and squashes the total into a probability; training nudges each weight by error × feature until the loss stops falling, and the learned weights can be read and checked.

5 · How to judge it: AUC

The obvious metric fails. Accuracy, the share of pairs predicted correctly, rewards the model "nobody ever becomes friends": it is right on 99.73% of candidates, because only 0.274% connect.

The idea. Pick one real future friend and one candidate who did not connect, at random. Does the model score the future friend higher? The AUC is the chance that it does, with ties counting half. 1 means every future friend outranks every non-friend; 0.5 is a coin flip. By hand: scores (3, 2, 2, 1) for pairs labelled (friend, friend, not, not). The four friend–non-friend comparisons are (3 vs 2): win, (3 vs 1): win, (2 vs 2): tie, half, (2 vs 1): win. AUC = 3.5 / 4 = 0.875. The name, "area under the ROC curve", comes from a plot you get by sweeping a threshold; its area equals this probability.

Comparing every pair would take 21,290 × 7,735,863 ≈ 1.6 × 10¹¹ comparisons on the test set. Sorting once gives the same number (the deep block shows why):

ranker/auc.py
def auc(scores, labels):
    """P(score of a random positive > score of a random negative), ties count 1/2 (Mann-Whitney)."""
    s, y = np.asarray(scores, float), np.asarray(labels, bool)
    order = np.argsort(s, kind="mergesort")
    s_sorted = s[order]
    ranks = np.empty(len(s))
    i = 0
    while i < len(s):                              # average rank over each run of tied scores
        j = i
        while j + 1 < len(s) and s_sorted[j + 1] == s_sorted[i]:
            j += 1
        ranks[order[i:j + 1]] = (i + j) / 2 + 1    # ranks are 1-based
        i = j + 1
    n_pos, n_neg = y.sum(), (~y).sum()
    return (ranks[y].sum() - n_pos * (n_pos + 1) / 2) / (n_pos * n_neg)

def auc_weighted(scores, labels, weights=None):
    """Same AUC, vectorized, with an optional weight per pair (used to bootstrap over users)."""
    s, y = np.asarray(scores, float), np.asarray(labels, bool)
    w = np.ones(len(s)) if weights is None else np.asarray(weights, float)
    order = np.argsort(s, kind="mergesort")
    s, y, w = s[order], y[order], w[order]
    starts = np.r_[0, np.flatnonzero(np.diff(s)) + 1]         # first index of each tie group
    pos = np.add.reduceat(w * y, starts)                      # positive weight per tie group
    neg = np.add.reduceat(w * ~y, starts)
    neg_below = np.cumsum(neg) - neg                          # negatives strictly lower
    return (pos * (neg_below + 0.5 * neg)).sum() / (pos.sum() * neg.sum())

The second version is vectorized and takes a weight per pair, which the error bars in §7 need; on a 200,000-pair sample both give 0.810048. The tests compare both with the slow pair-by-pair definition on data full of ties.

The math, step by step: why AUC is the Mann–Whitney statistic, computed by one sort

What we will do. Show that summing the ranks of the positives counts the (positive, negative) pairs the positive wins, so one sort replaces the double loop.

Notation. \(n_+\) positives, \(n_-\) negatives. Rank all scores from 1 (lowest) to \(n = n_+ + n_-\). Assume no ties for now. Let \(R_+\) be the sum of the positives' ranks and U the number of (positive, negative) pairs in which the positive scores higher: the Mann–Whitney U. By the definition, AUC = \(U/(n_+n_-)\).

Numbers first. Scores (4, 3, 2, 1) with labels (1, 0, 1, 0). Ranks: 4, 3, 2, 1. \(R_+ = 4 + 2 = 6\). Wins: 4 beats both negatives (2 wins), 2 beats the 1 (1 win), so U = 3. And \(6 - 2\cdot 3/2 = 3\).

Step 1. A positive's rank is 1 plus the number of items below it, and those items are positives or negatives: \(\text{rank}_i = 1 + \#\text{pos below } i + \#\text{neg below } i\).

Step 2. Sum over positives. The "1 + positives below" parts are the positives' ranks among themselves, 1, 2, …, \(n_+\), which add to \(n_+(n_++1)/2\). The "negatives below" parts add to U, by definition. So

\[ R_+ = \frac{n_+(n_++1)}{2} + U \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{AUC} = \frac{R_+ - n_+(n_++1)/2}{n_+ n_-}. \]

Step 3, ties. Give every member of a run of tied scores the average of the ranks the run spans. A positive tied with t negatives then gains t/2 over the rank it would have below all of them, exactly the ½ per tied pair in the definition. On the page's example (3, 2, 2, 1): ranks 4, 2.5, 2.5, 1; \(R_+ = 4 + 2.5 = 6.5\); \((6.5 - 3)/4 = 0.875\).

Cost. O(n log n) for the sort and O(n) for the ranks, instead of \(n_+n_-\) comparisons.

What we showed. AUC is the Mann–Whitney U divided by the number of pairs, and U falls out of the positives' rank sum.

6 · Two ways to compute AUC: pooled, or within each user

"A random future friend against a random non-friend" leaves open whose. There are two natural readings, and they give different numbers.

  • Pooled. Throw every user's candidates into one pile and compare any future friend with any non-friend, possibly of different users. This is the AUC used on this page.
  • Within each user. Pair each future friend with a non-friend candidate of the same user, and compare only within that pair. The worksheet computes it this way.

Pooled is higher because it also gets credit for telling users apart. Sociable users make more new friends, and their candidates tend to score higher, so a pooled comparison often pits a busy user's future friend against a quiet user's non-friend, an easy win. Within one user that help disappears: the user's own degree is the same for all their candidates and cannot change their order at all. The app only ever ranks one user's candidates, so within-user AUC is closer to the product; pooled is cheaper and fine for comparing models, as long as you say which one you report. Both readings order the three rankers the same way.

7 · What the user sees: recall@10, with error bars over users

AUC scores the whole ranking. The user sees ten names. So the metric that matches the product is recall@10: of a user's future friends, what share appeared in their top 10? Averaged over users. Future friends who were never candidates count as misses, which is why no ranker can exceed the 48.4% ceiling of §1. Two companions: precision@10, the share of the ten slots that were future friends, and NDCG@10, which also gives more credit to a hit in slot 1 than in slot 10.

ranker/metrics.py
def top_k(cands, scores, k=10):
    """The k best candidates; ties by smaller id, as in the exact baseline."""
    best = heapq.nsmallest(k, range(len(cands)), key=lambda i: (-scores[i], cands[i]))
    return [cands[i] for i in best]

def recall_at_k(top, future):
    """Share of u's future friends that appear in the shown list. Friends outside it are misses."""
    return len(set(top) & future) / len(future)

def precision_at_k(top, future, k=10):
    return len(set(top) & future) / k

def ndcg_at_k(top, future, k=10):
    dcg = sum(1 / math.log2(i + 2) for i, v in enumerate(top[:k]) if v in future)
    ideal = sum(1 / math.log2(i + 2) for i in range(min(k, len(future))))
    return dcg / ideal

Error bars: resample users, not pairs. One user's candidates share that user's friends and habits, so they are not independent evidence; users roughly are. To get an interval, draw 9,357 users with replacement, recompute the average, repeat 1,000 times, and keep the middle 95% (Day 2 used the same bootstrap for p99 latency, with requests as the unit). To compare two rankers, resample the same users for both and look at the difference: a user who is easy for one ranker is easy for the other, and pairing cancels that shared luck.

ranker/bootstrap.py
def bootstrap_ci(per_user, B=1000, alpha=0.05, seed=0):
    """Percentile CI for the mean of a per-user metric, resampling users with replacement."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    x = np.asarray(per_user, float)
    idx = rng.integers(0, len(x), size=(B, len(x)))
    means = x[idx].mean(1)
    return x.mean(), np.quantile(means, alpha / 2), np.quantile(means, 1 - alpha / 2)

8 · Calibration: does a 2% prediction mean 2%?

For ranking, only the order of the scores matters. But the numbers get used too: to decide whether a suggestion is worth a notification, or to merge lists from different sources (the Algorithms block below). A model is calibrated if, among all pairs it gives probability 2%, about 2% actually connect.

How to check. Sort the predictions, cut them into ten equal-sized bins, and compare the average prediction with the observed rate in each bin. One summary number is the expected calibration error (ECE): the average gap, weighted by bin size.

ranker/calibration.py
def reliability(p, y, bins=10):
    """Bin by predicted probability (equal-count bins); mean prediction vs observed rate per bin."""
    p, y = np.asarray(p, float), np.asarray(y, float)
    order = np.argsort(p)
    rows = []
    for chunk in np.array_split(order, bins):
        rows.append((p[chunk].mean(), y[chunk].mean(), len(chunk)))
    ece = sum(n * abs(pm - om) for pm, om, n in rows) / len(p)  # expected calibration error
    return rows, ece

The fix for sampled negatives. We trained on 7.5% positives, so the model predicts as if 7.5% of pairs connected. Keeping each negative with probability r multiplies every pair's odds by the same factor 1/r. So the weights are untouched and only the baseline is off, by exactly log r: add log 0.05 to every total and the probabilities are back on the right scale. The order, and so AUC and recall, do not change.

ranker/prior.py
def prior_correct(logit, r):
    """Negatives were kept with probability r: true log-odds = sampled log-odds + log r."""
    return logit + math.log(r)

The math, step by step: why sampling negatives shifts the log-odds by exactly log r

What we will do. Compare the odds of a positive before and after sampling, using Bayes' rule.

Numbers first. Suppose among pairs with some feature values, 2 connect and 998 do not: odds 2/998. Keep all positives and each negative with probability 0.05: about 2 positives and 49.9 negatives remain, odds 2/49.9, which is (2/998) × 20. The odds went up by 1/r = 20, whatever the features were.

In symbols. Let "kept" be the event that a pair survives sampling. Positives are always kept; negatives with probability r. By Bayes' rule, the odds among kept pairs with features x are

\[ \frac{P(y=1\mid x,\text{kept})}{P(y=0\mid x,\text{kept})} = \frac{P(y=1\mid x)\cdot 1}{P(y=0\mid x)\cdot r} . \]

Take logs: \(\text{logit}_{\text{sampled}}(x) = \text{logit}_{\text{true}}(x) - \log r\), so \(\text{logit}_{\text{true}} = \text{logit}_{\text{sampled}} + \log r\). The shift is the same constant for every x, so a logistic model absorbs it entirely into its baseline. With r = 0.05, log r = −3.0.

What we showed. Downsampling negatives leaves the feature weights alone and moves only the baseline, by −log r; adding log r back restores the probabilities on the sampled period. It cannot correct for a change of period.

9 · Feedback loops: the model changes the data it learns from

Our labels came from a graph that grew with no recommender in it. Once the app shows suggestions, many new friendships start from one. The next training set then records mostly the pairs the current model chose to show. A pair that is never shown rarely connects, however good a match, so it is logged as a negative. A model trained on those logs learns to imitate the old model, and whatever the old model ignored stays ignored. This is a feedback loop, and the bias it creates is called exposure bias.

Where it starts, measured. The 100 users with the most past friends (the top 1%) take part in 10.8% of the future friendships. They fill far more of the shown slots: 50.9% when ranking by mutual friends, 41.4% by Adamic–Adar, 43.9% by logistic regression, 22.6% by resource allocation (Jaccard, which penalizes degree, gives them 0.4%). Every useful ranker over-exposes hubs by 2–5× relative to their share of new friendships. A live system turns that exposure into more friendships for hubs; a model retrained on them learns even larger degree weights; Day 2's rich-get-richer effect speeds up. Ten slots are scarce, and quiet or new users lose them. So measure who gets exposure, for example by degree and account age, and set explicit limits, such as how many people a hub can be suggested to per day.

The standard fix: explore, then reweight. Fill most slots with the ranker and a few with controlled randomness, so that every candidate has a known, nonzero chance of being shown. Then weight each logged outcome by one over its chance of being shown. A pair shown with probability 10% stands in for the nine similar pairs that were not shown. This is inverse propensity weighting. The week's graph has no log of what was shown, so the exposure shares above are all we can measure here; the deep block shows why the reweighting is unbiased.

The math, step by step: why weighting by 1/π removes exposure bias

What we will do. Show that the reweighted average of logged outcomes equals the average we would have seen had every pair been shown.

Notation. For a candidate pair with features x, S = 1 if it was shown and 0 if not, and Y = 1 if it would connect when shown. \(\pi(x) = P(S = 1 \mid x) > 0\) is its known chance of being shown. f(Y) is anything we want to average, for example Y itself.

Numbers first. 1,000 similar pairs, 30 of which would connect if shown, each shown with probability 0.1. About 100 are shown, about 3 of them connect. Weight each shown pair by 1/0.1 = 10: 3 × 10 = 30 connections out of 100 × 10 = 1,000 pairs. The full-population rate, 3%, is recovered.

In symbols. Showing is decided by a coin with probability π(x), independently of Y given x. Average first over that coin, with x and Y fixed; \(\mathbb{E}[S\mid x] = \pi(x)\):

\[ \mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{S}{\pi(x)}\, f(Y)\Big] = \mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{\mathbb{E}[S\mid x]}{\pi(x)}\, f(Y)\Big] = \mathbb{E}\big[f(Y)\big]. \]

What we showed. As long as every candidate has a nonzero chance of being shown, weighting by 1/π gives unbiased estimates. When π is tiny the weights are huge and the estimate noisy, which is why exploration slots cannot be too rare.

In one line. A live recommender writes its own training data; without exploration and reweighting, it learns to repeat itself and to show hubs ever more.

ranker/newton.py
def newton(X, y, lam, iters=30):
    """Newton's method on the same objective: w <- w - H^-1 g, with H = X^T S X / n + lam (bias excluded)."""
    X = np.c_[np.ones(len(X)), X]
    w = np.zeros(X.shape[1])
    R = lam * np.eye(X.shape[1]); R[0, 0] = 0
    for _ in range(iters):
        p = sigmoid(X @ w)
        g = X.T @ (p - y) / len(y) + R @ w
        H = (X * (p * (1 - p))[:, None]).T @ X / len(y) + R
        w -= np.linalg.solve(H, g)
    return w

Each step jumps to the bottom of the quadratic that matches the loss's slope and curvature at the current point. The curvature is \(X^\top S X/n\) with \(S\) holding \(p_i(1-p_i)\), so each step is a weighted least-squares problem (hence its other name, iteratively reweighted least squares). On the training set it agrees with gradient descent to 2.3 × 10⁻⁵ in every weight. For two pairs and one feature, \(\partial\ell/\partial w = \tfrac12[(p_1-y_1)x_1 + (p_2-y_2)x_2]\). B's candidates are A (via You and X) and H (via You and Y). Both have 2 mutual friends, and AA and RA tie too: AA = 1/ln 3 + 1/ln 2 = 2.35 and RA = 1/3 + 1/2 = 0.83 for each, since You have 3 friends and X and Y have 2 each. Jaccard separates them, the wrong way round for this graph: 2/3 for A but 2/7 for H, whose six friends inflate the union. The tests check these numbers. With r = 0.2 the baseline should fall by about log(0.2/0.05) = 1.39.

Algorithms · merging ranked lists with a heap

1 · Several ranked lists, ten slots

The model ranks friends of friends. But Day 3 found candidates in other places too: people in your groups, and popular users. Each source hands back its own list, best first. And on Day 5 the graph will be split across many machines, each ranking the candidates it stores. Either way the app ends up with several sorted lists and needs the best ten overall, each person once.

A tiny case: lists (0.9 Ana, 0.5 Ben, 0.1 Cy), (0.8 Ben, 0.7 Dee), and an empty one; we want the best 3. The obvious thing, concatenating and sorting, is correct but reads every item when only three are needed. Scanning all list heads for each output is fine for three lists and slow for a thousand.

2 · The idea: keep only the heads in a heap

Each list's best unread item is its head. The best item overall must be one of the heads. So keep the heads in a heap (Day 1's top-k structure, here ordered so the best is on top), pop the best, output it, and push the next item from the same list. If that person was already output, because two sources found them, skip them.

ranker/merge.py
def merge_top_k(lists, k=10):
    """lists: several candidate lists, each sorted by probability, highest first: [(prob, user), ...].
    Returns the k best distinct users across all lists."""
    heap = [(-lst[0][0], lst[0][1], s, 0) for s, lst in enumerate(lists) if lst]
    heapq.heapify(heap)                         # one entry per list: its current head
    out, seen = [], set()
    while heap and len(out) < k:
        neg_p, user, s, i = heapq.heappop(heap) # the best head is the best remaining item overall
        if user not in seen:                    # dedupe: the first time a user pops is its best score
            seen.add(user); out.append((-neg_p, user))
        if i + 1 < len(lists[s]):
            p, v = lists[s][i + 1]
            heapq.heappush(heap, (-p, v, s, i + 1))
    return out

Cost, in words. The heap never holds more than one entry per list, so each pop and push costs about log(number of lists). The work grows with the number of lists and the number of items output, not with how long the lists are. For L lists, k outputs and D skipped duplicates: O(L + (k + D) log L) time, O(L + k) space. Merging everything (LeetCode 23) is O(N log L) for N items in total. The tests check the output against a brute force on 200 random cases.

The math, step by step: why the merge is correct, and why each list only needs its own top k

What we will do. State one fact that stays true before every pop, use it to show each pop is the best unread item, then show that duplicates and per-list truncation do not change the answer.

The invariant. Before each pop, the heap holds exactly one entry for each list that still has unread items: that list's head. It is true after building the heap from the first items. Each round removes one list's head and inserts that same list's next item (if any), so it stays true.

Each pop is the best unread item. Take any unread item. It sits in some list at or behind that list's head, and the list is sorted, so it is no better than its head. The top of the heap is the best head, so it is at least as good as every unread item. Repeating this, items come out from best to worst.

Dedupe keeps each person's best score. Copies of the same person come out from best to worst, like everything else, so the first copy to pop carries that person's highest score. Skipping later copies leaves the k people with the highest best scores.

Per-list top k is enough. Suppose person v is in the overall top k and lives in list s. Fewer than k items in all lists beat v, so certainly fewer than k items in list s do. So v is in list s's own top k. Asking each list (or machine) for only k items and merging gives exactly the overall answer.

What we showed. The heap of heads outputs the best k distinct people, and truncating each list to k first loses nothing, provided all lists are scored on the same scale.

Different sources, different scales. Now merge the friends-of-friends list with two other sources from Day 3: group-mates and popular users. Their scores mean different things, so before merging each was turned into a probability by histogram binning on the training cut (ten score bins, each mapped to its observed connection rate), and the friends-of-friends list used the corrected logistic regression. The alternative is not to merge lists at all: put all three sources' candidates into one pool and score the pool with one logistic regression (with one extra feature, the group score).

class ListNode:
    def __init__(self, val=0, next=None):
        self.val, self.next = val, next

def mergeKLists(lists):                         # 23
    heap = [(node.val, i, node) for i, node in enumerate(lists) if node]
    heapq.heapify(heap)
    dummy = tail = ListNode()
    while heap:
        _, i, node = heapq.heappop(heap)        # i breaks ties: nodes are not comparable
        tail.next = tail = node
        if node.next:
            heapq.heappush(heap, (node.next.val, i, node.next))
    return dummy.next

def kClosest(points, k):                        # 973: keep the k closest in a max-heap of size k
    heap = []
    for x, y in points:
        d = -(x * x + y * y)                    # negate: heapq is a min-heap
        if len(heap) < k:
            heapq.heappush(heap, (d, x, y))
        elif d > heap[0][0]:
            heapq.heapreplace(heap, (d, x, y))
    return [[x, y] for _, x, y in heap]

        out.append([nums1[i], nums2[j]])
        if j + 1 < len(nums2):
            heapq.heappush(heap, (nums1[i] + nums2[j + 1], i, j + 1))
    return out

def smallestRange(nums):                        # 632: heap of heads + the running max of the heads
    heap = [(lst[0], r, 0) for r, lst in enumerate(nums)]
    heapq.heapify(heap)
    hi = max(lst[0] for lst in nums)
    best = (heap[0][0], hi)
    while True:
        lo, r, i = heapq.heappop(heap)
        if hi - lo < best[1] - best[0]:
            best = (lo, hi)
        if i + 1 == len(nums[r]):
            return list(best)
        nxt = nums[r][i + 1]
        hi = max(hi, nxt)
        heapq.heappush(heap, (nxt, r, i + 1))

In 23 the list index i breaks ties, because nodes cannot be compared. In 373 the heap starts with at most k rows, since no pair from a row beyond the k-th can be among the k smallest. With 256 lists, most lists hold fewer than 10 of a user's candidates and return all of them, so the merge reads nearly every candidate: per-list top-k saves work only when each list holds many more than k items.

  • Cormen, Leiserson, Rivest and Stein, Introduction to Algorithms, chapter 6 (heaps and priority queues), including the exercise on merging k sorted lists.
  • R. Fagin, A. Lotem and M. Naor, "Optimal aggregation algorithms for middleware", PODS 2001 (the threshold algorithm).
  • Python's heapq documentation, including heapq.merge and nsmallest.

What breaks next. This works for 10,000 users on one laptop: the whole graph fits in memory, features are computed on the spot, and the model is trained once on a frozen history. A real app has a billion users. The graph no longer fits on one machine, so the two-hop scan and the merge must cross many machines without making the slowest one everyone's problem. The graph changes every second, so features and calibration go stale. Some suggestions must never appear, whatever the model says: yourself, current friends, people you blocked, and anything that leaks what others chose to keep private. And a better recall@10 on last month's data is not proof that the new ranker helps real people; that takes an experiment. Day 5 builds all of that.
