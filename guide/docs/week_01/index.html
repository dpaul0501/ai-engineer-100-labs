People You May Know: from two hops to a billion users

When a user opens a social app, it shows a short list of people they may want to connect with. "Design People You May Know" is a classic interview problem, asked at companies such as Meta and LinkedIn, and this week you solve it as a hypothetical design from the ground up. The main reading gives you the whole solution: the product contract, the problem stated as mathematics, the exact friends-of-friends algorithm worked by hand and in code, what it costs, the workload measured on a 10,000-user graph, an estimate at a billion users, and the full architecture. Each day then goes deeper on one part of that design from three sides: ML and statistics, algorithms, and system design. At the weekend you build and measure a working, tested recommender.

The problem, and the whole solution

This reading answers the interview question end to end. R.1 pins down what the product must do. R.2 turns that into mathematics and measures why "friends of friends" is the right place to look. R.3 and R.4 give the exact algorithm, by hand and in code, with its proof and its cost. R.5 measures that cost on the week's graph. R.6 scales it to a billion users on paper, and R.7 draws the architecture that the rest of the week builds, box by box.

R.1 · The interview problem and the product contract

1. The question as it is asked

The question. "Design People You May Know. When a user opens the app, show them 10 people they may want to connect with. There are a billion users." That is all the interviewer says. Everything else, you must ask for or decide, and write down.

First attempt. "Recommend the most popular users." It is easy: sort users by number of friends once, show everyone the same top 10. It is wrong for three reasons you can see on a tiny case. A user in Lisbon is shown a celebrity in Seoul they will never befriend. The same 10 people appear for everyone, so they are flooded with requests. And nothing depends on who you are. A recommendation must be personal: a function of the user and their place in the graph.

2. Inputs and output

Write the contract as a table before any design. "Contract" means the promises the service makes to whoever calls it.

PartWhat it is
InputA user id u, and k (here 10). The service may read the friendship graph, user profiles (city, groups), the block list, privacy settings, and the history of what was shown and what was accepted.
OutputAn ordered list of at most k user ids, best first. Optionally, for each, a reason the app can display ("12 mutual friends").
Called whenThe user opens a page. So the call is on the critical path: the user waits for it.

3. Hard constraints: must hold on every response

A hard constraint is a rule that may never be broken, not even once, not even to gain relevance. Break one and the product is wrong, or worse, unsafe.

  • Not yourself. u is never in its own list.
  • Not already friends. Suggesting an existing friend is a visible bug.
  • Not blocked, in either direction. If u blocked v, or v blocked u, neither is ever suggested to the other. Blocking is often a safety tool, so a single leak can do real harm.
  • Privacy. Respect the settings of the person being suggested ("do not suggest me", hidden friend lists). A reason like "12 mutual friends" must not reveal a friendship that someone chose to hide.

Why "hard" changes the design: a ranking model can be wrong on average and still be useful, but these rules cannot be "mostly" right. So the design ends with a separate, simple, exact final filter that checks every rule on the freshest data, right before the response leaves (R.7). Approximation is allowed everywhere else; never here.

4. Soft goals: traded off against each other

  • Relevance. Suggestions the user accepts and that turn into real, lasting connections (not just clicks).
  • Freshness. A friendship made a minute ago should change the list soon: the new friend disappears from suggestions and their friends become candidates.
  • Latency. The page waits for the answer. A typical target is a p99 (the time under which 99% of requests finish; Day 2 defines it) in the low hundreds of milliseconds. That number is a design assumption to agree with the interviewer, not a fact.
  • Cost. Machines and memory. At a billion users, a design that reads too much per request is unaffordable (R.6).

These pull against each other. More candidates scanned can mean better relevance but higher latency and cost. Precomputing lists overnight is cheap per request but stale. A good answer names the trade-off and picks a point on it, with numbers.

Questions to ask the interviewer. Is friendship mutual (an undirected graph) or a follow (directed)? How many users, and how many friends on average? How many requests per second? What latency? How fresh must suggestions be? Is there a signal beyond the graph (groups, city, contacts)? This week assumes mutual friendship, 1B users with about 200 friends on average, and the hard rules above.

R.2 · State it precisely

Name every object before any algorithm. All of them are shown on one small example graph, used again in R.3.

The running example. Thick lines: your friendships. Coloured lines: your friends' friendships that reach a candidate. B–C is a friendship between two of your friends, so neither is a candidate. Ids: YOU = 0, B = 1, C = 2, D = 3, E = 4, F = 5.

1. The graph

  • Graph \(G = (V, E)\). V is the set of users, E the set of friendships. A friendship is an unordered pair \(\{u, v\}\) with \(u \ne v\): if u is friends with v, then v is friends with u. Such a graph is called undirected. Write \(n = |V|\) for the number of users and \(|E|\) for the number of friendships.
  • Neighbourhood \(N(u) = \{v : \{u, v\} \in E\}\), the friends of u. In the example, \(N(\text{YOU}) = \{B, C\}\) and \(N(B) = \{\text{YOU}, C, D, E\}\).
  • Degree \(\deg(u) = |N(u)|\), the number of friends; \(d_u\) for short. Here \(d_\text{YOU} = 2\), \(\deg(B) = \deg(C) = 4\). Each friendship adds 1 to two degrees, so \(\sum_u d_u = 2|E|\) (the handshake lemma).
  • Adjacency matrix A: the \(n \times n\) table with \(A_{uv} = 1\) if u and v are friends and 0 otherwise. It is symmetric (\(A_{uv} = A_{vu}\)) and has zeros on the diagonal.

2. Candidates: friends of friends who are not yet friends

The friends of your friends are \(N(N(u)) = \bigcup_{f \in N(u)} N(f)\). In the example, \(N(B) \cup N(C) = \{\text{YOU}, B, C, D, E, F\}\). That set contains you (every friend's list contains you) and your own friends (B is in C's list, because B and C are friends). Remove both:

\[ C(u) \;=\; N(N(u)) \setminus N(u) \setminus \{u\}, \qquad C(\text{YOU}) = \{D, E, F\}. \]

These are exactly the users at distance 2: reachable in two steps, not in one or zero. Blocked and hidden users are removed later, by the hard rules.

3. The mutual-friend count, and why it equals \((A^2)_{uv}\)

Definition. The number of mutual friends of u and v is \(m(u, v) = |N(u) \cap N(v)|\). In the example, \(m(\text{YOU}, E) = |\{B, C\} \cap \{B, C\}| = 2\) and \(m(\text{YOU}, D) = |\{B, C\} \cap \{B\}| = 1\).

Claim. \(m(u, v) = (A^2)_{uv}\), the entry in row u, column v of the matrix product \(A \cdot A\).

Proof. By the definition of matrix multiplication,

\[ (A^2)_{uv} \;=\; \sum_{w \in V} A_{uw}\,A_{wv}. \]

Each term is a product of two numbers that are 0 or 1, so it is 1 exactly when both are 1, that is, when w is a friend of u and a friend of v; otherwise it is 0. The sum therefore counts the users w in \(N(u) \cap N(v)\), which is \(m(u, v)\). ∎

Three consequences, each used later. (a) Symmetry: \(m(u, v) = m(v, u)\), because \(A^2\) is symmetric: \((A^2)^\top = A^\top A^\top = A A\). (b) The diagonal is the degree: \((A^2)_{uu} = \sum_w A_{uw}^2 = d_u\). (c) A candidate is a pair with \(m \ge 1\) that is not a friendship and not the same user: \(v \in C(u) \iff v \ne u,\ A_{uv} = 0,\ (A^2)_{uv} \ge 1\), since \((A^2)_{uv} \ge 1\) says some friend of u has v as a friend. So PYMK's candidate set and scores are one row of \(A^2\), with some entries masked out. The row for YOU in the example is \((2, 1, 1, 1, 2, 1)\): the diagonal 2 is your degree, the 1s at B and C are masked because they are already friends, leaving D = 1, E = 2, F = 1.

Computing a whole \(n \times n\) matrix is out of the question at a billion users (Day 2 measures how much work all of \(A^2\) is). But as a check on tiny graphs, it is perfect, and the tests use it:

mutual_matrix.py · brute-force reference for tests
import numpy as np

def mutual_matrix(adj):
    """(A^2)[u][v] = number of common neighbours of u and v. Fine for tiny graphs only: O(n^2) memory."""
    n = len(adj)
    A = np.zeros((n, n), dtype=np.int64)
    for u in range(n):
        for v in adj[u]:
            A[u, v] = 1
    return A @ A

M = mutual_matrix(adj)
assert list(M[YOU]) == [2, 1, 1, 1, 2, 1]      # diagonal = deg(YOU); B and C are friends, not candidates
assert (M == M.T).all()                        # m(u, v) = m(v, u)

4. The real objective, and the proxy

The question. What exactly should the 10 suggestions maximize?

The real objective. Let \(\mathcal{E}(u)\) be the eligible users: everyone except u, u's friends, anyone blocked in either direction, and anyone whose privacy settings exclude them. Let \(p(u, v)\) be the probability that, if shown, v becomes a meaningful connection of u (accepted and actually used, not just clicked). Choose a set S of 10 to show:

\[ \begin{aligned} \max_{S}\quad & \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\textstyle\sum_{v \in S} \mathbf{1}[\,u, v \text{ connect}\,]\Bigr] \;=\; \sum_{v \in S} p(u, v) && \text{expected meaningful connections}\\ \text{s.t.}\quad & S \subseteq \mathcal{E}(u),\ \ |S| \le 10 && \text{hard rules}\\ & \text{latency} \le L,\ \ \text{cost per request} \le B && \text{budgets} \end{aligned} \]

The equality on the first line is linearity of expectation: the expected value of a sum of indicator variables is the sum of their probabilities, whether or not they are independent. (This model ignores that a suggestion's position on screen changes its chance; Day 5 returns to that.)

The objective tells us to rank. A sum of 10 terms chosen from a list is largest when we choose the 10 largest terms. Proof by exchange: if S leaves out some x with \(p(u, x) > p(u, y)\) for a y in S, swapping y for x raises the sum by \(p(u, x) - p(u, y) > 0\), so S was not optimal. So the whole problem reduces to estimating \(p(u, v)\) well enough to sort by it, cheaply, for the users that could be near the top.

Why we cannot use it directly. We do not know p. We have no labels on day one, and there are \(n - 1\) possible v for each u, a billion of them. We need a proxy: a score that is cheap to compute and that sorts users roughly as p would.

The proxy: rank by \(m(u, v)\). If p depends only on the mutual count through some increasing function, \(p(u, v) = g(m(u, v))\) with g increasing, then sorting by m gives exactly the same order as sorting by p, so ranking by m is optimal. That "if" is a claim about the world, and we can test it: if g is increasing, the measured chance of a friendship forming must rise with m. The next part measures it. Where the proxy is weak (a mutual friend with 1,000 friends is weaker evidence than one with 10; cities and groups matter too) is what Day 5's learned ranker fixes.

5. Why friends of friends: triadic closure, measured

The idea. If u–w and w–v are friendships, the three people form an open triangle. Triadic closure is the tendency for the third side, u–v, to form later. Sociologists give three reasons: u and v have opportunity to meet (both spend time with w), trust (each can vouch through w), and they tend to be similar (people befriend people like themselves, so two of w's friends resemble each other). Each extra mutual friend adds opportunity and trust, so the chance should rise with m. Day 1 derives the related clustering coefficient (on this graph, about 0.11: an average user's friends are friends with each other 11% of the time).

How to test it without cheating: the temporal split. Every friendship in the week's graph carries the order t in which it formed. Call the first 80,000 the past and the last 20,000 the future. Compute everything (neighbours, mutual counts) on the past graph only, then ask which pairs became friends in the future. Using only the past is the point: at serving time, the system only knows the past too. Measuring m on the full graph would leak the answer.

The population of pairs. A conditional probability is only meaningful once we say "out of which pairs?". Here it is every unordered pair \(\{u, v\}\) of distinct users that were not friends in the past graph: \(\binom{10{,}000}{2} - 80{,}000 = 49{,}915{,}000\) pairs, counted exactly, with no sampling. For each, m is its number of mutual friends in the past graph. Pairs with \(m \ge 1\) (the pairs at distance 2) are found by walking two hops out from every user; the pairs with \(m = 0\) are everything else, so their number is a subtraction. A pair is a "hit" if it is among the 20,000 future friendships. (Every future friendship is a non-friend pair in the past, because the graph has no repeated friendships.)

closure.py · P(future friendship | m) over all non-adjacent pairs
def rate_by_mutuals(past_adj, future_pairs, buckets):
    """For each bucket (lo, hi) of m: (pairs, future friendships) over ALL unordered
    non-adjacent pairs {u, v} of the past graph. Pairs with m >= 1 are enumerated two hops
    out; pairs with m = 0 are counted as (all non-adjacent pairs) - (pairs with m >= 1)."""
    n = len(past_adj)
    which = lambda m: next(i for i, (lo, hi) in enumerate(buckets) if lo <= m <= hi)
    pairs, hits, at_dist2 = [0] * len(buckets), [0] * len(buckets), 0
    for u in range(n):
        cnt = Counter()
        for f in past_adj[u]:
            for v in past_adj[f]:
                if v > u and v not in past_adj[u]:      # each unordered pair once
                    cnt[v] += 1
        at_dist2 += len(cnt)
        for m in cnt.values():
            pairs[which(m)] += 1
    past_edges = sum(len(s) for s in past_adj) // 2
    pairs[which(0)] += n * (n - 1) // 2 - past_edges - at_dist2
    for u, v in future_pairs:
        hits[which(len(past_adj[u] & past_adj[v]))] += 1
    return pairs, hits

What the numbers say. (1) The curve rises monotonically: on this graph, sorting by m is consistent with sorting by the chance of friendship, which is what the proxy assumed. (2) Even the best bucket is only 12%: m is a strong signal, not a certainty, which leaves room for a better ranker (Day 5). (3) 46.8% of future friendships had no mutual friend (9,355 pairs). (4) A caution on the data: the generator forms 45% of new friendships by closing a triangle, so closure is built into this graph. Where PYMK uses it. This is the justification for the first, and largest, candidate source: walk two hops out and score by mutual friends. Day 1 measures the curve in more depth; Day 5 turns m into one feature among several.

Solution

Neighbourhoods of D. \(N(D) = \{B\}\), \(N(N(D)) = N(B) = \{\text{YOU}, C, D, E\}\), so \(C(D) = \{\text{YOU}, C, E\}\), each with one mutual friend (B). The row of \(A^2\) for D is \((1, 0, 1, 1, 1, 0)\): YOU, C and E get 1 through B, the diagonal is \(\deg(D) = 1\), and B and F get 0.

Diagonal and row sums. \((A^2)_{uu} = \sum_w A_{uw}A_{wu} = \sum_w A_{uw}^2 = \sum_w A_{uw} = d_u\), using \(A_{wu} = A_{uw}\) and \(x^2 = x\) for \(x \in \{0, 1\}\). Row sum: \(\sum_v \sum_w A_{uw}A_{wv} = \sum_w A_{uw} \sum_v A_{wv} = \sum_{w \in N(u)} \deg(w)\). For YOU: \(2 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 2 + 1 = 8 = \deg(B) + \deg(C)\).

Ratios. 0.594% / 0.020% ≈ 29×; 12.2% / 0.020% ≈ 596×.

Two-hop ceiling. 53.2%: the share of future friendships with at least one past mutual friend. The other 46.8% need different candidate sources (groups, random walks, popular users nearby).

Clarifying questions. Directed or undirected (changes the candidate definition); number of users and mean degree (storage and reads, R.6); requests per second (fleet size); latency target (online vs precomputed); freshness (batch vs incremental updates, Day 3); extra signals (features, Day 5).

Directed variation. Two-step paths: \((A^2)_{uv}\) with a non-symmetric A. It is not symmetric: if 30 of the accounts you follow all follow a celebrity, you have 30 paths to the celebrity, but the celebrity, who follows few accounts, may have no path back to you.

R.3 · The exact algorithm, by hand

The question. Given u, compute \(m(u, v)\) for every candidate v and return the 10 largest, without building \(A^2\).

First attempt. Loop over all \(n - 1\) other users v, compute \(|N(u) \cap N(v)|\) for each, keep the best 10. It is correct, but it touches every user for every request: at a billion users that is a billion intersections per page view, almost all of them returning 0. Most users share no friend with you; we should never look at them.

The key idea: walk out from u, and let each friend vote. Every mutual friend f of u and v is, by definition, a friend of u who has v in their list. So go through your friends one at a time, and for each friend go through their friends, adding one vote to each person you find. A person's total is the number of your friends who have them as a friend: their mutual count. Skip yourself and your own friends as you go. People with no mutual friend are never touched.

For each friend f of u, for each friend v of f: if v is not u, not a friend of u and not blocked, add 1 to count[v]. Then return the k users with the highest count, ties broken by smaller id.

By hand on the example. \(N(\text{YOU}) = \{B, C\}\). The counter starts empty.

friend BYOU ✗C ✗D +1E +1Read N(B), 4 entries. YOU is yourself, C is your friend: skip. count = {D: 1, E: 1}
friend CYOU ✗B ✗E +1F +1Read N(C), 4 entries. Skip YOU and B. count = {D: 1, E: 2, F: 1}
top-kE · 2D · 1F · 1Sort by (−count, id). D and F tie at 1; D has the smaller id (3 < 5). With k = 2: [E, D]
Filled = a vote added; outlined = returned with k = 2. The answer matches the row of \(A^2\) for YOU with friends and yourself masked: D = 1, E = 2, F = 1.

Three things to notice. Skips cost reads: 8 entries were read but only 4 votes cast; the other 4 were yourself and your friend. Edges among candidates are never read: adding a friendship D–F would change nothing for YOU. Ties need a rule: without "smaller id first", D and F could come out in either order from run to run, and tests could not check the answer. Day 1 writes this rule into the function's contract.

pseudocode
suggest(u, k):
    count ← empty map from user to integer
    for each f in N(u):                         # d_u friends
        for each v in N(f):                     # deg(f) entries
            if v ≠ u and v ∉ N(u) and v not blocked:
                count[v] ← count[v] + 1
    return the k keys of count with the largest value (ties: smaller id first)

R.4 · The code, its proof and its cost

pymk_exact.py · the exact baseline
from collections import Counter
import heapq

def suggest(adj, u, k=10, blocked=frozenset()):
    """Top-k friends of friends of u by mutual-friend count. Ties: smaller id first."""
    direct = adj[u]
    count = Counter()                          # candidate -> mutual friends seen so far
    for f in direct:                           # d_u friends ...
        for v in adj[f]:                       # ... deg(f) entries each: W(u) reads in all
            if v != u and v not in direct and v not in blocked:
                count[v] += 1                  # f is one more mutual friend of u and v
    return heapq.nsmallest(k, count, key=lambda v: (-count[v], v)), count

def work(adj, u):
    """W(u): adjacency entries the double loop reads = the sum of u's friends' degrees."""
    return sum(len(adj[f]) for f in adj[u])

# The 6-person example: YOU = 0, B = 1, C = 2, D = 3, E = 4, F = 5
YOU, B, C, D, E, F = range(6)
adj = [set() for _ in range(6)]
for a, b in [(YOU, B), (YOU, C), (B, C), (B, D), (B, E), (C, E), (C, F)]:
    adj[a].add(b); adj[b].add(a)

top, count = suggest(adj, YOU, k=2)
assert top == [E, D]                           # E has 2 mutuals; D and F tie at 1, D has the smaller id
assert dict(count) == {D: 1, E: 2, F: 1}
assert work(adj, YOU) == 8                     # deg(B) + deg(C) = 4 + 4
assert suggest(adj, YOU, k=2, blocked={E})[0] == [D, F]

The function also returns the full counter, because the app may want to show "2 mutual friends", and because the tests compare it entry by entry with \(A^2\). adj is a list of Python sets, so v not in direct is a hash lookup.

Why it is correct

Let the friends of u be processed in the order \(f_1, \dots, f_d\), and call v eligible if \(v \ne u\), \(v \notin N(u)\) and v is not blocked.

Invariant. After the first j friends are processed, for every eligible v, \(\text{count}[v] = |\{f_1, \dots, f_j\} \cap N(v)|\) (a missing key means 0), and the counter has no other keys.

  • Start (\(j = 0\)): the counter is empty and the set \(\{\} \cap N(v)\) is empty, so both sides are 0.
  • Step (\(j \to j + 1\)): the inner loop visits each v in \(N(f_{j+1})\) exactly once (it is a set) and adds 1 if v is eligible. Because friendship is symmetric, \(v \in N(f_{j+1}) \iff f_{j+1} \in N(v)\). So an eligible v gains exactly 1 if \(f_{j+1}\) is a friend of v and 0 otherwise, which is exactly how much the right-hand side grows. Ineligible users are never added, so no other keys appear.
  • End (\(j = d\)): \(\text{count}[v] = |N(u) \cap N(v)| = m(u, v)\) for every eligible v, and the keys are exactly the eligible users with \(m \ge 1\): \(C(u)\) minus the blocked users.

heapq.nsmallest(k, count, key=(−count, id)) returns the first k keys in the order "highest count first, then smaller id". Ids are distinct, so this order has no ties at all: the output is unique and deterministic. ∎

What it costs

  • The double loop runs its body once for each entry of each friend's list: \(\sum_{f \in N(u)} \deg(f)\) times. Name this the work per request:
    \[ W(u) \;=\; \sum_{f \in N(u)} \deg(f). \]
    Each run of the body does two set lookups and one counter update, each \(O(1)\) expected time with hashing. So the loop is \(O(W(u))\). (The outer loop's own \(d_u\) steps are included: every friend's list contains u, so \(\deg(f) \ge 1\) and \(W(u) \ge d_u\).) In the example, \(W(\text{YOU}) = 4 + 4 = 8\): exactly the 8 entries read by hand.
  • The top-k keeps a heap of at most k items while it passes over the \(|C(u)|\) keys; each push or pop costs \(O(\log k)\). So \(O(|C(u)| \log k)\).
  • Space: the counter holds \(|C(u)|\) keys, plus k for the heap.
\[ \text{time} \;=\; O\bigl(W(u) + |C(u)| \log k\bigr), \qquad \text{space} \;=\; O\bigl(|C(u)| + k\bigr), \qquad |C(u)| \le W(u). \]

The last inequality holds because every key in the counter got at least one vote, and each vote is one of the \(W(u)\) reads. With \(k = 10\), \(\log k\) is a small constant, so the cost is set by \(W(u)\).

Your friends' degrees set the cost, not yours. Look at the formula: \(W(u)\) adds up the degrees of u's friends. A user with only 9 friends, one of whom has 1,480 friends, must read at least 1,480 entries. The bound \(W(u) \le d_u \cdot \max_f \deg(f)\) shows that one popular friend is enough to make a request expensive. And as the next section measures, the friends of a typical user are more popular than a typical user, which is Day 2's friendship paradox.

Solution

B–F added. F now gets a vote from B and from C, so count = {D: 1, E: 2, F: 2}. E and F tie at 2; E has the smaller id, so the top 2 is [E, F]. \(W(\text{YOU}) = \deg(B) + \deg(C) = 5 + 4 = 9\).

D–F added. Nothing changes for YOU. The edge is in N(D) and N(F), but the loop reads only N(B) and N(C), and the invariant says count[v] depends only on which of YOU's friends know v.

The star. The centre's friends each have degree 1, so W(centre) = 1,000 and it has no candidates. A leaf's single friend has degree 1,000, so W(leaf) = 1,000 and it has 999 candidates, all with m = 1: one hub friend makes every leaf's request read the hub's whole list.

star.py
def star_work(n_leaves):
    """W for the centre and for a leaf of a star with n_leaves leaves."""
    adj = [set() for _ in range(n_leaves + 1)]
    for leaf in range(1, n_leaves + 1):
        adj[0].add(leaf); adj[leaf].add(0)
    return work(adj, 0), work(adj, 1), len(suggest(adj, 1)[1])

LeetCode 1311. Breadth-first search (BFS) from id: a queue visits people in order of distance, so the first time someone is reached is their true distance (Day 1 proves it). Then count the videos of the people at distance exactly level and sort by (frequency, name). \(O(V + E + x \log x)\) for x distinct videos.

lc1311.py
from collections import deque

def watchedVideosByFriends(watchedVideos, friends, id, level):
    """LeetCode 1311: videos watched by people at distance exactly `level`, by frequency then name."""
    dist = {id: 0}
    q = deque([id])
    while q:                                   # BFS: the first time we reach a person is their distance
        p = q.popleft()
        if dist[p] == level:
            continue                           # no need to look further out
        for f in friends[p]:
            if f not in dist:
                dist[f] = dist[p] + 1
                q.append(f)
    freq = Counter(v for p, d in dist.items() if d == level for v in watchedVideos[p])
    return sorted(freq, key=lambda v: (freq[v], v))

LeetCode 692. The key (-freq, word) puts higher counts first and breaks ties alphabetically, the same shape as (-count[v], v) in suggest.

lc692.py
def topKFrequent(words, k):
    """LeetCode 692: k most frequent words; ties by alphabetical order. O(n + u log k)."""
    freq = Counter(words)
    return heapq.nsmallest(k, freq, key=lambda w: (-freq[w], w))

Differential test. See test_exact_vs_matrix in the solutions file: for each user, the counter must equal \(\{v : (A^2)_{uv} > 0\}\) with yourself, friends and blocked users removed, and work(g, u) must equal the row sum of \(A^2\).

Names of mutual friends. Each vote stores a friend id instead of adding 1, so space grows from \(O(|C(u)|)\) to \(O(W(u))\) minus the skipped reads. In practice store the count plus only the first few names.

R.6 · At a billion users, on paper

The question. Does the R.4 code survive a billion users? Answer with a back-of-envelope estimate: a calculation from round numbers, each one a labelled assumption, accurate to a factor of a few. Its job is to tell you which resource breaks first. Day 2 teaches this as a skill.

AssumptionValueWhy this value
Users1,000,000,000Given by the interviewer
Mean friends per user200Assumed. For scale, a 2011 study of Facebook's graph reported 721 million active users and 69 billion friendships, a mean near 190 (Ugander et al., below)
Bytes per stored friend id8A 64-bit integer, enough for any id space
Daily active users; PYMK requests each per day500M; 2Assumed
Peak / average traffic3Assumed: traffic follows the clock
RAM of one large server256 GBAssumed

Storage, derived. By the handshake lemma (R.2), friendships = users × mean degree / 2 = 10⁹ × 200 / 2 = 10¹¹ (100 billion). Each friendship is stored twice, once in each person's list, so there are 2 × 10¹¹ (200 billion) adjacency entries, and at 8 bytes each, 1.6 × 10¹² bytes = 1.6 TB. That is about 7 large servers just to hold one copy in memory, before replicas, indexes or overhead. So the graph must be sharded: split across machines (Day 3).

Reads per request, derived. A user with 200 friends, each with 200 friends: 200 × 200 = 40,000 reads per request. But R.5 measured that on our graph the mean W is 3.7× (mean degree)², because friends are more popular than average. If the same ratio held, that would be about 148,000 reads (an assumption; Day 2 derives the formula \(\mathbb{E}[W \mid d_u] = d_u \cdot \mathbb{E}[d^2]/\mathbb{E}[d]\)). And these reads land on many machines: each friend's list may live on a different shard.

Traffic, derived. 500M × 2 / 86,400 s ≈ 11,600 requests per second on average, about 35,000 at peak. At 40,000 reads each, that is about 1.4 × 10⁹ adjacency entries read per second at peak, before skew.

# Back-of-envelope at 1B users. Every input is an ASSUMPTION, labelled.

What breaks first. Not storage: 1.6 TB is large but ordinary across a few machines. The problem is reads per request: tens of thousands of entries, from many shards, inside a latency budget, tens of thousands of times a second, with a heavy tail. The rest of the week attacks that from three sides: spread and cache the reads (Day 3), read less with sampling and sketches (Day 4), or do the work before the request arrives (precompute, Day 3).

R.7 · The architecture, as the map of the week

A production recommender splits "find the right 10 out of a billion" into stages. Each stage sees fewer users and can afford more work per user. This is the standard shape of large recommenders (described publicly, for example, in YouTube's "Deep Neural Networks for YouTube Recommendations", RecSys 2016); here it is applied to PYMK as a hypothetical design. Every box answers a problem found above.

Why the filter comes last and stays exact. Candidates may be precomputed hours ago and the ranker may be approximate. In that time a user may have blocked someone or become their friend. Checking the hard rules once, at the end, on the freshest data, is what makes them hold on every response, whatever the earlier stages did. It is cheap: at most a few hundred lookups for the candidates that reach this stage.

Solution

Degree 500, 4-byte ids. 10⁹ × 500 / 2 = 2.5 × 10¹¹ friendships, 5 × 10¹¹ entries, × 4 bytes = 2.0 TB. Naive reads per request: 500 × 500 = 250,000, over 6 times more than at degree 200. Reads grow with the square of the degree; storage only linearly.

Precomputed top-100. 10⁹ × 100 × (8 + 4) bytes = 1.2 TB: about the size of the graph itself, but each request becomes one key lookup. The cost moves from read time to a nightly batch job and to staleness (Day 3).

Removing boxes. No candidate generation: scoring a billion users per request. No ranking: the 12% ceiling and hubs dominate. No final filter: a block or a new friendship leaks through a stale precomputed list. No cache or sharding: the graph does not fit, and hub lists are read by everyone.

Facts, assumptions, derived. Fact: 1B users (given). Assumptions: 200 friends, 8 bytes, 500M daily users, 2 requests, peak factor 3, 256 GB. Derived: 100B friendships, 200B entries, 1.6 TB, 7 servers, 40,000 reads, 11,600 and 35,000 requests per second. The 3.7× skew is measured on our graph and only assumed to carry over.
